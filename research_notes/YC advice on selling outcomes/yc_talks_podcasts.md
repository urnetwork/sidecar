# What YC partners and featured founders said in podcasts, videos and talks (2024 to Nov 2026) about AI companies selling outcomes rather than software

Research date: 27 November 2026. Scope: spoken YC content (Lightcone, AI Startup School, Startup School, Office Hours, How to Build the Future, Founder Firesides, Main Function), plus YC's written Requests for Startups (RFS) where they frame the same argument.

## Which Lightcone podcast episodes make the case, and what exactly do the partners say?

### Takeaway
The case runs across a chain of Lightcone episodes. The Nov 2024 "Vertical AI Agents Could Be 10X Bigger Than SaaS" episode set it out: the AI product is "the software plus the people in one product," it "eat[s]... the payroll," and you sell it top-down. May 2025 added "full-stack" companies, which "can look like software companies under the hood for the first time." Sept 2025 brought the forward-deployed-engineer (FDE) playbook ("You're selling an outcome"). Oct 2025 called per-seat pricing an "Achilles heel" and proposed "work delivered or tasks completed." The latest data point is Sept 2026: Diana Hu says the share of accepted companies "that do sort of full stack end to end work" rose "from just 10% to over 25% of the batch." By late 2025 the partners were also asking whether FDEs themselves will be replaced by "AI FDEs."

Where a speaker is identified only from context, I say so. — e.g. [YC library: Vertical AI Agents](https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Lt-vertical-ai-agents-could-be-10x-bigger-than-saas)

## How do YC partners structure the argument: labor spend versus software spend, replacing headcount budgets, "sell the result, not the tool", trust and adoption?

### Takeaway
The argument has four parts. First, size: companies "spend way more on employees than they do on software," so an agent that does the work can win a far larger share of wallet ("not 1%... You can get to 10"). Second, the buyer: displace work that is already outsourced or budgeted ("showing up where the budget already lives"), and sell above the team being replaced, or "they're going to sabotage it." Third, the product claim: "you're not selling the installation of software. You're selling an outcome." Fourth, trust: earn it through head-to-head comparisons with humans, low output variance, and an executive sponsor working on one of the CEO's top-five problems. Some framings push back on a pure zero-sum labor swap: Levie says most companies are "just not doing the thing," and Jevons-style demand growth comes up repeatedly.

## What pricing models do they recommend, and what go-to-market advice comes with it (founder-led sales, pilots, forward-deployed engineers, doing things that don't scale)?

### Takeaway
On pricing, YC says: price on value, anchored to the cost of the labor you replace; never use cost-plus or "straight line undercutting"; per-unit pricing ("per return, per claim, per loan") is "the cleanest"; outcome-based pricing "aligns incentives beautifully" but is harder to forecast; per-seat is the incumbents' "Achilles heel." Still, listen to customers: Casetext's buyers chose $6,000 per seat for predictable budgets. On go-to-market: founders act as their own FDEs; sell top-down; run short, paid pilots with agreed success metrics; cap early pilots ("early demand trap"); in FDE motions, grow contract size and the value of the outcome while getting "less custom per every customer."

## What caveats or failure modes do they raise?

### Takeaway
The recurring warnings are these. FDE work can collapse into consulting ("a real risk that it's right"). Full-stack and services businesses carry gross-margin and ops drag (the Triplebyte and Atrium lessons). Demo-grade accuracy and pilot revenue that never converts produce what Heller calls "a mass extinction event" of "pilot recurring revenue." There is the early demand trap, along with zero- or negative-margin pilots, humans papering over product gaps, and physical on-site labor. Per-unit prices compress as competitors arrive and token costs fall. Teams about to be replaced will sabotage a sale pitched to them. Full autonomy is premature: "We need humans in the loop."

## Does anything they say bear on infrastructure, developer-tool, networking, privacy or security companies offering custom deployments as an outcome?

### Takeaway
Indirectly, yes. YC's newest RFS, "AI for the Air-Gapped Economy" (Brad Flora, Winter 2027), funds on-prem and no-internet AI deployments: "slower and harder to sell," but "where the largest budgets and the most defensible companies are." "Own Your Intelligence" (Jon Xu, Winter 2027) pitches owned, governed models for regulated industries. Bob McGrew's FDE playbook speaks directly to custom on-prem deployments: you "have to fight the IT team," you need a CEO-level sponsor to grant "authority to operate," you build a customizable platform delivered per site, and margins start negative. For dev tools, YC says to monetize open source "through services and hosting." YC also sees agents increasingly choosing dev tools ("make something agents want"), and floats "Self-Maintaining APIs," where vendors "apply" changes in customer codebases instead of announcing them. I found nothing networking-specific.

### Inferences
- For an infrastructure, privacy or security vendor, the YC material supports presenting a custom deployment as an outcome when four things hold: it solves a CEO top-five problem; an executive sponsor clears IT and security ("authority to operate"); pricing and contract size grow with the value delivered; and each deployment adds measurable "product leverage," moving from gravel road to paved road. Otherwise McGrew's warning applies: "You're probably going to end up doing services."
- YC's two current infrastructure theses sit close to outcome selling. Air-gapped AI is sold as a hard, high-budget deployment outcome. "Own your intelligence" implies ongoing work done for the customer: turning workflows into evals and failures into improvements.
- For dev tools, YC's lines are open source plus hosting or services monetization, technical founders selling directly, and increasingly agents as the buyers. That is a self-serve, agent-readable route, which differs from the FDE outcome route.

### Gaps
- I found no YC talk or RFS that addresses networking companies, VPN or connectivity products, or network security specifically.
- I found no YC discussion of pricing on-prem or air-gapped deployments by outcome, or of security accreditation costs beyond the RFS mention. The Giga fireside has a chapter titled "Designing for Compliance and Security" (@ 11:45) that I did not review. — [YC library: Giga fireside](https://www.ycombinator.com/library/cz-giga-the-ai-platform-for-enterprise-support)
