# TEE attestation verification for remote AI workloads (Intel TDX, AMD SEV-SNP, NVIDIA Hopper/Blackwell CC) and its worth against an operator with physical access

Notes compiled 2026-11-23. Every finding carries the date of its source; anything dated before 2026 is marked "older, may be superseded". "Observed 2026-11-23" means the researcher ran the request with curl that day (single sample, from the researcher's machine). Generation names: TDX = 4th Gen Xeon Scalable (Sapphire Rapids) and later; SEV-SNP = EPYC 7003 (Milan, Zen 3), 8004/9004 (Siena/Genoa, Zen 4), 8005/9005 (Turin, Zen 5); NVIDIA CC = Hopper (H100/H200) and Blackwell (B100/B200/GB200/B300).

## Q1. What is in a TDX quote, an SEV-SNP attestation report and an NVIDIA GPU attestation report; which fields must a verifier check; where collateral comes from; how TCB status and revocation are checked; how long it takes and which network calls it needs (alone and composed)

### Takeaway
Each TEE gives a signed blob holding a 64-byte caller-chosen field (TDX REPORTDATA, SNP REPORT_DATA), launch measurements, debug and policy bits, and a TCB/SVN level. The vendor's verification library checks only the signature chain and TCB currency. Measurements, debug bits, policy and REPORT_DATA are the relying party's job, and Intel says so explicitly. Collateral (Intel PCS TCB Info/QE Identity/CRLs; AMD KDS VCEK/ASK/ARK/CRL; NVIDIA RIMs plus OCSP or an NRAS token) costs about 0.2 to 0.6 s per HTTPS call uncached (observed 2026-11-23). It is cacheable: Intel TCB Info is valid for 30 days. GPU evidence is only meaningful when the attested CPU TEE collects it and binds it, because the GPU report alone is not bound to any particular CVM.

### Cited Findings

**Intel TDX quote: structure and who signs it**
- (2023-12, Intel TDX DCAP QGL/QVL Rev 0.9; older, may be superseded) Inside the TD, REPORTDATA goes into a TD report. The host's TD Quoting Enclave (TDQE, an SGX enclave) signs it with an Attestation Key (AK). The SGX Provisioning Certification Enclave (PCE) certifies the AK by signing the TDQE Report with the Provisioning Certification Key (PCK), "an asymmetric key unique to the processor package". The PCK Cert is "an X.509 certificate chain rooted in an Intel CA" published by the Intel SGX/TDX Provisioning Certification Service (PCS). A TD quote "contains at least the public key-part of the AK, the PCK Cert, the signature of the PCK" — [Intel TDX DCAP Quote Generation/Verification Library, Rev 0.9](https://download.01.org/intel-sgx/sgx-dcap/1.21/linux/docs/Intel_TDX_DCAP_Quoting_Library_API.pdf)
- (2023-12) The QVL "only support[s] QE Report Certification Data.CertType = 5", meaning the PCK cert chain is embedded in the quote, so a verifier does not have to fetch the PCK cert separately — [Intel TDX DCAP QGL/QVL](https://download.01.org/intel-sgx/sgx-dcap/1.21/linux/docs/Intel_TDX_DCAP_Quoting_Library_API.pdf)
- (2023-12) Minimum QVL checks: PCK cert signature chain; PCK cert not on CRL; signature chains of the collateral (PCK cert chain, TCB info chain, QE identity chain); collateral not on CRL; TDQE Report signature and the AK hash inside it, using the PCK cert; TDQE measurements; TD quote signature under the AK; "Evaluate the TDX TCB information contained in the TD Quote" — [Intel TDX DCAP QGL/QVL](https://download.01.org/intel-sgx/sgx-dcap/1.21/linux/docs/Intel_TDX_DCAP_Quoting_Library_API.pdf)
- (2023-12) Intel puts the body checks on the relying party: "the QVL does not verify the data contained in the TD Quote Body. The quote verifier is responsible to define what data needs to be checked." The list it gives: MROWNER/MROWNERCONFIG (assigned by the TD's creator), MRCONFIGID, MRTD (initial TD contents), RTMR[0]/RTMR[1] (by convention extended by the TD virtual firmware, TDVF; "may differ depending on the TDVF vendor"), RTMR[2]/RTMR[3] (OS and runtime; "For RTMR[3], contact the TD workload owner"), XFAM, and REPORTDATA — [Intel TDX DCAP QGL/QVL](https://download.01.org/intel-sgx/sgx-dcap/1.21/linux/docs/Intel_TDX_DCAP_Quoting_Library_API.pdf)
- (2023-12) Debug and security attributes: "Verify that all TD Under Debug flags (i.e., the TDATTRIBUTES.TUD field ...) are set to zero. If any flag is non-zero, the TD should not be trusted and thus should not be provisioned with production secrets." TUD is bits 7:0, with bit 0 = DEBUG. The TDATTRIBUTES.SEC bits must be set as expected, for example SEPT_VE_DISABLE (bit 28) for Linux. Other bits listed: PKS (30), KL (31), PERFMON (63) — [Intel TDX DCAP QGL/QVL](https://download.01.org/intel-sgx/sgx-dcap/1.21/linux/docs/Intel_TDX_DCAP_Quoting_Library_API.pdf)
- (2023-12) For multi-package Xeon platforms (sgx_type=1), the QVL's supplemental data also reports platform_instance_id, dynamic_platform, cached_keys and smt_enabled — [Intel TDX DCAP QGL/QVL](https://download.01.org/intel-sgx/sgx-dcap/1.21/linux/docs/Intel_TDX_DCAP_Quoting_Library_API.pdf)
- (Secondary, undated crate) The v4 quote body is 584 bytes and v5 adds 64 bytes — [docs.rs tdx-quote](https://docs.rs/tdx-quote/0.0.4/src/tdx_quote/lib.rs.html)
- (2024-12, expired individual IETF draft) Microsoft/Intel's TDX EAT profile names the claims a verifier exposes: tdx_mrsignerseam, tdx_mrseam, tdx_mrtd, tdx_rtmr0–3, tdx_mrconfigid, tdx_mrowner, tdx_mrownerconfig, tdx_report_data (64 bytes, "a nonce, a public key, or a hash"), tdx_seam_attributes, tdx_tee_tcb_svn, tdx_xfam, tdx_seamsvn, tdx_td_attributes plus the _debug/_key_locker/_perfmon/_protection_keys/_septve_disable sub-claims, attester_tcb_status and attester_advisory_ids — [draft-kdyxy-rats-tdx-eat-profile-02](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/html/draft-kdyxy-rats-tdx-eat-profile-02)
- (Undated vendor doc) Phala/dstack's register convention: RTMR0 = virtual hardware environment, RTMR1 = Linux kernel, RTMR2 = kernel cmdline + initrd, RTMR3 = app-id, compose-hash, instance-id and key-provider, extended as "RTMR3_new = SHA384(RTMR3_old || SHA384(event))". MRCONFIGID/MROWNER/MROWNERCONFIG are "Usually all zeros for dstack deployments" — [Phala attestation fields](https://docs.phala.com/phala-cloud/attestation/attestation-fields)

**Intel collateral, TCB status and revocation**
- (Observed 2026-11-23) Intel PCS TDX TCB Info for FMSPC 00806F050000 and for 90C06F000000: `tcbInfo.id`="TDX", version 3, `tcbEvaluationDataNumber`=20, issueDate 2026-11-23, nextUpdate 2026-12-23, so the collateral is valid for 30 days. It carries a `tdxModule` identity (mrsigner, attributes, attributesMask) and 2 `tdxModuleIdentities`. Its tcbLevels hold only UpToDate and OutOfDate, and the newest UpToDate level has tcbDate 2025-08-13. - (Undated) Real verifiers differ on policy. Oasis's lax mode accepts OutOfDate, Atlas uses grace periods, and Intel Trust Authority warns that a policy accepting only UpToDate can reject platforms that meet a minimum standard — [Atlas TCB status policy](https://www.mintlify.com/concrete-security/atlas/policy/tcb-status); [Intel Trust Authority](https://docs.trustauthority.intel.com/main/articles/concept-platform-tcb.html)

**Timing and network cost**
- (Observed 2026-11-23) Single uncached fetches from Intel PCS, AMD KDS and NVIDIA took 0.19 to 0.64 s each (see the endpoint findings above).
- (2025-12-18) Tinfoil's Go verifier compiled to WASM was "about 80MB", and initial page load was "sometimes reaching over 15 seconds". Its TypeScript rewrite is "just under 50KB" — [Tinfoil blog](https://tinfoil.sh/blog/2025-12-18-browser-native-verification)
- (2026-09-28) The TEE Anchor prototype reports provisioning and verification completing "within a few milliseconds" for its chip-ID endorsement check. That figure is for their scheme, not DCAP or SNP verification — [arXiv 2609.35919](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.35919)

### Inferences
- Minimum TDX checks before sending a byte, combining the Intel doc and the observed collateral (inference). Verify the quote signature, the embedded PCK chain to a pinned Intel SGX Root CA, the PCK CRL and the Root CA CRL, plus the TCB Info and QE Identity signatures. Require TCB status UpToDate, or an explicit, dated exception policy keyed on tcbEvaluationDataNumber/tcbDate. Check the QE identity, and check the TDX module identity (MRSIGNERSEAM/SEAMATTRIBUTES) against TCB Info's tdxModuleIdentities. Require TUD == 0 and the expected SEC bits (SEPT_VE_DISABLE for Linux) and XFAM. Pin MRTD and RTMR0–2, replay the event log for RTMR3, and require MRCONFIGID/MROWNER/MROWNERCONFIG to be zero or pinned. REPORTDATA must equal a hash over the channel binding, the client nonce and the GPU evidence digest.
- Minimum SNP checks (inference). Verify the signature with the VCEK or VLEK, chained to the pinned ARK of the right product (Milan/Genoa/Turin). Take TCB values from the VCEK cert extensions, not from the report body. Require REPORTED_TCB at or above a floor (for example the Mior floors), plus POLICY DEBUG=0, MIGRATE_MA=0, PAGE_SWAP_DISABLE=1 and ALIAS_CHECK_COMPLETE=1. Choose an SMT policy (see StackWarp in Q3). Pin VMPL, MEASUREMENT, HOST_DATA and the ID/author key digests. Reject a masked CHIP_ID wherever chip allowlisting is used.
- GPU checks (inference): for each GPU, require overall success, measres success, secboot, dbgstat disabled, a nonce match, good OCSP status, verified RIM signatures, driver/VBIOS versions at or above a floor, and CC mode on rather than DevTools. Accept GPU evidence only if the attested CVM collected it and REPORTDATA/REPORT_DATA binds its digest. A GPU token fetched over an unattested path can be "borrowed" (TEE.fail).
- Warm-cache verification (TCB Info valid 30 days, VCEK per chip and TCB, RIMs per driver/VBIOS version) should be crypto-only and fast. A cold verification is a handful of parallelizable HTTPS calls, each 0.2 to 0.6 s. These figures are from single samples; a real benchmark is a gap.

### Gaps
- No authoritative measurement of DCAP/SNP/GPU verification CPU time was found. The only figures are single-sample network timings and the TEE Anchor number for a different check.
- AMD KDS rate limits were not found; neither the KDS spec excerpt nor the libraries state them. The AMD ABI spec PDF (56860) and KDS spec PDF could not be downloaded (timeouts / HTML returned), so SNP field semantics come from go-sev-guest's implementation, not the spec text. Turin-specific TCB fields (such as an FMC SPL) were not confirmed.
- The exact format of NVIDIA's SPDM measurement blocks and opaque-data fields, including precisely how CC mode versus DevTools appears in the signed report, was not documented in the pages reached.
- The current Intel TDX quote v5 / TDX 2.0 body changes were not verified beyond the secondary "+64 bytes" note.

## Q2. How to bind attestation to the session so a man-in-the-middle (including the provider's API gateway) cannot read data; known pitfalls (relay/diversion, stale quotes, debug mode, missing GPU attestation, CPU-GPU bounce buffers)

### Takeaway
Intel's model puts a public key or a nonce in REPORTDATA. As of 2026 the IETF SEAT effort and a formal-analysis team hold that evidence embedded before or during the TLS handshake (RA-TLS style, "intra-handshake") is relay-prone. CVE-2026-33697 (CVSS 7.5) was filed against one such aTLS implementation. They recommend post-handshake attestation bound to a TLS exporter (draft-fossati-seat-expat-04, 2026-09-19), but no RFC or Go implementation exists yet. Application-layer HPKE to an attested key (Tinfoil's EHBP; Meta uses OHTTP plus RA-TLS) is the practical way to keep a provider's gateway from reading bodies, though headers, sizes and timing stay visible. Documented pitfalls in shipped systems: attestation with no client freshness, GPU attestation never verified, unmeasured config and ACPI tables, TCB levels read from the report, and evidence not bound to the operator's own machines. CPU-GPU bounce buffers are AES-GCM encrypted, not plaintext, but several control-plane metadata structures are not protected.

### Cited Findings
**REPORTDATA binding (vendor model)**
- (2023-12, older) Intel lists three uses of REPORTDATA: a verifier nonce for replay protection, a hash of data, and "a public key corresponding to a private key that has been generated within the TD" to start a secure channel — [Intel TDX DCAP QGL/QVL](https://download.01.org/intel-sgx/sgx-dcap/1.21/linux/docs/Intel_TDX_DCAP_Quoting_Library_API.pdf)
- (Undated) Phala/dstack uses report_data for "binding a public key to prove control of its private key, nonces for challenge-response, and hashes of application state" — [Phala attestation fields](https://docs.phala.com/phala-cloud/attestation/attestation-fields)
- (Undated, v0.10.21) The Tinfoil verifier flow is: the enclave returns "Report + TLS pubkey"; the client verifies the chain, fetches reference measurements from Sigstore and compares them, and "the enclave-generated certificate fingerprint is pinned for the session" — [tinfoilsh/verifier](https://pkg.go.dev/github.com/tinfoilsh/verifier@v0.10.21)

**Relay and diversion attacks on RA-TLS-style (intra-handshake) attestation**
- (NVD record, last modified 2026-03-30; search excerpt) CVE-2026-33697, CVSS 7.5: the attested TLS in Cocos AI's CoCoS v0.4.0–v0.8.2 (SEV-SNP and TDX) "is vulnerable to a relay attack". The reason: "the attestation evidence is bound to the ephemeral key but not to the TLS channel, possession of that key is sufficient to relay or divert the attested TLS session". Exploitation "requires the attacker to first extract the ephemeral TLS private key ... through physical access to the server hardware, transient execution attacks, or side-channel attacks". No patch existed at publication — [NVD CVE-2026-33697](https://nvd.nist.gov/vuln/detail/CVE-2026-33697)
- (2026-09-26, draft-intra-handshake-fail-46, Informational, SEAT; authored by the researchers making the claims) Lists as vulnerable: "Meta's AI" under CVE-2026-33697/EUVD-2026-16488; Edgeless Systems Contrast (GHSA, CVSS 7.4); the CCC attested-tls-poc (archived); Cocos AI up to v0.8.2 (since migrated to post-handshake); Privasys rustls and go (archived). Vulnerable specs: draft-fossati-tls-attestation-09 (-10 withdrawn after the CVE), draft-fossati-seat-early-attestation, draft-ritz-seat-facts. On the binder: it "does not prevent relay attacks as there is no shared secret in the binder", and "Binder MUST contain shared secrets". The draft states "post-handshake attestation alone, such as draft-fossati-seat-expat, can achieve level 3 binding" and that implementers "MUST urgently move to post-handshake attestation". **Conflict:** NVD names Cocos AI's CoCoS as the CVE's product while this draft lists the CVE under "Meta's AI"; most CVSS values in the draft are labeled preliminary — [draft-intra-handshake-fail-46](https://www.ietf.org/archive/id/draft-intra-handshake-fail-46.html)
- (2026-09-19, draft-fossati-seat-expat-04, Standards Track, workgroup SEAT, not an RFC) Uses RFC 9261 Exported Authenticators to carry `cmw_attestation` after the handshake. The binding value comes from the TLS exporter (label "EXPORTER-cmw-attestation" plus certificate_request_context) and is hashed with the end-entity public key, so freshness "is already ensured by the exporter value". Appendix B.2: intra-handshake "does not bind the Evidence to the application traffic secrets, resulting in relay attacks" and "is vulnerable to diversion attacks". Section 5.2: a platform could "generate the AIK private key outside the TEE"; "A Relying Party cannot detect this attack unless additional safeguards are in place"; the evidence must include AIK_pub_hash. "Formal analysis of post-handshake attestation is a work-in-progress." Cited formal work: "Identity Crisis in Confidential Computing: Formal Analysis of Attested TLS" (ACM CCS Asia 2026) and Sardar et al. (IEEE Access 2024) — [draft-fossati-seat-expat-04](https://www.ietf.org/archive/id/draft-fossati-seat-expat-04.html)
- (2026-02-01, FOSDEM) SEAT "has been formed to exclusively tackle this specific problem". The talk showed "replay, diversion and relay attacks" on pre- and intra-handshake designs. The implementation is a Rust work in progress (attestation-exported-authenticators, tested with QUIC and Intel TDX); no Go implementation was mentioned — [FOSDEM 2026 talk](https://archive.fosdem.org/2026/schedule/event/GHGFBM-attestedtls/)
- (2025-11, IETF 124 minutes, search excerpt) At SEAT's first meeting, pre- and intra-handshake solutions were judged "not well-suited for strong binding properties" — [IETF 124 SEAT minutes](https://ietfminutes.org/minutes/ietf124/seat.html). A related individual draft (2026-10) proposes binding attestation to forwarded TLS connections in split-endpoint deployments — [draft-larraia-seat-forward-attestation-00](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/draft-larraia-seat-forward-attestation/)

**HPKE / application-layer encryption to an attested key (gateway cannot read bodies)**
- (Current docs; Go module v0.2.0 published 2026-02-21) Tinfoil's EHBP encrypts HTTP bodies with HPKE (RFC 9180) and leaves headers "in the clear for routing", so a backend proxy can add credentials and rate limits "without reading content". Because browsers cannot pin certificates, the SDK fetches the enclave's attested HPKE public key. The encrypted request body "provides implicit authentication of the encapsulated key", so bodiless requests (GET/HEAD/DELETE/OPTIONS) "cannot have encrypted responses". An `Ehbp-Response-Nonce` header guards against stripping. Implementations: Go (reference), JavaScript, Swift — [Tinfoil EHBP docs](https://docs.tinfoil.sh/resources/ehbp); [EHBP SPEC.md](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/tinfoilsh/encrypted-http-body-protocol/HEAD/SPEC.md); [pkg.go.dev EHBP](https://pkg.go.dev/github.com/tinfoilsh/encrypted-http-body-protocol)
- (2025-04-29, older) WhatsApp Private Processing routes device traffic over OHTTP through a third-party relay (hiding IP from Meta), then runs an RA-TLS session between device and TEE, and checks measurements against a third-party ledger — [Meta engineering blog](https://engineering.fb.com/2025/04/29/security/whatsapp-private-processing-ai-tools/)

**Pitfalls found in shipped or audited systems**
- (2026-04-07 blog; 2025-08-26 report) In Trail of Bits' WhatsApp audit (28 issues, 8 High), the attestation lacked a client-provided value (TOB-WAPI-7), "so a stolen report could be replayed indefinitely". Meta fixed this by including the TLS `client_random` in every attestation. ToB recommends attesting "over a value derived from the handshake transcript". The report also lists "GPU LLMs do not verify NVIDIA GPU attestation" (High) and "SEV-SNP attestation is not bound to Meta-specific machines" — [Trail of Bits blog](https://blog.trailofbits.com/2026/04/07/what-we-learned-about-tee-security-from-auditing-whatsapps-private-inference/); [Trail of Bits report page](https://trailofbits.com/library/meta-whatsapp-private-processing/); [heise](https://heise.de/-10623797)
- (2026-04-07) Config loaded after measurement let an insider inject `LD_PRELOAD` while attestation still verified (TOB-WAPI-13). Unmeasured ACPI tables let a malicious hypervisor inject fake devices with memory access (TOB-WAPI-17). ToB's rule: "Every piece of data your TEE loads must either be part of the measured boot process or be treated as potentially hostile" — [Trail of Bits blog](https://blog.trailofbits.com/2026/04/07/what-we-learned-about-tee-security-from-auditing-whatsapps-private-inference/)
- (2026-09-14; 2026-11-13) Debug bits are necessary but not sufficient. DDRop (DDR5 interposer) switched a TDX victim "into debug mode, copied its memory in plaintext, then restored the original data". Mior (malicious BIOS/hypervisor plus a PCIe device, on an unpatched EPYC 9135 Zen 5) "enabled debugging on a confidential VM that had it disabled" and used PSP debug commands — [THN: DDRop](https://thehackernews.com/2026/09/new-ddrop-attack-breaks-intel-tdx-and.html); [THN: Mior and Skyfall](https://thehackernews.com/2026/11/new-mior-and-skyfall-attacks-let-rogue.html)

**CPU-GPU path: bounce buffers and multi-GPU**
- (2025-08-14, whitepaper v1.3) Bounce buffers are "nonsecure pages" in shared memory. Data in them "is encrypted and signed" by DMA engines using "256-bit AES-GCM encryption across all its ingress and egress paths", and the IV increments so replays fail the AuthTag check. TDISP/IDE inline encryption (no bounce buffers) is described as an option "In the future" for Blackwell B100/B200 with TDISP/IDE-compatible CPUs — [NVIDIA whitepaper](https://docs.nvidia.com/nvidia-secure-ai-with-blackwell-and-hopper-gpus-whitepaper.pdf)
- (2025-08-14) In Hopper Protected PCIe mode (only in eight-GPU, four-NVSwitch HGX Hopper air-cooled systems), "data traffic between GPUs over NVLINK remains unencrypted". In Blackwell multi-GPU pass-through "the NVLINK pathway is also encrypted", for up to eight GPUs — [NVIDIA whitepaper](https://docs.nvidia.com/nvidia-secure-ai-with-blackwell-and-hopper-gpus-whitepaper.pdf). (Older release notes) PPCIe was available "only with the Hopper GPUs and Intel CPUs with TDX", and confidentiality holds "up to the limits of the NIST SP800-38D AES-GCM standard", after which "the VM needs to be restarted" — [NVIDIA r550 release notes](https://docs.nvidia.com/confidential-computing/release-notes/r550-trd1.md)
- (arXiv v2 2026-04-17, MLSys 2026; tested on H100 SXM5 with an AMD EPYC 9634 SEV-SNP host) In CPU-GSP RPC, "only the command and status RPC payloads are encrypted". Address tables and queue/element headers are plaintext, and unprotected readPtr/writePtr allow reordering, repeating or omitting calls. Scrub pushbuffers are "signed but not encrypted". GPFIFO, GPPUT and tracking semaphores "remain unprotected in shared memory". There is a transfer-size timing channel, and 1,042 BAR0 fields still return values under CC. All findings went to NVIDIA PSIRT, and no vendor response is reported — [Gu et al., Blueprint, Bootstrap, and Bridge](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.02770v2)

### Inferences
- Against a gateway run by the provider, the client must encrypt to a key generated inside the attested TEE: HPKE/EHBP, or TLS that terminates inside the CVM over TCP passthrough. Plain server TLS that ends at the gateway gives no protection. Headers, request sizes, timing and routing metadata remain visible to the gateway either way.
- Binding only a TEE public key into REPORTDATA (RA-TLS style) is exactly the construction CVE-2026-33697 describes. Its exploit precondition, extracting the endpoint's private key, is what a physical-access operator can do (TEE.fail, DDRop). A client should therefore bind evidence to a per-session secret-derived value: a TLS exporter, a handshake-transcript hash, or for SSH the session's exchange hash. It should also include a fresh client nonce, and refuse evidence obtained outside the session. This is an inference; no SSH-specific attested-binding spec was found.
- For rented "enclave" VMs reached over SSH, the SSH host key should be generated inside the CVM and its digest placed in REPORT_DATA together with a client nonce. Even then, anyone who extracts that key with a physical attack can impersonate the enclave (inference).
- Plaintext bounce buffers are not the risk. The risks are Hopper multi-GPU NVLink (plaintext by design), control-plane metadata and timing leaks (Gu et al.), and any GPU whose evidence the attested CVM did not collect.

### Gaps
- No Go implementation of draft-fossati-seat-expat was found; the only one is a Rust work in progress. Whether any Go TLS stack supports RFC 9261 Exported Authenticators was not checked.
- The "ID-Crisis" CCS Asia 2026 paper itself was not read; it is cited only through the drafts.
- Whether NVIDIA fixed the metadata findings of Gu et al. (driver versions or CVEs) was not found.
- Meta's fix status for the GPU-attestation finding was not confirmed in the sources reached.

## Q3. Can an untrusted operator with physical access (e.g., an anonymous miner) defeat TDX, SEV-SNP or NVIDIA CC with published attacks, at what cost, does attestation reveal it, and what do Intel, AMD and NVIDIA say?

### Takeaway
Yes. Published, low-cost DRAM-bus interposers defeat every current CPU TEE for an operator who can open the chassis. TEE.fail (2025-10, under $1,000, passive DDR5) extracted an Intel PCK and forged TDX quotes that Intel's own QVL rated "UpToDate". It also extracted ECDSA keys from SEV-SNP VMs with ciphertext hiding on Zen 4/Zen 5, and it undermines NVIDIA CC because GPU trust is anchored in the CPU CVM. DDRop (2026-09, about $159 in parts, active DDR5) took over TDX VMs, forced debug mode and forged launch measurements. Battering RAM (2025-10, about $50, DDR4) broke Scalable SGX and replayed SEV-SNP launch measurements. Attestation does not reveal any of this, because the forged or replayed evidence is cryptographically valid. Intel, AMD and Arm call physical attacks on DRAM out of scope. NVIDIA scopes in only "basic physical attacks" and excludes "sophisticated physical attacks". NVIDIA's on-package HBM is out of reach of these interposers, but its attestation inherits the CPU's weakness. Separately, 2025-2026 brought host-software attacks (RMPocalypse, Heracles, StackWarp, Mior, Skyfall, TDXRay). These are fixable by firmware or microcode, and a verifier can partly detect them through TCB floors.

### Cited Findings
**Physical DRAM-interposer attacks**
- (2025-10; IEEE S&P 2026 poster) TEE.fail, from Georgia Tech, Purdue and others. A passive DDR5 interposer, needing only one DDR5 channel and built for "under $1000", exploits deterministic AES-XTS memory encryption. Intel: affects "all Intel CPUs using DDR5 memory ... Scalable Xeon CPUs from the 4th generation onwards, i.e., all CPUs currently supporting TDX". The PCK was extracted from the PCE "in a fully automated manner from a single signing operation". Forged TDX quotes verified with Intel's DCAP QVL at "UpToDate", and "properly forged SGX and TDX attestation quotes are cryptographically indistinguishable from legitimate ones". AMD: SEV-SNP on Zen 4 and Zen 5 EPYC with DDR5, with Ciphertext Hiding (introduced on Zen 5) enabled ("Ciphertext Hiding only prevents the software view of encrypted memory"); ECDSA keys were extracted from constant-time OpenSSL inside an SNP VM. NVIDIA: affected indirectly, with H100/H200 and B100/B200 named, because GPU reports are not bound to a CVM. Demonstrated against BuilderNet (TDX on Azure, including forging the Azure TPM attestation that relies on TDX), dstack ("a malicious provider could run a workload in a non-confidential VM with forged attestation") and Secret Network (SGX consensus seed) — [TEE.fail](https://tee.fail/); [IEEE S&P 2026 poster](https://www.ieee-security.org/TC/SP2026/downloads/posters/sp2026posters-final75.pdf); [SecurityWeek](https://securityweek.com/new-attack-targets-ddr5-memory-to-steal-keys-from-intel-and-amd-tees)
- (2025-10) TEE.fail vendor positions: Intel "Considers interposer attacks out of the threat model for SGX and TDX", with no mitigation beyond physical security. AMD "does not plan firmware updates" (AMD-SB-3040). On NVIDIA, the authors say "there are no mitigations from the Nvidia CC side" — [TEE.fail](https://tee.fail/). Intel's 2025-10-28 announcement says TEE.fail does not change its "previous out of scope statement" — [Intel security announcement 2025-10-28](https://intel.com/content/www/us/en/security-center/announcement/intel-security-announcement-2025-10-28-001.html)
- (2025-10) WireTap, from Georgia Tech and Purdue. A passive DDR4 interposer costing under $1,000 recovered the SGX Quoting Enclave's ECDSA attestation key on Intel Scalable SGX (DDR4 servers), allowing forged quotes. Intel: out of threat model; AES-XTS gives "limited confidentiality, and no integrity or anti-replay protection against attackers with physical capabilities"; no CVE. It was not tested on TDX because TDX systems use DDR5 — [THN: WireTap](https://thehackernews.com/2025/10/new-wiretap-attack-extracts-intel-sgx.html); [Keysight](https://www.keysight.com/blogs/en/tech/nwvs/2025/10/22/security-highlight-dram-interposer-attacks-on-confidential-computing); [Intel: encrypted memory frameworks](https://www.intel.com/content/www/us/en/developer/articles/news/more-information-encrypted-memory-frameworks.html)
- (2025-09-30 embargo end) Battering RAM, KU Leuven and Birmingham. An active DDR4 interposer with a $47.62 bill of materials. On Intel Scalable SGX and Client SGX it gives "arbitrary plaintext access". On SEV-SNP it captures and replays launch measurements, which "breaks SEV's attestation feature on fully patched systems". DDR5 is not affected by this interposer, Arm CCA was untested, and boot-time alias checks do not stop it because the interposer is toggled after boot. Intel and AMD said "physical attacks on DRAM are out of scope for their current products" (AMD-SB-3024). The authors: "defending against Battering RAM would require a fundamental redesign of memory encryption itself" — [batteringram.eu](https://batteringram.eu/); [AMD-SB-3024](https://www.amd.com/en/resources/product-security/bulletin/amd-sb-3024.html). Intel points to TME-MK's cryptographic-integrity mode as extra protection against alias-based attacks — [Intel: encrypted memory frameworks](https://www.intel.com/content/www/us/en/developer/articles/news/more-information-encrypted-memory-frameworks.html)
- (2026-09-14; ACM CCS 2026) DDRop, from KU Leuven, ETH Zurich, Durham and Google. An active DDR5 interposer, about $159 in parts per unit ("under $200") and fitted "in minutes", silently drops writes so stale ciphertext is read back. Affected: Intel TDX, Intel Scalable SGX and AMD SEV-SNP, all of which "encrypt memory without the freshness check". Not affected: Intel Client SGX, which has an integrity tree. NVIDIA CC GPUs are out of reach because their memory sits inside the package. On TDX, under the default logical-integrity mode, the attacker read victim memory, toggled debug mode, and "overwrote the launch measurement, so an attacker-controlled VM could pass attestation as trusted". On SNP it copied pages. Intel's cryptographic-integrity mode "already blocks part of DDRop" (reads and debug toggling), but attestation forgery "would still work" according to the researchers; this was untested. AMD: "falls outside the scope of the published threat model for SEV / SNP" (AMD-SB-3048). Intel: no CVE, "out of scope, but not out of mind" — [THN: DDRop](https://thehackernews.com/2026/09/new-ddrop-attack-breaks-intel-tdx-and.html). Per a search excerpt, Intel said it is "evaluating additional architectural hardening options and detection mechanisms" — [CSA DDRop note](https://labs.cloudsecurityalliance.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/CSA_research_note_ddrop_confidential_computing_trust_20260916-csa-styled.pdf)
- (2024-12, older) BadRAM: SPD metadata is changed so a DIMM reports double its size, creating aliases. Affects 3rd and 4th Gen EPYC (CVE-2024-21944, AMD-SB-3015). It is fixed by firmware (Milan PI 1.0.0.D with SEV FW 1.55.22; Genoa PI 1.0.0.D with SEV FW 1.55.38) that runs alias checks and sets ALIAS_CHECK_COMPLETE in the report, so attestation does reveal the mitigation — [AMD-SB-3015](https://www.amd.com/en/resources/product-security/bulletin/amd-sb-3015.html); [SecurityWeek: BadRAM ($10 equipment)](https://www.securityweek.com/badram-attack-uses-10-equipment-to-break-amd-processor-protections/)

**Vendor threat-model statements**
- (2025-08-14, NVIDIA whitepaper v1.3) In scope: "Software attacks", "Basic physical attacks", "Software rollback attacks", "Cryptographical attacks", "Data rollback and replay attacks". Out of scope: "Sophisticated physical attacks", "Denial of service attacks". Goal: "Ensure that interposers on buses such as PCIe and DDR memory cannot leak data or code". JTAG is disabled and BMC paths are reduced to "an anonymous card health-only level" in CC mode — [NVIDIA whitepaper](https://docs.nvidia.com/nvidia-secure-ai-with-blackwell-and-hopper-gpus-whitepaper.pdf)
- (Older Intel TDX whitepaper, via search excerpt) TDX does not defend against replay of memory through physical attacks. TDX has two modes: Logical Integrity (Li), using TME-MK, and Cryptographic Integrity (Ci), using TME-MKi with a 28-bit MAC per 64-byte line. Per-SKU support for Ci was not established — [Intel TDX whitepaper](https://www.intel.com/content/dam/develop/external/us/en/documents/tdx-whitepaper-final9-17.pdf); [Intel SoC memory protection doc (2026 WW23)](https://cdrdv2-public.intel.com/869103/2026%20WW23%20SoC%20Memory%20Protection%20869103-0011.pdf); [enclaive TDX integrity explainer](https://docs.enclaive.cloud/confidential-cloud/technology-in-depth/intel-tdx/technology/fundamentals/memory-encryption/confidentiality-and-integrity)

**Host-software / firmware attacks (no physical access needed; patchable; partly detectable via TCB)**
- (2025, CCS 2025, AMD-SB-3020, CVE-2025-0033) RMPocalypse: a host writes to the RMP during SNP initialization. Zen 3, Zen 4 and Zen 5 are affected; fixed by firmware, with some embedded parts scheduled for 2025-11 — [AMD-SB-3020](https://www.amd.com/en/resources/product-security/bulletin/AMD-SB-3020.html); [RMPocalypse paper](https://rmpocalypse.github.io/rmpocalypse-CCS2025.pdf)
- (2025, CCS 2025, AMD-SB-3021) Heracles, from ETH Zurich: a chosen-plaintext oracle in which the hypervisor moves guest pages and re-encrypts them. Affects 3rd and 4th Gen EPYC per AMD. Mitigation: "the ability of the hypervisor to move or swap guest pages can be disabled via guest-specific policy controls", i.e. PAGE_SWAP_DISABLE, which a secondary source dates to ABI 1.58 (May 2025) — [AMD-SB-3021](https://www.amd.com/en/resources/product-security/bulletin/amd-sb-3021.html); [Heracles](https://heracles-attack.github.io)
- (2023-11, older) CacheWarp (CVE-2023-20592, AMD-SB-3005): INVD abuse. Among SNP parts it affects Zen 3 (EPYC 7003, fixed by microcode). Zen 4 Genoa is not affected — [AMD-SB-3005](https://www.amd.com/en/resources/product-security/bulletin/amd-sb-3005.html)
- (2026-01-19) StackWarp, from CISPA (CVE-2025-29943, AMD-SB-3027, CVSS 4.6): an attacker on a hyperthread sibling corrupts the guest stack pointer, which yielded RSA key recovery and kernel code execution. Zen 1–5 are affected (EPYC 7003/8004/9004/9005). Microcode shipped in July and October 2025. The researchers advise "a temporary disablement [of hyperthreading] for CVMs that have particularly high integrity requirements" — [THN: StackWarp](https://thehackernews.com/2026/01/new-stackwarp-hardware-flaw-breaks-amd.html)
- (2026-11-13; CCS 2026) Mior (CVE-2026-0462, AMD-SB-3059, CVSS 4.0 5.9) needs hypervisor control, BIOS control and a PCIe device. PSP writes to the RMP are redirected so part of the RMP stays uninitialized. The team enabled debugging and read and wrote VM memory, and forged attestation by copying a benign VM's measurement into a malicious VM. Tested only on an EPYC 9135 (Zen 5), with 1,000 of 1,000 attempts succeeding and ciphertext hiding no help. AMD's advisory lists EPYC 7003, 8004, 8005, 9004 and 9005. SEV firmware fixes came 2026-08-12; platform firmware is targeted for 2026-12. **Conflict:** the two advisories give different target months for TurinPI 1.0.0.C — [THN: Mior and Skyfall](https://thehackernews.com/2026/11/new-mior-and-skyfall-attacks-let-rogue.html); [CSA research note](https://labs.cloudsecurityalliance.org/research/csa-research-note-confidential-ai-inference-tee-limits-20261/)
- (2026-11-13; IEEE S&P) Skyfall, from ETH Zurich and Google (CVE-2026-40671, AMD-SB-3061, CVSS 4.9): a transient-execution leak of SNP guest register state left over after deliberately failed VM starts. A disk-encryption key was recovered. Shown on Zen 3 (EPYC 7313) and Zen 4 (EPYC 9124/9334), not on Zen 5. Microcode came 2026-08-12, platform firmware is due 2026-11, and PoC code is public — [THN: Mior and Skyfall](https://thehackernews.com/2026/11/new-mior-and-skyfall-attacks-let-rogue.html)
- (2026-04-02) TDXRay, from UCSD, CISPA and Google: host-side microarchitectural tracing of unmodified TDX guests (SEPTrace, Load+Probe, TSX-Probe, MWAIT-Probe). Intel says it can be mitigated in software by following its side-channel guidance — [Intel announcement 2026-04-02](https://www.intel.com/content/www/us/en/security-center/announcement/security-announcement-2026-04-02-001.html); [CISPA](https://cispa.de/en/research/publications/104815-tdxray-microarchitectural-side-channel-analysis-of-intel-tdx-for-real-world-workloads)
- (2026-02) A Google Cloud review of TDX Live Migration and TD Partitioning, done with Intel, found one VMM-to-full-TD-compromise bug and four memory leaks in those features — [arXiv 2602.11434](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2602.11434)
- (2026-11-16) The NEAR AI SGLang RCE (reported 2026-11-09) shows the limit from the inside: "a TEE does not protect a workload from its own software"; the inference engine is part of the TCB — [CSA research note](https://labs.cloudsecurityalliance.org/research/csa-research-note-confidential-ai-inference-tee-limits-20261/)

**Detection and mitigation proposals**
- (2025-10) TEE.fail's advice: physical security; "Do not trust attestation reports without understanding the machine's location"; design assuming the TEE can be compromised — [TEE.fail](https://tee.fail/)
- (2026-04-12) Flashbots: attestation "provides no evidence of the processor's physical location" and "looks identical whether the machine sits in a hyperscaler data center or a garage". Proof of Cloud can be built via DCEA (binding CVM attestation to a TPM rooted in the infrastructure owner, prototyped on Google Cloud and on TDX with Intel TXT), Intel POE, or "cloud-provider-signed PPID lists". Limitations: "essentially a supply chain snapshot"; a colluding operator or a stolen, unreset machine keeps valid IDs until revoked — [Flashbots: Mind the Gap](https://writings.flashbots.net/mind-the-gap-tee-poc); [DCEA arXiv 2510.12469](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.12469)
- (2026-09-28) TEE Anchor: an organization issues X.509 certificates over CPU Chip IDs, and verifiers match the Chip ID in the evidence against them, so the evidence proves "organizational affiliation" — [arXiv 2609.35919](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.35919)

### Inferences
- With a physical-access operator, CPU TEE attestation proves at most "some genuine Intel/AMD chip signed this, or someone holding a key extracted from one did". Since TEE.fail, a PCK extracted from any one TDX machine lets its holder forge UpToDate quotes for arbitrary measurements, including on a machine with no TEE. Accepting "any genuine Intel platform" therefore accepts forgeries. Pinning the PPID or CHIP_ID to an allowlist of machines in trusted custody limits the damage to those machines. The listed vendors and research groups converge on this approach (Proof of Cloud, TEE Anchor, Meta's CPU allowlist); it is not an Intel/AMD guarantee.
- For anonymously operated hardware, the honest statement is: TDX, SEV-SNP and NVIDIA CC protect against a remote or software-only host attacker who has not yet extracted keys, and only at current TCB. They do not protect against a motivated owner with about $200 to $1,000 of interposer hardware and lab skill. Such an owner can read memory or forge attestation, and no current attestation field reveals it.
- Firmware/microcode-class bugs (RMPocalypse, Heracles, StackWarp, Mior, Skyfall, CacheWarp, BadRAM) can partly be enforced against with TCB floors and policy bits (PAGE_SWAP_DISABLE, ALIAS_CHECK_COMPLETE, SMT off). Mior's BIOS-level fix is not reflected in TCB, so a hostile owner can simply not install it.

### Gaps
- Exact affected-generation lists for TEE.fail by SKU (Emerald Rapids, Granite Rapids; Genoa vs Turin) were not given on the TEE.fail page beyond "4th gen onward" and "Zen 4/Zen 5". DDRop's CPU generations were not stated.
- No published attack on NVIDIA HBM or the GPU itself by physical means was found. The NVIDIA impact is via the CPU anchor and GPU-report borrowing.
- Which Xeon SKUs ship TDX Cryptographic Integrity (Ci), and whether Ci blocks DDRop's attestation forgery, are unconfirmed.
- TDXdown, Heckler, WeSee and other 2024 TDX/SNP interrupt and #VC attacks were not researched here.

## Q4. Which open-source verifiers exist (Go preferred), with licenses and maturity; what dstack, Tinfoil, Edgeless Constellation/Contrast, Confidential Containers and Azure/GCP use; what runs on iOS/Android/wasm and what needs a helper

### Takeaway
Go has solid CPU-side verifiers from Google: go-tdx-guest and go-sev-guest (Apache-2.0, "not an officially supported Google product"). Tinfoil's verifier (AGPL-3.0) wraps them with Sigstore and has been compiled to both WASM and gomobile. Intel's Go client for Intel Trust Authority is BSD-3-Clause, with TDX GA and SNP/GPU in preview. GPU verification is the weak spot. NVIDIA's local verifier is C++ (the Python one is deprecated) and still calls NVIDIA's RIM and OCSP services. The only Go GPU library found (go-nvtrust) verifies through NRAS. In browsers, AMD KDS and the NRAS JWKS allow CORS, but Intel PCS and NVIDIA's RIM service do not (observed 2026-11-23), so a browser client needs a collateral relay or a pre-aggregated bundle.

### Inferences
- A Go sidecar can verify TDX and SNP evidence natively. The verify and validate paths in go-tdx-guest and go-sev-guest are ordinary Go code; only their `client` packages need a Linux guest device, and Tinfoil shipped this logic via gomobile and WASM. On iOS and Android (gomobile) there is no CORS barrier, so direct PCS/KDS fetches work. In the browser extension, Intel PCS and NVIDIA RIM need a relay or a bundle. A relay that only forwards vendor-signed collateral is not trusted for integrity, but it can withhold or serve stale collateral. The client must therefore enforce issueDate/nextUpdate on TCB Info and CRLs, and enforce tcbEvaluationDataNumber floors.
- WASM size is a real constraint: about 80 MB for Go with Sigstore. Choices include TinyGo or a smaller dependency set; Tinfoil instead rewrote the browser verifier in TypeScript.
- For GPUs, a Go client realistically has two choices. One is to trust NRAS-signed JWTs (verify via the CORS-enabled JWKS) and accept NVIDIA's service as an online dependency. The other is to run NVIDIA's C++ verifier in a helper (not possible in a browser). No pure-Go local GPU verifier was found.
- Licenses matter for embedding: Google's libraries are Apache-2.0 and Intel's client is BSD-3-Clause, but Tinfoil's verifier is AGPL-3.0, which constrains reuse in a distributed client.

- Not researched: Phala's Rust dcap-qvl (and its wasm build), Veraison, virtee/sev (Rust) and Intel's QVL license and maturity.
- Whether NRAS's POST attestation endpoint allows CORS was not tested. OCSP CORS was also not determined, since GET returns 405.
- Whether go-tdx-guest supports quote v5/TDX 2.0 bodies today was not verified.

## Q5. How should a client pin acceptable measurements for a VM image the customer builds (e.g., a Debian image with an agent harness), and how does the customer learn and check that measurement?

### Takeaway
Measurements are reproducible only if every launch input is pinned. For TDX that means the provider's TDVF/OVMF binary (MRTD), the virtual hardware and ACPI (RTMR0), the kernel/UKI (RTMR1), and cmdline+initrd (RTMR2). For SNP it means OVMF plus the hashes of kernel, initrd and cmdline, plus vCPU count and type (MEASUREMENT). The pattern deployments use: build reproducibly (Yocto, or mkosi/Nix), compute expected values offline with tooling (dstack-mr, sev-snp-measure), and publish them with provenance (Sigstore/Rekor, on-chain allowlists, CLI-embedded values, transparency logs). Clients then pin the tuple and replay event logs for runtime registers. Anything loaded after measurement must be measured into RTMR3 or treated as hostile.

### Cited Findings
- (2023-12, older) RTMR conventions: RTMR[0]/[1] are extended by TDVF and "may differ depending on the TDVF vendor"; RTMR[2]/[3] by the OS and runtime — [Intel TDX DCAP QGL/QVL](https://download.01.org/intel-sgx/sgx-dcap/1.21/linux/docs/Intel_TDX_DCAP_Quoting_Library_API.pdf). go-tdx-guest's `rtmr` package extends digests or event logs (SHA-384 only) — [go-tdx-guest](https://github.com/google/go-tdx-guest)
- (Undated) dstack: MRTD measures OVMF, RTMR1 holds the UKI, RTMR0–2 cover the boot chain, and RTMR3 holds runtime data. dstack-mr outputs MRTD, RTMR0–2 and aggregates (`mr_aggregated = SHA256(MRTD + RTMR0 + RTMR1 + RTMR2)`). Building the same dstack version on different machines gives identical measurements. Per a search excerpt of an arXiv paper, the dm-verity root hash is embedded in the UKI measured into RTMR1. Sources disagree slightly on which RTMRs count as "OS" — [Phala verify the platform](https://docs.phala.com/phala-cloud/attestation/verify-the-platform); [Atlas: computing measurements](https://www.mintlify.com/concrete-security/atlas/policy/computing-measurements); [arXiv 2606.03323](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.03323)
- (Undated) virtee/sev-snp-measure computes the expected SEV/SEV-ES/SEV-SNP launch measurement. Inputs include the vCPU type (as a model name or family/model/stepping) and OVMF, with an `snp:ovmf-hash` precompute. Under QEMU/OVMF "kernel-hashes", the kernel, initrd and cmdline hashes are put in a table measured with OVMF, and OVMF refuses to boot on a mismatch. For SNP, each page update folds into a SHA-384 chain that becomes MEASUREMENT — [sev-snp-measure](https://github.com/virtee/sev-snp-measure); [QEMU design discussion (2021, older)](https://lore.kernel.org/all/YbtkzdpERCnODpft@redhat.com/); [SNP primer (arXiv 2608.04039)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.04039)
- (go-sev-guest) SNP launch identities a verifier can pin instead of, or alongside, raw MEASUREMENT: HOST_DATA (32 B, set at launch), FAMILY_ID/IMAGE_ID, ID_KEY_DIGEST/AUTHOR_KEY_DIGEST (with `RequireIDBlock`/`RequireAuthorKey` and trusted key lists), and GUEST_SVN — [go-sev-guest](https://github.com/google/go-sev-guest); [go-sev-guest abi.go](https://github.com/google/go-sev-guest/blob/main/abi/abi.go)
- (2024-02, FOSDEM 2024; older) Edgeless engineers described building reproducible OS images with mkosi and Nix, "from source code in Git to the reference values for remote attestation"; the talk was titled "why remote attestation is worthless without it" — [FOSDEM 2024 talk](https://archive.fosdem.org/2024/schedule/event/fosdem-2024-1769-reproducible-builds-for-confidential-computing-why-remote-attestation-is-worthless-without-it/)
- (Undated) Contrast embeds reference values in its CLI, reproducible offline. Constellation notes that only some PCRs are reproducible, and that cloud-provider-controlled values need manual config updates — [Contrast attestation](https://docs.edgeless.systems/contrast/architecture/attestation); [Constellation attestation](https://docs.edgeless.systems/constellation/2.6/architecture/attestation)
- (2025-12-18) Tinfoil publishes releases via GitHub Actions to the Sigstore transparency log. The verifier must "check that the signed measurements from the attestation report match the open-source code and weights" — [Tinfoil blog](https://tinfoil.sh/blog/2025-12-18-browser-native-verification); [tinfoilsh/verifier](https://pkg.go.dev/github.com/tinfoilsh/verifier@v0.10.21)
- (2026-04-07) "Transparency requires reproducibility". Full reproducibility of Meta's CVM images "remained difficult" (TOB-WAPI-18). Meta added build pipelines with provenance verification and published authorized host identities in external logs — [Trail of Bits blog](https://blog.trailofbits.com/2026/04/07/what-we-learned-about-tee-security-from-auditing-whatsapps-private-inference/)
- (2024-06, older; bounty and SOC 3 current through 2026) Apple PCC publishes the binaries it runs, a transparency log researchers can check for consistency, and a Virtual Research Environment that boots releases — [Apple PCC security research](https://security.apple.com/blog/pcc-security-research/); [Apple PCC blog](https://security.apple.com/blog/private-cloud-compute/)

### Inferences
- Recommended pattern for a customer-built Debian image with an agent harness (inference, not a vendor recipe):
  1. Build reproducibly (Debian reproducible packages plus mkosi), producing a UKI (kernel + initrd + cmdline) and a dm-verity root filesystem whose root hash is on the measured cmdline. Writable state goes on a disk encrypted with a key derived or released only after attestation.
  2. Get the exact provider firmware (TDVF/OVMF) and VM shape (vCPU count/type, memory, ACPI), or ship your own firmware if the provider allows it. MRTD/RTMR0 (TDX) and MEASUREMENT (SNP) depend on these.
  3. Compute expected values offline: dstack-mr-style tooling for MRTD/RTMR0–2, sev-snp-measure for SNP.
  4. Publish the tuple {firmware digest, MRTD/RTMR0–2 or SNP MEASUREMENT, policy/attribute bits, min TCB} with signed provenance to a transparency log (Sigstore Rekor, as Tinfoil does).
  5. The sidecar pins that tuple, replays the CCEL/RTMR3 event log for anything the harness measures at runtime (container digests, config), and requires REPORT_DATA to bind the session (Q2).
- Because MRTD and RTMR0 vary by provider firmware and VM configuration, a single "image hash" is not enough. Each provider/firmware/VM-shape combination yields its own pinned value, so the customer must either control the firmware or maintain an allowlist per provider configuration.
- SSH access into the enclave changes post-launch state that launch measurements do not cover. For the attestation to keep meaning anything, the harness must extend RTMR3 (or an SNP vTPM PCR) for code it loads, or the design must accept that the customer's own SSH session is trusted.

### Gaps
- No vendor-neutral, maintained tool was found that computes TDX MRTD/RTMR0 for arbitrary (non-dstack) OVMF/TDVF builds and VM shapes. dstack-mr is dstack-specific, and its GCP fork's README has placeholder data.
- Whether specific providers let tenants supply their own TDVF/OVMF, and how their ACPI tables are measured, was not researched.
- Rekor's use for publishing reference measurements was confirmed only for Tinfoil. No Rekor use was found for Edgeless.

## Q6. How do real confidential-AI deployments handle these limits (TCB requirements, cloud-only hardware, multiple attesters, composite CPU+GPU)?

### Takeaway
Production systems do not rely on attestation alone against physical attackers. Apple, Google, Meta and Secret Labs keep hardware in their own or certified data centers. Meta binds attestation to its own CPUs and logs host identities. Apple designs so that targeting individual users needs a broad, detectable compromise. After TEE.fail, several decentralized TEE networks (Phala, Secret, Oasis, Flashbots, Nillion and others) formed the Proof of Cloud Alliance: an on-site-verified registry of PPIDs and chip IDs. Composite CPU+GPU verification is now standard in vendor tooling (Intel Trust Authority, NRAS). But audits found shipped systems that skipped GPU attestation, freshness and TCB checks, and November 2026 incidents (Mior/Skyfall, NEAR AI SGLang RCE) show TCB floors and in-TEE software hardening are both still required.

### Cited Findings
- (2025-04 design; 2025-08 audits; 2026-04 ToB blog) Meta WhatsApp Private Processing: SEV-SNP plus NVIDIA CC, OHTTP plus RA-TLS, and a third-party ledger. NCC: "enforceable guarantees primarily rely on the robustness of AMD SEV-SNP and NVIDIA Confidential Compute", and hardware attacks were "deemed infeasible as long as transparency logs include device IDs". ### Inferences
- The industry pattern for an untrusted physical operator is not stronger attestation but restricting which machines count: hyperscaler or certified data-center custody, chip-ID/PPID allowlists maintained by a party the client trusts (Proof of Cloud, TEE Anchor, Meta's CPU controls), and transparency logs of host identities. A design that accepts anonymous, self-hosted hardware should label that path "integrity/attestation against remote software attackers only". It should not call it confidential against the operator, unless it adds a custody proof the client can check (e.g., a Proof of Cloud registry entry for the CHIP_ID/PPID in the evidence).
- Where custody cannot be proven, the remaining risk-reduction levers are diffusion (spread requests so one compromised node sees little, as in Apple's anti-targeting goal), data minimization, and short-lived per-session keys. They reduce the blast radius; they do not restore confidentiality.

### Gaps
- How Proof of Cloud entries are queried programmatically (API, signed list format) and how many machines are registered were not found.
- Whether any production network enforces SMT-off, PAGE_SWAP_DISABLE or TCB floors for third-party GPU hosts was not found in public documentation.
- Phala's own post-TEE.fail policy (beyond Proof of Cloud membership) was not found in Phala's docs.
