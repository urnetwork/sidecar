# Buyer language: agents that do not compound

Scope: words operators, engineering leaders, and 2025–2026 studies use for the pain of agents (and genAI tools) that do not retain context, reach internal systems, or turn use into reusable value. Audience is companies that already run AI. URnetwork was not researched. Today is 2026-10-31.

Source rule used below: a claim is cited only if the page or PDF was opened in this pass. Search snippets that were not opened (including McKinsey’s State of AI pages, which returned HTTP errors on fetch) are gaps, not findings.

Provenance note on the MIT NANDA report: an mit.edu-hosted original was not found. The file opened is a third-party rehost of a PDF whose title page reads “The GenAI Divide / STATE OF AI IN BUSINESS 2025 / MIT NANDA,” authors Aditya Challapally, Chris Pease, Ramesh Raskar, Pradyumna Chari, July 2025: [v0.1 PDF mirror](https://cloudelligent.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/02/v0.1_State_of_AI_in_Business_2025_Report.pdf). Page 2 calls it “Preliminary Findings,” research period January–June 2025, and says the views are the authors’, not affiliated employers. Methodology on that page: systematic review of over 300 publicly disclosed AI initiatives, structured interviews with representatives from 52 organizations, and survey responses from 153 senior leaders at four industry conferences. Several exhibits are labeled directional, not official company reporting. The same PDF uses “95%” for more than one denominator (organizations with zero return; enterprise solutions that fail; custom tools that do not reach production). Those are quoted separately and not collapsed.

## What pains do production-agent teams name in 2025–2026 primary sources?

### Takeaway
The buyer-side language that shows up in the opened NANDA PDF is not “we need more AI value.” It is that tools do not learn, do not keep context between sessions, do not plug into existing systems, and therefore stall as pilots while employees keep using personal chat tools. Engineering-side language (Anthropic, Cognition, Chroma, LangChain, Every, Block) names the same break as lost session memory, context rot, handoffs, and work that does not get written back into the next run.

### Cited Findings
- Context does not survive the session. A corporate lawyer, quoted in the NANDA PDF: “It’s excellent for brainstorming and first drafts, but it doesn’t retain knowledge of client preferences or learn from previous edits. It repeats the same mistakes and requires extensive context input for each session. For high-stakes work, I need a system that accumulates knowledge and improves over time.” Barrier labels on the same exhibit include “Too much manual context required each time” and “It doesn’t learn from our feedback.” — [NANDA v0.1 PDF, July 2025, pp. 13–15](https://cloudelligent.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/02/v0.1_State_of_AI_in_Business_2025_Report.pdf)
- The report’s own summary of that pain: “The core barrier to scaling is not infrastructure, regulation, or talent. It is learning. Most GenAI systems do not retain feedback, adapt to context, or improve over time.” Myth 4 in the same PDF: “What’s really holding it back is that most AI tools don’t learn and don’t integrate well into workflows.” — [NANDA v0.1 PDF, July 2025](https://cloudelligent.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/02/v0.1_State_of_AI_in_Business_2025_Report.pdf)
- Agents that cannot reach internal systems, in a buyer’s words: “If it doesn’t plug into Salesforce or our internal systems, no one’s going to use it.” Adjacent procurement lines in the same table: “Most vendors don’t get how our approvals or data flows work”; “I can’t risk client data mixing with someone else’s model, even if the vendor says it’s fine.” — [NANDA v0.1 PDF, July 2025, p. 15](https://cloudelligent.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/02/v0.1_State_of_AI_in_Business_2025_Report.pdf)
- No shared memory across people, stated as a product requirement rather than a feature name: “Winning startups build systems that learn from feedback (66% of executives want this), retain context (63% demand this), and customize deeply to specific workflows.” A separate quote: “It’s useful the first week, but then it just repeats the same mistakes. Why would I use that?” — [NANDA v0.1 PDF, July 2025, pp. 14–15](https://cloudelligent.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/02/v0.1_State_of_AI_in_Business_2025_Report.pdf)
- Shadow AI, using that phrase. Section title: “The shadow AI economy.” “While only 40% of companies say they purchased an official LLM subscription, workers from over 90% of the companies we surveyed reported regular use of personal AI tools for work tasks.” The report says this use is “often without IT knowledge or approval,” and that official initiatives stay in pilot while individuals use personal ChatGPT and Claude accounts. — [NANDA v0.1 PDF, July 2025, §3.3](https://cloudelligent.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/02/v0.1_State_of_AI_in_Business_2025_Report.pdf)
- Failed pilots and brittle internal builds. “Sixty percent of organizations evaluated such tools, but only 20 percent reached pilot stage and just 5 percent reached production. Most fail due to brittle workflows, lack of contextual learning, and misalignment with day-to-day operations.” “Internal builds fail twice as often.” “External partnerships see twice the success rate of internal builds.” A CIO: “We’ve seen dozens of demos this year. Maybe one or two are genuinely useful. The rest are wrappers or science projects.” Top performers reported about 90 days from pilot to full implementation; enterprises took nine months or longer. The PDF says these deployment figures are “directionally accurate based on individual interviews rather than official company reporting.” — [NANDA v0.1 PDF, July 2025, §§3.2 and executive summary](https://cloudelligent.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/02/v0.1_State_of_AI_in_Business_2025_Report.pdf)
- Cost without reuse / investment that does not compound into operations. Executives allocating a hypothetical $100 put sales and marketing at “approximately 70 percent” of AI budget in one sentence and “~50% to Sales & Marketing” in the research note on the next page — the PDF contradicts itself. A Fortune 1000 pharma procurement VP: “If I buy a tool to help my team work faster, how do I quantify that impact? How do I justify it to my CEO when it won’t directly move revenue or decrease measurable costs?” — [NANDA v0.1 PDF, July 2025, §3.4](https://cloudelligent.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/02/v0.1_State_of_AI_in_Business_2025_Report.pdf)
- For complex work, buyers still prefer a person. Exhibit prompt: “Would you assign this task to AI or a junior colleague?” Complex projects (multi-week work, client management): 10% AI preferred, 90% human. Quick tasks (emails, summaries, basic analysis): 70% AI, 30% human. The authors’ gloss: “The dividing line isn’t intelligence, it’s memory, adaptability, and learning capability.” — [NANDA v0.1 PDF, July 2025, p. 13](https://cloudelligent.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/02/v0.1_State_of_AI_in_Business_2025_Report.pdf)
- Session amnesia, from the lab that ships a coding agent. “The core challenge of long-running agents is that they must work in discrete sessions, and each new session begins with no memory of what came before.” Analogy used: engineers on shifts, each arriving with no memory of the previous shift. “Compaction isn’t sufficient.” Two failure modes named: the agent one-shots the task and leaves a half-implemented, undocumented feature; a later session “look[s] around, see[s] that progress had been made, and declare[s] the job done.” — [Anthropic, “Effective harnesses for long-running agents”](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/effective-harnesses-for-long-running-agents) (byline date not in the fetched body; search index lists 2025-11-26; page discusses Opus 4.5)
- Context rot, named and measured. Chroma, July 2025, 18 LLMs: “model performance degrades as input length increases, often in surprising and non-uniform ways,” even when task difficulty is held fixed. On LongMemEval, focused prompts (~300 tokens, relevant parts only) beat full histories (~113k tokens) across the models tested. Anthropic’s context-engineering post cites that result as “context rot: as the number of tokens in the context window increases, the model’s ability to accurately recall information from that context decreases,” and says this showed up “across all models.” — [Chroma, July 2025](https://www.trychroma.com/research/context-rot); [Anthropic, “Effective context engineering for AI agents”](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/effective-context-engineering-for-ai-agents) (byline date not in the fetched body; search index lists 2025-09-29)
- Context loss between agents, not only between sessions. Cognition: “Context engineering … is effectively the #1 job of engineers building AI agents.” Principle stated: “Share context, and share full agent traces, not just individual messages.” “Actions carry implicit decisions, and conflicting decisions carry bad results.” The post says that in 2025, “running multiple agents in collaboration only results in fragile systems” because “context isn’t able to be shared thoroughly enough.” — [Cognition, “Don’t Build Multi-Agents”](https://cognition.com/blog/dont-build-multi-agents) (page says “it is now 2025” and “as of June 2025” about Claude Code; no calendar byline in the fetched body)
- Handoffs and review queues inside a company that already runs an agent. Angie Jones, VP of Engineering, AI Tools & Enablement at Block, 5 Dec 2025: “Most companies rely on handoffs. A product manager submits a ticket. An engineer builds it. A support team flags a recurring issue. A developer scripts a fix. These workflows protect quality, but they slow things down.” At Block, “60% of our workforce — around 6,000 employees — use goose weekly,” connected to Slack, Google Drive, Snowflake, Databricks, Jira, and others. A support escalation that “wait[ed] three to five days” is described as a 30-second ask to goose. Governance is described as the thing that makes that reach allowed: legal approval for tools that handle sensitive information, “curated lists of approved extensions,” and tools that “separate what users can read versus what they can modify.” — [The New Stack, Angie Jones / Block, 5 Dec 2025](https://thenewstack.io/ai-agents-are-morphing-into-the-enterprise-operating-system/)
- Work that is not written back for the next agent. Every (Dan Shipper and Kieran Klaassen), 11 Dec 2025: traditional engineering expects each feature to make the next harder; “compound engineering creates a learning loop for your agents and members of your team, so that each bug, failed test, or a-ha problem-solving insight gets documented and used by future agents.” “These learnings are also automatically distributed to the team… A new hire who’s never been in the codebase before is as well-armed to avoid common mistakes as someone who’s been on the team for a long time.” They say roughly 80% of the loop is plan and review, 20% is work and compound. This is a description of their own five in-house products, not a survey. — [Every, 11 Dec 2025](https://every.to/chain-of-thought/compound-engineering-how-every-codes-with-agents)

### Inferences
- The pains the assignment listed are not equally attested. Context loss, failure to integrate, shadow personal tools, pilots that do not reach production, and “spend that does not learn” are in the opened buyer document (NANDA) in the buyer’s own sentences. “Lack of audit” and “review bottleneck” are only adjacent: Block talks about approval lists and read-vs-modify boundaries; Every and the METR RCT talk about review as where quality is decided. Neither opened source uses “audit trail,” “non-human identity,” or “review bottleneck.”
- NANDA’s buyers describe the missing object as a system that remembers and fits a workflow. Agent-builders describe the same object as a harness, a memory file, or a compound step. Those are different dialects for one gap: the run does not leave a reusable trace.

### Gaps
- McKinsey’s agent-deployment articles could not be fetched (HTTP error on both the Nov 2025 State of AI URL and the Sept 2025 “one year of agentic AI” URL). Any McKinsey pain quotes are omitted.
- No Shopify, Stripe, or Block engineering-blog URL was opened. The Block evidence is a VP’s contributed essay on The New Stack, not block.xyz.
- No primary CISO, NIST, OWASP, or non-human-identity page was opened, so audit and agent-identity pains are not quoted here even though search results pointed at them.
- Simon Willison’s own posts were not opened. A LangChain post quotes him secondhand; that is not treated as his wording.
- Gartner was not opened.

## What do credible studies actually claim about genAI or agent ROI, pilot failure, or time-to-value?

### Takeaway
Opened studies do not say “AI has no ROI” or “95% of AI projects fail” as a single clean fact. NANDA’s preliminary 2025 PDF says 95% of organizations in its set were getting zero return and that only 5% of task-specific tools reached a production bar the authors defined as sustained productivity or P&L impact. Stanford’s 2025 Index, using a different survey, says use is high and reported financial effects are mostly small. METR’s early-2025 RCT found experienced open-source developers were slower with AI and wrong about that; its May 2026 survey of technical workers found large self-reported value gains and warned that self-reports run hot.

### Cited Findings
- NANDA, July 2025, population as on page 2 (300 public initiatives, 52 organizations interviewed, 153 senior leaders; January–June 2025; preliminary; authors’ views only). Executive summary, verbatim: “Despite $30–40 billion in enterprise investment into GenAI, this report uncovers a surprising result in that 95% of organizations are getting zero return… Just 5% of integrated AI pilots are extracting millions in value, while the vast majority remain stuck with no measurable P&L impact.” Later: “The 95% failure rate for enterprise AI solutions represents the clearest manifestation of the GenAI Divide.” Separately, the pilot exhibit: general-purpose LLMs about 80% investigated / 50% piloted / 40% successfully implemented; embedded or task-specific GenAI about 60% / 20% / 5%. “Successfully implemented” for task-specific tools is defined as users or executives remarking a “marked and sustained productivity and/or P&L impact.” Generic chatbot pilot-to-implementation is described as “~83%,” with the caveat that this “masks a deeper split” because those tools “primarily enhance individual productivity, not P&L performance.” — [NANDA v0.1 PDF](https://cloudelligent.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/02/v0.1_State_of_AI_in_Business_2025_Report.pdf)
- Same PDF, time-to-value: “Top performers reported average timelines of 90 days from pilot to full implementation. Enterprises, by comparison, took nine months or longer.” “Tools with low setup burden and fast time-to-value outperform heavy enterprise builds” is the authors’ takeaway, not a measured distribution. Back-office is where they say ROI shows up (reduced BPO and agency spend); they also say more than half of budgets, and in one sentence ~70%, go to sales and marketing. Do not cite a “67% of purchased solutions succeed” figure from this pass: that number was in secondary coverage and was not on the PDF pages read (1–16). — [NANDA v0.1 PDF](https://cloudelligent.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/02/v0.1_State_of_AI_in_Business_2025_Report.pdf)
- Stanford HAI 2025 AI Index, economy chapter highlights (data year 2024; page does not name the underlying survey firm in the text that was fetched). “In 2024, the proportion of survey respondents reporting AI use by their organizations jumped to 78% from 55% in 2023… using generative AI in at least one business function more than doubled—from 33% in 2023 to 71%.” Financial impact: “Most companies that report financial impacts from using AI within a business function estimate the benefits as being at low levels.” Examples given: 49% of respondents whose organizations use AI in service operations report cost savings, but “most of them report cost savings of less than 10%.” On revenue, 71% of respondents using AI in marketing and sales report revenue gains, “but the most common level of revenue increases is less than 5%.” The same report’s top-line takeaway still says research “confirms that AI boosts productivity and, in most cases, helps narrow skill gaps.” Both sentences are on the opened HAI pages; they are not the same claim. — [2025 Index overview](https://hai.stanford.edu/ai-index/2025-ai-index-report); [Chapter 4: Economy](https://hai.stanford.edu/ai-index/2025-ai-index-report/economy)
- Stanford HAI 2026 AI Index overview page (publication date not printed on the fetched overview; the page is the current Index and discusses 2025 outcomes). “Organizational adoption reached 88%.” Generative AI “reached 53% population adoption within three years, faster than the PC or the internet.” U.S. consumer value of generative AI tools “reached $172 billion annually by early 2026.” OSWorld agent success “from 12% to ~66%,” “still fail roughly 1 in 3 attempts.” SWE-bench Verified “rose from 60% to near 100% in a single year.” A secondary write-up’s “14% to 26% productivity” line was not on this page and is omitted. — [2026 Index overview](https://hai.stanford.edu/ai-index/2026-ai-index-report)
- METR time-horizon study, 19 March 2025, on software and reasoning tasks with human expert times. “Current models have almost 100% success rate on tasks taking humans less than 4 minutes, but succeed <10% of the time on tasks taking more than around 4 hours.” The 50% time horizon “has been doubling approximately every 7 months for the last 6 years.” The post says Claude 3.7 Sonnet’s 50% horizon was about one hour. The same page, as fetched in 2026, warns that “some of the text and figures in this post are out of date” and points at a later Time Horizon 1.1 page that was not opened here, so later doubling-time revisions are not quoted. — [METR, 19 March 2025](https://metr.org/blog/2025-03-19-measuring-ai-ability-to-complete-long-tasks/)
- METR RCT, 10 July 2025. Population: 16 experienced developers on large open-source repositories they already knew, 246 real issues averaging about two hours, early-2025 tools (primarily Cursor Pro with Claude 3.5/3.7 Sonnet). “When developers are allowed to use AI tools, they take 19% longer to complete issues.” “Developers expected AI to speed them up by 24%, and even after experiencing the slowdown, they still believed AI had sped them up by 20%.” The authors explicitly say this does not show that AI fails to speed up most developers, or developers in other settings. The page now says these results are out of date and links a February 2026 update that was not opened. — [METR, 10 July 2025](https://metr.org/blog/2025-07-10-early-2025-ai-experienced-os-dev-study/)
- METR survey, 11 May 2026, fielded February–April 2026. Population: 349 technical workers (87 software engineers, 71 researchers, 129 academics and PhD students, 48 founders and managers), convenience sample from GitHub, academic directories, METR’s network, and X; email response rate about 2%; about 70% were paid. “Participants self-reported a median 1.4–2x change in the value in their work due to AI tools. The median self-reported speed change … is 3x.” Retrospective median about 1.3x value in March 2025, 2x in March 2026, forecast 2.5x for March 2027. Median claimed willingness to sacrifice 29% of salary for one month of access, which the author calls weak evidence. The post says “there are tentative reasons to be skeptical of the magnitude,” flags selection bias, and says public survey estimates “have tended to be greater than those from field experiments.” It summarizes the early-2025 RCT as people overestimating AI’s effect on time spent “by 40 percentage points on average.” — [METR, 11 May 2026](https://metr.org/blog/2026-05-11-ai-usage-survey/)
- Block’s internal adoption number is a company claim, not a study: 60% of about 6,000 employees use goose weekly, with before/after anecdotes (days to minutes; three-to-five-day escalation to 30 seconds), dated 5 Dec 2025. No P&L figure is in the essay. — [The New Stack, 5 Dec 2025](https://thenewstack.io/ai-agents-are-morphing-into-the-enterprise-operating-system/)
- Every’s “one developer can do the work of five” is explicitly “based on our experience at Every,” 11 Dec 2025, across five products each “primarily built and run by a single person” and “used by thousands of people every day.” Not a controlled study. — [Every, 11 Dec 2025](https://every.to/chain-of-thought/compound-engineering-how-every-codes-with-agents)

### Inferences
- A buyer who already runs AI can be shown three different “ROI” sentences that do not contradict once the denominator is kept: individual tools feel productive (NANDA chatbot adoption; METR 2026 self-reports; Stanford skill-gap studies), measured function-level P&L is usually a few percent where it exists (Stanford 2025), and task-specific systems rarely clear a sustained P&L bar (NANDA). Selling “more value from AI” blurs those three.
- The METR pair is the sharpest warning against leading with self-reported time savings. In early 2025, experienced developers on familiar repos were slower and thought they were faster. By early 2026, a broader technical sample reported 1.4–2x value. Both can be true of different populations and dates. The opened 2026 survey tells the reader not to treat the larger number as ground truth.

### Gaps
- McKinsey State of AI 2025 (and the “six lessons” agent article) could not be opened. Widely repeated figures from search snippets — 62% experimenting with agents, 23% scaling an agent in at least one function, 39% reporting enterprise EBIT impact — are omitted on purpose.
- The February 2026 METR RCT update and the Time Horizon 1.1 post were not opened. Do not treat the July 2025 slowdown or the March 2025 doubling time as METR’s latest number.
- Stanford 2026 economy-chapter body was not opened beyond the overview bullets. Productivity percentages that appear only in third-party summaries of that report were omitted.
- No Gartner primary page was opened.

## What alternate frames appear in practitioner and vendor writing aimed at people who already deploy agents?

### Takeaway
Among pages opened, the frames that already have a name are “context engineering,” “context rot,” “agent harness,” “compound engineering,” “learning gap,” and “shadow AI economy.” “Institutional memory,” “agents as colleagues who need an office,” and “private-by-default” were not used by any source opened in this pass. Close cousins were: a junior-colleague comparison, human “inboxes” as a metaphor, and buyer demands for data boundaries.

### Cited Findings
- “Context engineering.” Anthropic: “a new term has come to prominence: context engineering.” Definition: “the set of strategies for curating and maintaining the optimal set of tokens (information) during LLM inference, including all the other information that may land there outside of the prompts.” They call it “the natural progression of prompt engineering,” and the “art and science of curating what will go into the limited context window.” Techniques they name for long-horizon work: compaction, structured note-taking (“or agentic memory”), and sub-agent architectures. They also say agents should keep “lightweight identifiers (file paths, stored queries, web links, etc.)” and compare that to “file systems, inboxes, and bookmarks.” The inbox line is about human memory, not about giving an agent an office. — [Anthropic context-engineering post](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/effective-context-engineering-for-ai-agents)
- Same phrase, LangChain, 2 July 2025: “Context engineering is the art and science of filling the context window with just the right information at each step of an agent’s trajectory.” They quote Karpathy: “delicate art and science of filling the context window with just the right information for the next step.” Their four buckets: “write, select, compress, and isolate.” They distinguish scratchpads (inside one session) from memories “across many sessions,” and say unexpected memory retrieval can make users feel the context window “no longer belongs to them” (their paraphrase of a Simon Willison talk; his post was not opened). — [LangChain, 2 July 2025](https://www.langchain.com/blog/context-engineering-for-agents)
- Same phrase, Cognition: “Context engineering is the next level of this [prompt engineering]. It is about doing this automatically in a dynamic system. It takes more nuance and is effectively the #1 job of engineers building AI agents.” — [Cognition](https://cognition.com/blog/dont-build-multi-agents)
- “Context rot.” Chroma’s July 2025 report title is “Context Rot: How Increasing Input Tokens Impacts LLM Performance.” Anthropic uses the term and links Chroma. LangChain, citing Drew Breunig (22 June 2025, linked but not opened), lists related failure names: “context poisoning,” “context distraction,” “context confusion,” and “context clash.” — [Chroma](https://www.trychroma.com/research/context-rot); [LangChain, 2 July 2025](https://www.langchain.com/blog/context-engineering-for-agents)
- “Harness” / “agent harness.” Anthropic, long-running agents post: “The Claude Agent SDK is a powerful, general-purpose agent harness.” The fix they describe is not a better prompt but an initializer agent, a `claude-progress.txt` log, a JSON feature list, git commits, and an `init.sh`, so the next session can “get up to speed.” A footnote: they “refer to these as separate agents in this context only because they have different initial user prompts. The system prompt, set of tools, and overall agent harness was otherwise identical.” — [Anthropic harness post](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/effective-harnesses-for-long-running-agents)
- “Compound engineering” / “compound.” Every, 11 Dec 2025, defines it against ordinary engineering: “in compound engineering, you expect each feature to make the next feature easier to build.” Loop names they use: Plan, Work, Assess/Review, Compound. “This is the money step.” They also use “harness” once, generically: “They just need a good system to harness its power.” A 22 April 2026 Every podcast transcript (opened via search excerpt, then the Dec 2025 essay was the page fetched in full) has Kieran Klaassen calling compound engineering “a philosophy” whose last step is to “compound that knowledge back into the system.” — [Every, 11 Dec 2025](https://every.to/chain-of-thought/compound-engineering-how-every-codes-with-agents)
- “Learning gap,” “retain context,” “shadow AI economy.” These are NANDA’s phrases, aimed at buyers, not at agent engineers. See the pain section for the quotes. They also place “Agentic workflows, vertical SaaS” in a cell labeled “High Memory / Learning” and “High Customization,” opposite “Copilot, GPT wrappers” (low memory, low customization) and “Internal builds (fragile).” — [NANDA v0.1 PDF, July 2025](https://cloudelligent.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/02/v0.1_State_of_AI_in_Business_2025_Report.pdf)
- Governance so the agent is allowed to act, without the word “governance” as a slogan. Block, 5 Dec 2025: legal approval, approved-extension lists, and read-versus-modify boundaries “aren’t bureaucratic barriers; they’re design choices that let teams move fast while keeping important information secure.” NANDA buyers ask for “clear data boundaries” in the same breath as workflow fit. — [The New Stack, 5 Dec 2025](https://thenewstack.io/ai-agents-are-morphing-into-the-enterprise-operating-system/); [NANDA PDF, p. 15](https://cloudelligent.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/02/v0.1_State_of_AI_in_Business_2025_Report.pdf)
- “Enterprise operating system” and “central conductor.” Jones’s headline and deck: agents “are becoming internal ‘operating systems’ that connect and orchestrate data flows between software tools.” Goose “serves as a central conductor.” — [The New Stack, 5 Dec 2025](https://thenewstack.io/ai-agents-are-morphing-into-the-enterprise-operating-system/)
- Colleague frame, only as a survey prompt. NANDA asks whether the user would “assign this task to AI or a junior colleague.” It does not say agents need an office, an inbox, or an identity. — [NANDA v0.1 PDF, p. 13](https://cloudelligent.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/02/v0.1_State_of_AI_in_Business_2025_Report.pdf)

### Inferences
- “Get more value from AI” is the frame these sources are already leaving. NANDA’s replacement is the learning gap. Anthropic’s and LangChain’s replacement, for people building agents, is context engineering. Every’s replacement, for a team that already lets agents write the code, is compound engineering. Block’s replacement, for a company that already has an agent, is reach into internal tools plus a permission boundary.
- Private-by-default versus cloud copilot was not a frame in the opened set. The nearest buyer sentence is NANDA’s “I can’t risk client data mixing with someone else’s model,” paired with heavy use of personal ChatGPT and Copilot. That is a data-boundary complaint sitting on top of shadow SaaS, not a “private sidecar” pitch.

### Gaps
- “Institutional memory,” “agents as colleagues that need an office,” and “private-by-default” did not appear on any page opened. Search hits used “institutional knowledge” (a patterns blog) and non-human identity (vendor and NIST-adjacent pages). Those pages were not opened, so the phrases are not recorded as in-market frames.
- Many 2026 posts with “agent harness” in the title showed up in search (LangChain Terminal-Bench writeups, security blogs). They were not opened. Crowding of “harness” outside Anthropic is therefore not established from full pages.
- Latent Space and Pragmatic Engineer were not opened. Simon Willison was not opened.

## Which of those frames is crowded versus rare, based on the sources opened?

### Takeaway
On the pages actually opened, “context engineering” is the crowded builder frame (Anthropic, LangChain, Cognition, and Chroma’s “context rot,” all in a mid-2025 cluster). “Learning gap / doesn’t learn / retain context” is the crowded buyer frame, but it is crowded inside one report, not across many firms. “Compound engineering” and “agent harness” are real but narrow. “Institutional memory,” “office for agents,” and “private-by-default” were absent.

### Cited Findings
- Crowded among opened builder sources: “context engineering” is defined, not just mentioned, by Anthropic, by LangChain on 2 July 2025 (explicitly citing Karpathy and Cognition), and by Cognition as “the #1 job.” Chroma’s “context rot” (July 2025) is the empirical hook Anthropic uses. LangChain also says the idea is already “a craft that agents builders should aim to master,” which is a crowding claim from a vendor. — [Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/effective-context-engineering-for-ai-agents); [LangChain](https://www.langchain.com/blog/context-engineering-for-agents); [Cognition](https://cognition.com/blog/dont-build-multi-agents); [Chroma](https://www.trychroma.com/research/context-rot)
- Present but not crowded in this set: “agent harness” is Anthropic’s term for the shell around the model in the long-running-agents post. Every uses “harness” only as a verb. Block, NANDA, Stanford, and METR do not use it. — [Anthropic harness post](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/effective-harnesses-for-long-running-agents); [Every](https://every.to/chain-of-thought/compound-engineering-how-every-codes-with-agents)
- Rare in this set, and aimed at practitioners who already code with agents: “compound engineering” appears as a named method only in Every’s 11 Dec 2025 essay (and their own podcast). No other opened source uses the phrase. — [Every, 11 Dec 2025](https://every.to/chain-of-thought/compound-engineering-how-every-codes-with-agents)
- Buyer-side and not used by the engineering posts: “GenAI Divide,” “learning gap,” and “shadow AI economy” are NANDA’s. The engineering posts talk about memory, compaction, and traces without those labels. — [NANDA PDF](https://cloudelligent.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/02/v0.1_State_of_AI_in_Business_2025_Report.pdf)
- Not found on opened pages: “institutional memory,” “private-by-default,” “non-human identity,” and any sentence that agents need an office, an inbox of their own, or a colleague identity. Anthropic’s “inboxes” are an analogy for how humans store pointers. NANDA’s “junior colleague” is a forced-choice survey item.

### Inferences
- A message built only on “context engineering” or “harness” will sound like agent-infrastructure shop talk to the NANDA buyer, who says “it doesn’t learn,” “it doesn’t plug into Salesforce,” and “I can’t risk client data mixing.” A message built only on “get more value” collides with Stanford’s “benefits at low levels” and with NANDA’s claim that individual ChatGPT use is already the part that works.
- “Compound” is the least crowded name for the job these sources actually describe (the next run starts from what the last run learned), and it is also the least validated outside one company’s coding workflow.

### Gaps
- Crowding is only as wide as the opened set. Search results suggested “harness” became common in engineering blogs through 2026 and that non-human identity became common in security writing. Those pages were not opened, so they are not counted as evidence of crowding.
- No corpus count (share of voice, ad copy, analyst keyword frequency) was done.

## What would a buyer who already runs agents hear as table stakes versus a new job-to-be-done?

### Takeaway
From the opened sources, table stakes for a company that already runs AI are a chat tool people will actually use, a way for an agent to call internal systems, and a permission boundary so that calling is allowed. The job those same sources say is still open is making the work accumulate: less re-explaining, fewer repeated mistakes, a trace the next person or agent can pick up, and a path from individual use to something that shows up in the P&L.

### Cited Findings
- Already true in the NANDA sample, so a weak promise: generic chat is deployed and liked. “Over 80 percent of organizations have explored or piloted [ChatGPT and Copilot], and nearly 40 percent report deployment.” Users praise them for “flexibility, familiarity, and immediate utility.” The report’s point is that this is not the P&L outcome. — [NANDA v0.1 PDF, executive summary and §3.2](https://cloudelligent.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/02/v0.1_State_of_AI_in_Business_2025_Report.pdf)
- Already the production pattern at Block, so “connect the agent to Slack and the warehouse” is in market: goose is on MCP extensions across Slack, Drive, Snowflake, Databricks, and Jira, used weekly by 60% of a ~6,000-person company, with an approval list in front of sensitive tools. Dated 5 Dec 2025. — [The New Stack](https://thenewstack.io/ai-agents-are-morphing-into-the-enterprise-operating-system/)
- Still the unmet ask, in procurement language: learn from feedback (66% of executives in the NANDA coding), retain context (63%), “deep understanding of our workflow,” “minimal disruption to current tools,” “clear data boundaries,” and flexibility “when things change” (“Our process evolves every quarter. If the AI can’t adapt, we’re back to spreadsheets.”). — [NANDA v0.1 PDF, pp. 14–16](https://cloudelligent.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/02/v0.1_State_of_AI_in_Business_2025_Report.pdf)
- Still an engineering problem even after context windows and compaction exist. Anthropic: each new session “begins with no memory of what came before,” and compaction does not stop one-shotting or false “done.” Their concrete artifacts are a progress log, a feature list, and git, not a larger prompt. — [Anthropic harness post](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/effective-harnesses-for-long-running-agents)
- Still how a team that already delegates coding to agents says the work gets easier: write the review lesson back into the system so the next agent and the next hire inherit it. Every, 11 Dec 2025. — [Every](https://every.to/chain-of-thought/compound-engineering-how-every-codes-with-agents)
- The financial bar buyers are failing is not “adoption.” Stanford 2025: where companies report cost savings, “most” of those reports are under 10%; the most common revenue increase reported is under 5%. NANDA: individual productivity is common, sustained P&L impact from task-specific tools is the 5% tail in their directional exhibit. — [Stanford HAI economy chapter](https://hai.stanford.edu/ai-index/2025-ai-index-report/economy); [NANDA PDF](https://cloudelligent.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/02/v0.1_State_of_AI_in_Business_2025_Report.pdf)

### Inferences
- Table stakes, inferred only from the pattern above: a copilot people already prefer to a rigid internal tool; some MCP- or API-style reach into systems of record; an allow-list so security will permit that reach; and in-session context tricks (compaction, scratchpads), which agent builders already treat as the job.
- A new job, inferred from what those sources still call unsolved: memory that is shared across people and across agent sessions, not just a longer transcript; integration that matches a real approval path rather than a demo; a record of what the agent did that is good enough for the next session (Anthropic’s progress file) and, at Block, good enough to separate read from modify; and proof that goes past “it feels faster,” because METR showed self-reported speed can be wrong and Stanford showed reported dollars are usually small.
- “Auditability” as a buyer headline is an inference, not a quote. The opened buyer quotes are about data not mixing and tools not fitting the workflow. The opened operator quote (Block) is about approval before connect, and read versus write. A sidecar message that leads with audit will be ahead of the sentences these buyers used. A message that leads with “it keeps the context and plugs into the systems you already have, without sending client data into someone else’s model” is inside those sentences.
- Do not lead with “95% of AI projects fail.” The opened PDF’s own wording is “95% of organizations are getting zero return” and, separately, a 5% production rate for task-specific tools under a P&L/productivity definition, on a preliminary, non-random sample. Buyers who already run ChatGPT will hear the viral line as an insult to tools they know work for drafts.

### Gaps
- No opened source ranks these jobs in a conjoint, win/loss, or message test. The table-stakes split is an inference from what is described as already deployed versus what authors still call the barrier.
- Identity, shared inbox, and admin audit as distinct purchase criteria were not in the opened buyer interviews. They may still be how security buyers talk; that literature was not opened.
- McKinsey’s 2025–2026 agent lessons, which would have been the closest large-sample check on “what high performers do differently,” could not be fetched.
