## 1. How common are client AI-use terms in the contracts of 5–150-engineer suppliers, beyond the 13 large buyers with public terms?

### Takeaway
- **Common at the top, a minority at the bottom, and rising.** Explicit AI-use terms reach small suppliers through three main channels:
  1. **Large buyers' standard supplier terms**, which apply to every supplier whatever its size. This round adds four buyers beyond 2.01's 13: Cisco (October 2026), Carrier (May 2025), Zuellig Pharma (December 2025) and Raiffeisen Informatik (June 2026).
  2. **Government standard terms.** Since February 2025, California's General Terms require a contractor to notify the State before using GenAI "to write source code" for a State system. New York's ITS Appendix C-AI (April 2025) requires 90 days' notice and written approval for AI.
  3. **NDAs and MSAs in regulated sectors**: government, defense, fintech and healthcare.
- **Across all small-supplier contracts, explicit terms are a minority.** Each figure below is a lower bound or a measure from one platform:
  - Spellbook (vendor data): about **8% of independent-contractor agreements** carry AI-use provisions.
  - Bloomberg Law: AI terms "rarely" appear in 670+ publicly filed technology services and development agreements.
  - Adweek/NewtonX: **39% of ad-agency contracts don't address AI at all**, and 27% address it only broadly.
  - CHI '26 study: **14% of freelance-platform clients discourage AI entirely**, while 81% encourage it.
  - AMI: only **45% of small and mid-sized clients have fully discussed their AI rules** with their agencies.
- **Among 22 listed IT-services and agency firms, only Endava** says in its annual report that "some clients prohibit autonomous code generation or require explicit disclosure of agentic methods". That sentence is new in its FY2025 20-F. The other firms frame client AI terms as demands for indemnities, warranties and audit rights, or as AI-driven price cuts.
- **Clients push in two opposite directions.** Some clients restrict suppliers' AI. Many others expect suppliers to use it and to pass on the savings.

### Cited Findings

#### A. Annual reports of IT-services and agency firms (EDGAR, primary)
- **Endava is the only filer of the 22 whose report says clients restrict AI coding.** FY2025 20-F (period 30 Jun 2025; filed 4 Sep 2025):
  - "Finally, some clients prohibit autonomous code generation or require explicit disclosure of agentic methods; inadvertent use of such technology could therefore trigger breach of contract claims or indemnity demands."
  - "Our personnel may also forget to obtain client consent to incorporate code generated using AI tools into client deliverables or may operate in violation of client policy by taking shortcuts and using AI tools without authorization."
  - "...including contractual claims if the agreement prohibits the use of AI-generated content in the deliverables".
  - On agents: they "may take unanticipated actions, generate unauthorized code paths or initiate transactions that breach client policies".
  - Also: "The dynamic planning modules that underpin agentic AI may not yield a stable artefact that auditors can reproduce, making it harder to evidence compliance with transparency obligations".
  - Source: [Endava 20-F FY2025](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1656081/000165608125000039/dava-20250630.htm) [opened]

  - **Item bases (my arithmetic):** 14.06%, 4.69%, 81.25% and 78.13% are exact multiples of 1/64, so those items had about 64 respondents, not 145. The text I read does not state the bases.

#### E. Public procurement templates that pass AI-use terms down to suppliers
- **California, General Terms and Conditions (GTC 02/2025).** These are binding standard terms in all state contracts. The DGS slide deck is dated 11 Apr 2025.
  - **The duty:** "Bidder / Offeror must notify the State in writing if it: (1) intends to provide GenAI as a deliverable to the State; or (2), intends to utilize GenAI, including GenAI from third parties, to complete all or a portion of any deliverable that materially impacts: (i) functionality of a State system, (ii) risk to the State, or (iii) Contract performance."
  - **Penalty:** "Failure to report GenAI to the State may result in disqualification."
  - **What the State may do next:** "the State reserves the right to incorporate GenAI Special Provisions into the final contract or reject bids/offers that present an unacceptable level of risk".
  - **Other changes:** the "Duty of notification is shifted to the bidder / contractor"; on notice, "a risk assessment must be done by the agency's CIO"; "GenAI contract language is now incorporated into the General Terms and Conditions (GTC 02/2025)"; "STD 1000 has been eliminated".
  - Source: [DGS 2025 Summary of GenAI Changes](https://www.dgs.ca.gov/-/media/Divisions/OLS/Resources/2025-Summary-of-GenAI-Changes.pdf) [opened]
- **California SAM §4986.2, "Material Impact/Materially Impacts"** ("New: 02/2025"):
  - Under "Functionality of a System", the example is "**Using GenAI to write source code; using GenAI to perform acceptance testing.**"
  - Under "Contract Performance", the example is "Using GenAI to draft reports or other deliverables without human review."
  - Source: [SAM 4986.2](https://www.dgs.ca.gov/Resources/SAM/TOC/4900/4986-2) [opened via fetch tool]
- **California State Model GenAI Special Provisions** (CDT, "January 2025"; PDF 19 Feb 2025), added when the risk assessment finds moderate or high risk:
  - §3: "Contractor shall allow the State reasonable access to the GenAI security logs, latency statistics, and other related GenAI security data that affect this Contract and Generated Data, at no cost to the State."
  - §6(c): "Contractor shall ensure that all Generated Data that materially impacts Deliverables created pursuant to the Contract contains a digital watermark or other digital identification that clearly identifies that the Generated Data was created by GenAI."
  - §6(b): the contractor must "track and disclose the quality of the Generated Data", and share the metrics and output "upon request".
  - Source: [CDT GenAI Special Provisions](https://www.cdt.ca.gov/wp-content/uploads/2025/02/GenAI-Special-Provisions-January-2025.pdf) [opened]
- **California Executive Order N-5-26** (30 Mar 2026) reportedly requires state contractors to attest to and explain their AI policies. I did not verify it — [search summary]
- **New York, ITS "Appendix C-AI: Standard Terms for AI Purchases"** (April 2025; PDF 8 Apr 2025):
  - **90 days' notice:** "Contractor or Vendor agrees to provide ITS with written notice at least 90 days prior to the implementation or use of any artificial intelligence (AI) technology in Contractor or Vendor's software, hardware, or in any dealings or communication with the State, the State's employees, contractors or consultants…"
  - **Content of the notice:** "a detailed description of the AI technology to be used, its intended purpose, its connections with the State, when the AI will be deployed within a tool, whether that AI is open-source, and how the AI will be implemented."
  - **Approval:** "For AI tools not integral to the system, AI tools should be disabled by default. Any AI usage must be approved, in writing, by ITS."
  - **Audit reports:** "ITS may require vendor to provide reports that are necessary for auditing purposes by third parties."
  - **Retention:** "The Vendor shall not store, retain, or repurpose the State's data for any purpose other than providing the contracted services."
  - Source: [NY ITS Appendix C-AI](https://its.ny.gov/system/files/documents/2025/04/appendix-c-ai-april-2025_1.pdf) [opened]
- **New York policy NYS-P24-001.** It reportedly covers "consultants, vendors, and contractors" who use or access state IT resources. NYSERDA's vendor page requires vendors to use "approved AI Systems" — [ITS policy page](https://its.ny.gov/acceptable-use-artificial-intelligence-technologies) [search summary]; the [policy PDF](https://its.ny.gov/system/files/documents/2026/01/nys-p24-001-acceptable-use-of-ai.pdf) [did not load: 403]

### Inferences
- **Scaling the 2.01 count down to small suppliers.** Terms written into a large buyer's standard purchase terms or supplier policy apply to every supplier of that buyer, including a 10-person agency. That is true of 2.01's 13, of Cisco, Carrier, Zuellig and Raiffeisen Informatik, and of California's GTC. "Large buyers' terms" are therefore not confined to large vendors. What decides whether a small supplier is bound is **who its clients are, not how big the supplier is**.
- **In the general contract population, explicit AI-use terms are a minority but not rare.** The best available indicators all land between 8% and about a third:
  - Spellbook: ~8% of contractor agreements (vendor data).
  - Adweek: about 34% of ad-agency contracts address AI more than broadly. This is my arithmetic (100 − 39 − 27); the article does not state it.
  - CHI '26: 14% of platform clients discourage AI entirely.
  - Bloomberg Law: "rarely" in filed tech-services agreements.

  None of these is a direct measure of 5–150-engineer software agencies.
- **Even a low per-contract rate makes conflicting terms the norm for a multi-client agency.** This is illustrative arithmetic, assuming clients are independent:
  - if 8% of client contracts restrict AI, an agency with 15 clients has a 1 − 0.92^15 ≈ **71%** chance of at least one restrictive client;
  - at 14%, the chance is about **90%**.

  DC_9000's three-client mix (no AI; go fast; case-by-case) is what this predicts.
- **The trend is upward but recent.**
  - Endava's language escalated from "forget to obtain client consent" (2024) to "prohibit autonomous code generation" (2025).
  - 40% of TPRM programs were adding AI usage language to vendor contracts (Venminder, 2025).
  - TermScout reports a +240% rise in disclosure obligations (vendor data).
  - California moved the duty to notify onto contractors in February 2025.
- **Public filings lag.** Bloomberg Law's "rarely" and the near-absence in 10-Ks fit terms that sit in SOWs, NDAs, supplier policies and purchase terms, which are rarely filed.
- **Clients pull both ways.** Some say "no AI" or "approve first". Many expect AI use and lower prices (CHI's 81% encourage; Everest; EPAM; Infosys). For an agency, the binding requirement is often that it can **show which AI it used, for which client, under which terms**, whichever way the client leans.

## 2. How are the terms run in practice: what do clients approve, how finely and how fast, and what evidence do they accept?

### Takeaway
- **What clients approve: tools, providers, use cases and data inputs, not runs.**
  - The common unit is the **engagement**: an SOW, Work Order or contract notice.
  - Stricter clients approve **per use case** (Zuellig, and Gallagher and C&W in 2.01).
  - Approval **per instance** (AT&T in 2.01) is rare. Supplier-side lawyers call it "neither realistic nor operationally practical" (Davis+Gilbert).
- **How fast:** set by notice clocks, not approval SLAs.

  | Who | Notice clock |
  |---|---|
  | Zuellig | 2 weeks before material changes to AI systems |
  | Raiffeisen Informatik; AT&T's AI subcontractors (2.01) | 30 days |
  | BuiltByGo (a supplier's own policy) | 30 days' notice plus a 14-day objection window |
  | New York ITS | 90 days |

  ProcessUnity says third-party assessments take about four months (vendor claim). I found no public data on how long approval of a single request takes.
- **What evidence clients accept: mostly documents and attestations, rarely run-level logs.** They ask for:
  - written notice or descriptions of the AI used;
  - lists of tools and providers, treated like sub-processors;
  - contracts with AI providers barring training and storage;
  - a governance program and a human-review policy;
  - "documentation on request".
- **Logs are demanded only in narrow cases:**
  - Zuellig, when client data is used for AI training;
  - California, the GenAI security logs under the Special Provisions;
  - New York, reports for third-party audit.
- **Labels appear in public templates:** California's watermark or digital identification, and Carrier's provenance tracking.
- **The most visible client challenge to a small supplier is "prove the AI tool keeps nothing".** A supplier cannot answer that from its own records. Today it is answered with vendor documents (privacy pages, SOC 2), and one client found them insufficient.

### Inferences
- **The working model is "authorize at the engagement, approve tools as sub-processors, keep documents, show on request".**
  - Five ingredients recur across buyers and small suppliers: an engagement-level authority (SOW or notice), a tool and provider list, provider terms against training and storage, human review, and documentation on request.
  - Run-level records appear only where data is used for training (Zuellig) or as a supplier's own register (Geeks).
  - So **records exist as a backstop, not as the routine proof**.
- **The demand for evidence is weakest where pitch4's ledger is strongest.** Few clients ask for per-run receipts. Many ask questions a run ledger can answer cheaply but that suppliers today answer from memory:
  - which tools and providers touched our code;
  - under what terms;
  - since when, with whose approval;
  - and whether the "no AI" repositories stayed clean.

  The packet is valuable when it *generates the documents clients already ask for* and keeps the runs as backing.
- **The hardest client question today, "prove the tool kept nothing", is a provider attestation.** The supplier cannot produce it for hosted models. pitch4's model-server sidecar can produce it for self-hosted models, by signing which weights it served and that no content was logged. That answer exists only on the private-model path.
- **Notice clocks of 2 to 90 days and assessments of about four months mean approvals are slow and batch-like.** A product that makes the *first* approval complete (a full inventory and notice text) shortens the cycle. Per-run approval workflows would add load nobody asked for.
- **Silence under prohibition (CHI '26) argues for enforcement over attestation.** A client cannot rely on supplier self-reporting where the incentive is to stay silent. Enforcement at the network or harness level, plus an exception log, is more credible than an attestation of non-use.

### Gaps
- No public data on approval turnaround for an individual supplier request at any buyer: not Gallagher's inbox, not New York's written approvals, not California CIO risk assessments.
- No first-hand post by buyer-side procurement or TPRM staff describing how they approve supplier AI use was reachable. LinkedIn was not searchable as opened pages, and the vendor blogs found were marketing.
- Unknown whether any client has accepted a signed log or ledger export in place of documents.
- Unknown how California agencies have applied "using GenAI to write source code", for example how many notifications they received and whether Special Provisions were added.

## 3. What does this mean for pitch4's differentiation and product offering?

### Takeaway
- **The answer sits between the brief's two poles.**
  - Terms *do* reach 5–150-engineer suppliers, through large buyers' standard terms, government standard terms and regulated-sector NDAs. A multi-client agency is likely to hold at least one restrictive client.
  - But clients mostly ask for **enforcement of a per-client rule and a short packet of documents**, not run records.
  - Blanket and engagement-level clauses are common: Upwork's default "unless the client restricts it", BuiltByGo's SOW opt-out, Bicara's blanket ban, and Citi's and AT&T's "authorized in the SOW".
- **The client packet as a "records on request" export is a niche export.**
- **As "a per-client AI policy enforced across every harness, plus an auto-generated disclosure packet", it is a credible Team feature for agencies and outsourcers.** It also has one sharp differentiator: a private-models-only mode that proves no content was kept.

### Cited Findings
- **Agencies ask for enforcement per repository or per client:**
  - DC_9000/PAQT's AI-POLICY file request (Cursor 136492 and claude-code #9070);
  - Agile Collective's per-client "AI Policy" document;
  - BuiltByGo's SOW-level "no AI";
  - JetBrains' `.noai` (one harness only);
  - lifeinchords and khaismile1997 working under client NDAs.

### Inferences
- **Direction 1: lead the agency offer with per-client AI policy, enforced.**
  - **The policy.** An engagement file in the repository, or in the team ledger, names the mode: "no AI", "approved tools and providers only", or "private models only". It also names the approved harnesses, models and providers, with each one's authority (an SOW clause, an email approval ID, a notice date).
  - **Enforcement.** The sidecar enforces the mode at the model-access and reach layer for every harness in that repository. It travels to freelancers and subcontractors with the repository, which matches Davis+Gilbert's flow-down advice and Zuellig §3.6.
  - **Why it is differentiated.** The vendors' controls are single-harness: JetBrains `.noai` covers only JetBrains AI Assistant. Copilot content exclusion and Cursor's ignore files are not client-contract policies.
  - **Confidence:** medium. Agencies ask for it, but only a handful of voices are on record.
- **Direction 2: generate the client packet from that policy, matched to what buyers ask.**
  - **Contents:**
    - a per-engagement inventory of tools, models and providers, as a sub-processor list with first-use dates and notice and objection dates;
    - the providers' terms on training and retention, with Cisco §2.3 as the checklist;
    - approvals with their authority;
    - evidence of human review (pull-request approvals);
    - "no-AI repos stayed clean" exception reports;
    - run-level records available on request as backing.
  - **Ready-made notices:** California GTC/SAM 4986.2, New York Appendix C-AI, Raiffeisen-style 30-day notices, and UK PPN 02/24 Question 3.
  - **Confidence:** medium. The document asks are well evidenced; that clients will prefer a generated packet is untested.
- **Direction 3: make "private models only, no content kept" a named engagement mode.**
  - The model-server sidecar signs a serving receipt naming the weights and attesting that no prompt or output content was logged.
  - It answers the most visible small-supplier challenge ("prove the tool kept nothing", Cursor 5418) and the government and defense NDA bans (HN 44565144, and Northrop and L3Harris in 2.01).
  - Hosted-tool vendors cannot match this for their own models.
  - **Confidence:** medium-low. The fit is strong, the voices are few, and open-weight models are a minority of spend.
- **Direction 4: price the packet inside Team and aim it at multi-client agencies.**
  - Restrictive clients are a minority per contract but likely per agency (the illustrative 71–90% above). The value grows with the number of clients, and agencies already pay for tooling by card (2.08).
  - Selling it as a separate Assured audit product would compete with "a blanket SOW clause plus a tool list", which costs suppliers nothing today.
  - **Confidence:** low-medium. Willingness to pay is untested.
- **Direction 5: reuse the same record for clients who mandate AI.**
  - Attribution per client and per task covers AI-assisted work, model cost and merged AI-written code. That lets an agency show AI use and pass costs or savings through, which 2.08's outsourcer asked for.
  - This may be the larger demand, because more clients encourage AI than ban it (CHI: 81% vs 14%).
  - **Confidence:** low-medium.
- **Claim discipline for pitch4.**
  - Do not pitch an "attestation of non-use" as proof. It covers only what passed through the sidecar.
  - Pitch **enforcement plus an exception log** instead. CHI '26 shows that self-reporting fails under prohibitions, and clients expect disclosure when a rule is broken.

### Gaps
- No client has been asked whether a generated packet or signed export would replace its questionnaire, email approval or notice process.
- Untested whether enforcement at the sidecar satisfies "no AI" or "no third-party AI access" clauses, given bypasses such as browser chats and personal devices.
- No willingness-to-pay data for per-client policy enforcement at agency price points.
