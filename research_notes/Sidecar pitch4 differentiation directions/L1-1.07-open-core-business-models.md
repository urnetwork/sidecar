# How open-source and end-to-end-encrypted products package, price, and license an open core plus a hosted service (for pitch4's "open core, hosted blind")

Scope: this round adds new evidence. Earlier rounds already recorded the Tailscale ($8/$18), Copilot ($19/$39), Claude Team and Agent 365 ($15) seat prices, Privacy Guides' E2EE criteria, Element's AuditBot, and Keybase's failed business model. They are not repeated here except where new feature-gating detail matters. All pages were fetched on 1 November 2026 unless another date is given. "Vendor claim" marks a statement a company makes about itself. "Search excerpt" means the figure came from a search-result summary of a page I did not open.

Method for user evidence: I downloaded full comment trees from the Hacker News Algolia API (`hn.algolia.com/api/v1/items/<id>`) on 1 Nov 2026. "Distinct authors" means unique usernames among all comments in a thread. Stance counts come from my reading of top-level comments, or of keyword-matched comments where stated, and count each person once. HN commenters are self-selected and lean toward self-hosters, so the counts show which arguments people make, not how common each view is. Reddit's search endpoint returned an HTML page instead of JSON to a script, so this round has no Reddit counts.

## 1. What is free and what is paid, how self-hosted differs from cloud, 2026 seat prices, enterprise add-ons, and disclosed revenue or self-hosted share

### Takeaway
The comparables follow three models:
- **Paid license keys for self-hosting:** Bitwarden, GitLab, Mattermost, Langfuse, Infisical, NetBird.
- **Self-hosting free but unsupported, with every paid feature in the cloud:** PostHog, Sentry, Supabase.
- **Charging only for infrastructure the vendor runs:** Zulip's push-notification relay, Tailscale's closed coordination server, and the consumer E2EE storage services.

Network and security tools put SSO, SCIM and audit logs in their first or second paid tier, at $4–18 per user per month:
- Bitwarden: SCIM in Teams at $4.
- NetBird: SSO, SCIM and audit logging in Team at €6.
- Tailscale: SCIM in Standard at $8.

Pitch4's "Assured ~$39" sits above every published per-user price for the network, VPN, password-manager and E2EE-communication tools in this set. The highest of those are Proton Workspace Premium ($19.99) and Tailscale Premium ($18). Its nearest anchors are AI-governance seats (Zed Business $30) and per-identity secrets pricing (Infisical Advanced $40 per human or machine identity). Primary revenue disclosures exist only for GitLab. The few vendor figures show large self-hosted bases monetized mainly through cloud and enterprise features (Langfuse, PostHog).

### Inferences
- **Seat price.**
  - Network and security comparables price their first business tier at $4–8 per user per month (Bitwarden $4, NetBird €6, Tailscale $8) and their second at $6–18 (Bitwarden $6, NetBird €12, Tailscale $18). Pitch4's Team band of $8–25 overlaps the top of that range, and its $8 floor matches Tailscale.
  - Assured at about $39 has no per-user comparable among the network, VPN, password-manager or E2EE tools. Its nearest anchors are AI-governance seats (Zed Business $30) and machine-inclusive identity pricing (Infisical Advanced $40). An Assured price of $20–30 would sit beside Proton Workspace Premium ($19.99), Tailscale Premium ($18) and Zed Business ($30). This is an inference from list prices, not willingness-to-pay evidence.
- **SCIM belongs in Team, not Assured.**
  - Three of the closest comparables put SCIM in their first paid tier (Bitwarden Teams, NetBird Team, Tailscale Standard), and NetBird's €6 Team also includes enterprise-IdP SSO and audit logging. Pitch4 currently places SCIM in Assured, which would look like an "SSO/SCIM tax" next to them.
  - What enterprise tiers actually hold back is retention controls, legal hold, eDiscovery and compliance export (Mattermost Enterprise), SIEM streaming (Infisical Enterprise, NetBird Business), FIPS and air-gapped builds (Langfuse Government), and SLAs. That set matches pitch4's other Assured items: legal hold, compliance mappings and the visible audit member.
- **Don't meter agents per seat, but expect low per-non-human-resource prices.**
  - Tailscale prices tagged resources at $1 a month each beyond 50, and NetBird charges €0.50 a month per extra machine. Infisical charges $20–40 per machine identity and $2 per 1,000 brokered agent requests. Pitch4's rule "Agents are never metered" is therefore a positioning choice against Infisical, not the market norm.
  - A defensible version: never charge per agent seat, and meter only hosted relay and edge traffic, storage and mail, at small unit prices.
- **"Hosted blind" fits the Zulip and Tailscale pattern.** Both give away the software and charge for infrastructure only the vendor runs: push relays, and coordination with network effects. Pitch4's paid items (relays, names, certificates, mail delivery, and an edge "we run and cannot read") fit this pattern.
- **The revenue risk is the PostHog and Sentry pattern.** When every software feature is free to self-host, revenue depends on hosted convenience. Agent infrastructure users skew toward self-hosting (Headscale has more stars than Tailscale's client repo; Vaultwarden has more than Bitwarden's server).
- **GitLab shows the other side.** Its revenue is mostly self-managed (about 69% by the secondary Q3 figure), so enterprises will pay license fees for software they run themselves. Pitch4 should therefore keep a paid self-hosted enterprise license ("Assured, on your servers too"), not just hosted seats.
- **Infisical Agent Vault overlaps pitch4's "way out".** It brokers credentials for Claude Code, Codex and OpenCode with encrypted logs in the customer's own S3 bucket, and it shipped as a preview in 2026. Pitch4's competitor table does not name it. The differentiation that survives: the sidecar records sessions, edits, approvals and reach across harnesses under team keys, while Agent Vault brokers HTTP credentials. This is my reading of the two scopes, not a tested comparison.

## 2. Which licenses kept trust while protecting hosted revenue, and what backlashes followed relicensing (HashiCorp, Redis, Elastic)

### Takeaway
Between 2023 and 2025 the direction was toward OSI-approved copyleft (AGPL-3.0) for server components, a commercial license made possible by a CLA, and permissive licenses kept for clients:
- Element (Nov 2023)
- Elastic (Aug 2024)
- Redis (May 2025, after a 2024 move to SSPL)
- NetBird (Aug 2025)

The heaviest backlash and durable forks (OpenTofu, Valkey, OpenSearch) followed moving previously open code to source-available terms. Redis's own CEO says the SSPL move "hurt our relationship with the Redis community". Two smaller incidents drew sharp reactions even without a license change, because they took away something users already had. Bitwarden's client briefly became unbuildable as free software (Oct 2024), and Mattermost hid stored messages on self-hosted servers (Dec 2025).

### Cited Findings
- **HashiCorp (10 Aug 2023).**
  - Moved future releases from MPL 2.0 to BSL 1.1. Users may use the code "except where providing a competitive offering to HashiCorp". Competing vendors "will no longer be able to incorporate future releases, bug fixes, or security patches" — [HashiCorp blog](https://www.hashicorp.com/en/blog/hashicorp-adopts-business-source-license).
  - The HN thread drew 632 points and 731 comments (HN Algolia search metadata, 1 Nov 2026; [HN 37081306](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=37081306)).
  - The fork followed: "OpenTF is now OpenTofu" (20 Sep 2023, 438 points, 246 comments, [HN 37581132](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=37581132)), and "Oracle dumps Terraform for OpenTofu" (15 May 2024, 255 points, [HN 40365198](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=40365198)).
  - `opentofu/opentofu` has 30,690 GitHub stars ([GitHub API](https://api.github.com/repos/opentofu/opentofu), 1 Nov 2026).
  - IBM completed its $6.4 billion acquisition of HashiCorp on 27 Feb 2025 (secondary: [heise](https://heise.de/-10303743); [linuxiac](https://linuxiac.com/ibm-completes-6-4-billion-acquisition-of-hashicorp/)).
- **Redis.**

### Inferences
- **What kept trust.** OSI-approved licenses applied from the start, or added back (AGPL plus a commercial exception), retained or restored community standing. Non-OSI relicensing of code that was already open produced forks backed by competitors, and the vendor later partly reversed (Redis, Elastic).
- **Fair source was received more calmly** (FSL in Sentry's case) when adopted openly. But it is not "open source" in Privacy Guides' or OSI's sense, so it conflicts with pitch4's line "Open source, so no one has to take our word for it".
- **Split license by layer.**
  - Apache-2.0 (or MIT) for the sidecar, entry format and SDKs, so harness vendors and enterprise legal teams can embed them without AGPL review. jchw's "corporate lawyers are horrified" point cuts both ways.
  - AGPL-3.0 for the relay and admin server, to block a competitor's closed hosted resale, with a commercial license offered (the NetBird and Element pattern).
  - Avoid BSL, FSL, SSPL and ELv2 entirely.
- **The CLA is the trust weak point** that NetBird commenters raised. Pitch4 could pair any CLA with a public, dated commitment that released code will not be relicensed to non-OSI terms, or use a DCO. This is an inference; I found no evidence on which choice draws more contributors.
- **The most damaging pattern for a record product is the Mattermost one.** Limiting access to data a user already stored, in their own database, after an upgrade. A product whose pitch is "never lose a session" must commit in writing that plan limits never hide, delete or lock anything already written. Expired licenses should degrade to read-only and export, not to "the organization will be disabled", which is Bitwarden's current terms for self-hosted organizations.

### Gaps
- I found no quantitative evidence of revenue effects from relicensing: HashiCorp before and after, Redis after AGPL, or Elastic after AGPL, beyond vendor statements.
- I found no primary data on how many enterprise buyers ban AGPL. The commonly cited Google policy was not fetched.
- I did not measure whether CLAs reduce outside contributions.
- The OpenTofu–CNCF timeline is reported inconsistently in secondary sources and was not verified.

## 3. How these products pass security reviews, and at what cost and timeline

### Takeaway
The E2EE comparables combine SOC 2 Type II (and often ISO 27001) for the hosted service with independent code and crypto audits, often published:
- **Bitwarden:** SOC 2 Type II, SOC 3, ISO 27001, annual client audits and a HackerOne bounty.
- **Proton:** first SOC 2 Type II in July 2025, on top of ISO 27001.
- **Tailscale:** SOC 2 Type II and audits on request, but "does not have a bounty program".
- **Ente:** Cure53 audits in 2023 and 2025, with the full 2025 report published.
- **Standard Notes:** four audits, including Trail of Bits and Cure53.

The developer comparables gate the SOC 2 report or HIPAA behind a tier: Langfuse Pro $199 a month, Supabase Team $599, PostHog Boost $250 for HIPAA. Available cost figures are a SOC 2 audit fee of about $10,000–50,000 with a 3–12 month observation window (vendor figures from Vanta), and independent code audits in the tens of thousands of dollars (an OSTIF example quoted at $143,000 and settled at $71,000). E2EE alone did not replace SOC 2: Proton added it in 2025 because SOC 2 is "often a baseline requirement" in regulated industries.

### Inferences
- **Add SOC 2 Type II for the hosted service to "Trust documents first".**
  - Pitch4's list (open source with reproducible builds, an independent client and protocol audit, a malicious-server threat model, a warrant canary, transparency reports) matches what privacy reviewers ask for. It omits the item vendor-risk reviewers ask for.
  - Proton, the purest E2EE brand in the set, still added SOC 2 in 2025, and the one buyer-side anecdote credits a compliance page with getting approval. Pitch4's beachhead (regulated teams, Microsoft suppliers, agent vendors facing AIUC-1) will run vendor questionnaires.
  - The observation window is 3–12 months, so the clock should start when the first hosted relay goes live, not after launch. That puts a Type II report about 6–15 months after the hosted service starts.
- **Budget.** A client and protocol audit of Cure53, Trail of Bits or NCC scope is likely in the tens of thousands of dollars ($71,000 in the one OSTIF example). SOC 2 adds roughly $10,000–50,000 in audit fees plus tooling and staff time. Ente's CERN sponsorship and OSTIF programs show that third parties sometimes fund audits of open-source privacy software. Whether URnetwork would qualify is unknown.
- **Publish rather than gate.** Ente publishes full reports, while Tailscale gives audits on request and SOC 2 under NDA. Publishing the audit and the metadata statement would separate pitch4 from Tailscale, its nearest reach incumbent, and counter URnetwork's trust gaps. Tailscale's plain metadata sentence ("We do receive metadata about which of your private nodes connect…") is a model for pitch4's planned "lists all metadata our servers see".
- **Bug bounty.** Bitwarden has one and Tailscale explicitly does not. A small public bounty is cheap evidence of good faith, but it is not required by either reviewer community, based on this evidence.
- **E2EE is a trust property, not a purchase reason.** Infisical shows that E2EE can block server-side features buyers want (integrations, syncs). For pitch4, the hosted ledger's E2EE will block server-side search, SIEM export, retention enforcement and legal hold unless they run in a customer-held component. Examples are the admin app, a customer-run reader, or the visible audit member. The Assured tier must be designed around that constraint, or "hosted blind" and "Assured" will conflict.

### Gaps
- I did not research reproducible builds, warrant canaries or transparency reports for these comparables in this round. Earlier notes found that Privacy Guides' cloud criteria omit reproducible builds.
- I did not open Proton's SOC 2 post directly. NetBird's and Infisical's SOC 2 status is unverified.
- No vendor disclosed what its Cure53 or Trail of Bits audits cost. Bitwarden does not name its audit firms on its compliance page.

## 4. What users pay for when self-hosting is free

### Takeaway
Users who pay for hosted open-source software mostly say they are buying their time back from running servers, plus support, accountability and someone else on call. The open-source licence matters mainly as an exit option, not as a reason to self-host.
- In the one direct HN poll-style thread, 20 of 26 top-level respondents said they would pay. Their most common reason was not wanting to run servers (11 people).
- Businesses pay for SSO, but resent having to buy a sales-led enterprise tier just to get it.
- People keep self-hosting when hosted usage pricing becomes large at volume, for data-control reasons (GDPR), or when budgets are per-volunteer.
- Users react strongly to self-hosted friction they believe is deliberate (Sentry) and to caps on data they already hold (Mattermost).
- A maintainer of a 100% open-source service reports it is "super hard" to get users to pay when self-hosting is easy.

- **Zulip's model, cited by HN commenters as the fair alternative to Mattermost.** Valodim: "Zulip self hosted has all features with no restrictions, except for mobile notifications which require a subscription for $3.50/u/m (unless you are less than 10 users...)". emptysongglass: "It's a yearly fee that amounts to a couple hundred dollars." Both are in the HN 46383675 thread. Prices were confirmed on [Zulip plans](https://zulip.com/plans/).

### Inferences
- **For pitch4's developer side** (free for one person), HN evidence suggests individuals will not pay for what they can self-host. The ntfy seller's experience and Vaultwarden's popularity point the same way.
- **For the team side**, people say they pay for:
  - not running the relay or ledger themselves;
  - someone accountable when it breaks, including SLAs and "someone to sue";
  - SSO;
  - a compliance packet that gets a manager's approval.
- **This supports pitch4's choice of paid items**, with three adjustments:
  - Make the Team tier, including SSO and SCIM, self-serve with a card, without a sales call. This answers icambron's complaint.
  - Sell supported self-hosting and SLAs as their own line, the way Mattermost, Langfuse and Tailscale sell support packages.
  - Keep the self-hosted install deliberately simple, for example a single binary plus plain git, and say so publicly. The Sentry thread shows users read heavy self-hosting as a ploy, and the PostHog post shows supporting many self-host topologies is costly. Supporting one or two install shapes resolves both.
- **Volume pricing is the main reason teams leave hosted.** Weryj and BenGosub cite it, the latter with a $3–5k tracing bill. If pitch4 meters hosted storage and edge traffic, it needs caps and predictable pricing, or the heaviest agent users will self-host. That is acceptable only if self-hosted users still buy Assured or support.
- **Small, many-member organizations will self-host.** Volunteer orgs and homelabs (prometheon1) have little budget. NetBird's "active users only" billing and Zulip's free threshold of 10 users are low-friction ways to keep them inside the hosted service.

### Gaps
- Reddit could not be queried by script, so the user evidence is HN-only and self-selected. The single "would you pay" thread dates from 2023.
- I found no survey with stated methods that measures why buyers pay for the hosted edition of open-source software, or the share of users who self-host versus buy hosted, for any comparable.
- The Linux Foundation 2025 support figures come from blog excerpts; the full PDF was not opened.
