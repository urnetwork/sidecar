## 1. What do the draft's points of focus for CC5.1, CC5.3 and CC8.1 (and its change-management criteria) say about automated agents, unapproved AI and third-party models, and when would they apply?

### Takeaway
- **Substance confirmed by two independent secondary readings (Noru and Elevate):**
  - **CC5.1:** access control "considers robotic process automation and AI agents", and access reviews cover AI accounts.
  - **CC5.3:** controls "detect unsanctioned AI platforms and unauthorized AI tools and models".
  - **CC8.1:** "Third-party AI models, APIs and plugins are evaluated before use and as they change."
  - Neither source quotes the draft itself.
- **The draft renumbers the criteria.**
  - CC5 becomes logical and physical asset protection.
  - Change management moves to CC7.1–CC7.3.
  - CC8 becomes vendor management.
  - So "CC5.1" and "CC8.1" mean different things in 2017 and 2027 reports.
- **No source says the draft names coding agents or AI-written code.** The nearest hook is a segregation-of-duties point of focus in change management: limits on one person developing and deploying alone. It bites when the engineer who prompts an agent also approves its pull request.
- **Timing.** The AICPA says the criteria "would be effective for engagements beginning on or after Dec. 31, 2027, with early adoption permitted". Comments close on 15 Jan 2027, and points of focus require no specific control.
- **Pitch4's line "December 2027: Draft SOC 2 criteria … apply" has the right date but overstates it:**
  - the criteria are a proposal;
  - they attach to when an engagement begins;
  - most reports in 2027 still use the 2017 criteria;
  - the AI points of focus are prompts for auditors, not required controls.

### Inferences
- **How firm the readings are.** The substance of the three points of focus is reasonably firm, because two independent secondary readings agree. The exact wording is not; treat Noru's phrasings as close paraphrases.
- **Where CC5.3 likely comes from.** Elevate's "unauthorized AI software" wording suggests draft CC5.3 descends from the 2017 criterion on unauthorized or malicious software. That is an inference from the wording and is unverified.
- **The point most likely to touch agent-written code is the segregation-of-duties test in change management, not CC5.1, CC5.3 or CC8.1.**
  - Today an auditor checks author ≠ approver from forge data.
  - With agents, the forge author is often a bot or the engineer's own account. If the engineer who directed the agent then approves the pull request, the check either passes falsely (the bot is the author) or fails.
  - Only a record that names the human who started or directed each agent run can settle it.
- **Corrected timeline wording for pitch4 page 5:** "From 31 Dec 2027, if the AICPA finalizes them, SOC 2 engagements move to the 2027 criteria (early adoption allowed). Their AI points of focus ask about agent accounts, unapproved AI and third-party models. They prompt the auditor's questions; they are not required controls."
  - Type 2 periods typically run 3–12 months, so the first mandatory Type 2 reports would arrive in late 2028 to 2029 (inference).
- **The "moat is time" line needs rewording.**
  - A SOC 2 Type 2 tests only the examination period. A year of signed approvals held *before* that period is not what the auditor samples.
  - What counts is that the record is complete from the first day of the first period under the 2027 criteria (Noru: "Plan the switch for the first audit period that starts after 31 December 2027").
  - Earlier history still helps elsewhere, in client questionnaires and incident reconstruction.
- **Compliance mappings must carry the criteria version.** From 2027 to 2029 some customers will report under 2017 IDs and others under 2027 IDs, and the same ID means different things. AIUC-1's crosswalk already uses 2017 IDs.

## 2. How will CPA firms test these points (populations, sampling, system-generated versus hand-assembled evidence)?

### Takeaway
- **Auditors test the controls the company describes.** They draw samples from populations that the company produces. They must test that information produced by the entity (IPE) is complete and accurate.
- **Hand-assembled populations are a known deficiency.** Linford's example is a change list that "was manually prepared", where "changes may have been missed". Firms prefer a population pulled directly from the system of record, for example "directly obtained from the source code library".
- **Samples are small.** Linford's AICPA-aligned example starts at **25** items with no expected deviations and expands to **40** after one deviation; a second deviation fails the control. No AICPA-mandated table exists, and firm tables differ.
- **Automated controls can be tested once** when IT general controls (change management and access) are effective. This comes from secondary sources and SOX practice. A control that the sidecar *enforces* could therefore be tested once instead of sampled.
- **The only concrete auditor requests about AI-specific evidence are vendor-reported:**
  - "Pull a sample of 10 production commits and identify which were AI-assisted";
  - "Show the review record for each AI-assisted commit, including reviewer identity".

### Cited Findings
**Information produced by the entity**
- **Linford & Co.** (Rhonda Willert, CPA, partner; 28 Apr 2021) — [Linford IPE/IUC](https://linfordco.com/blog/iuc-ipe-audit-procedures-for-soc-audits/) [opened]:
  - Who checks what: "For IUC, the entity should have checks or controls in place to be confident that the information is complete and accurate." "While for IPE, the practitioner (auditor) should have checks in place to be confident that the information is complete and accurate."
  - For a change listing, the practitioner must "perform procedures over the listing to ascertain that it is reasonable to believe that that population provided is complete and accurate."
  - A listed reason a population was insufficient: "The change population was not systematically generated from the Application XYZ."
  - Manual populations: "IPE/IUC that is not system generated will also still require testing for completeness and accuracy". Example: "The change population may not include all changes, as the list was manually prepared and changes may have been missed."
  - The preferred alternative: "a program change population directly obtained from the source code library".
  - System-generated IPE "is reliant on the accuracy and completeness of the source data". IUC testing covers whether "The report logic and/or source data can not be changed inappropriately or without authorization."
  - It cites PCAOB AS 15: "test the controls over the accuracy and completeness of the information" and "Evaluate whether the information is sufficiently precise and detailed for purposes of the audit."
- **AT-C 205.36**, the attestation standard used for SOC 2, requires evidence about the accuracy and completeness of information the entity produces. — [search summary; the standard was not opened]
- **Completeness checks named in practitioner guides:**
  - a stated date range covering the whole period;
  - a record count that matches an independent source;
  - documentation of the source system, query, parameters, who ran it, and the reconciliation used.
  - Sources: [TCSA](https://www.tcsa.in/learn/soc-2-evidence-collection), [Safeguard](https://safeguard.sh/resources/blog/system-generated-reports-as-audit-evidence-completeness-and-accuracy) [search summary; practitioner or vendor blogs]

**Sampling**
- **Linford & Co.** (Nicole Hemmer, partner; first published 18 Dec 2018, updated 23 Apr 2025) — [Linford audit sampling](https://linfordco.com/blog/audit-sampling/) [opened]:
  - Its tables "align with the guidance set forth in the audit sampling guide from the AICPA", which was last updated in December 2019.
  - "Table 1 is used for larger sample sizes (250 or greater in the population)."
  - Worked example: an initial sample of 25 with no expected deviations. "If it is found that one of the 25 selected did not attend training, the sample would be expanded to 40 people." "If another deviation is found, sampling would stop", and the control is concluded not to be operating effectively.
  - For small populations, "any deviations would be a failure of the operating effectiveness of the control."
  - The per-frequency table values are images and were not read.
- **No binding table.** No AICPA-mandated SOC 2 sample-size table exists. Published tables conflict; for daily controls, one gives 15–25 samples and another 25–30. — [soc2auditors.org](https://soc2auditors.org/insights/soc-2-sample-size/) and others [search summary]
- **Type 2 change testing:** the auditor selects "a sample of changes made during the audit period" and traces each "from its initial request through to its final deployment". — [soc2auditors.org](https://soc2auditors.org/insights/soc-2-change-management-controls/) [search summary]

- **Already covered elsewhere, not repeated:** HITRUST scores "Implemented" with sample-based tests (L2-2.03); IANS tells buyers to confirm that auditors "designed and executed their own tests" (L1-1.08).

### Inferences
- **The main test of agent-written code is the change-management sample.**
  - The auditor pulls the change population from the forge or deploy system and samples about 25 items.
  - Where agents write a large share of merges, many sampled changes will be agent-authored even without stratification.
  - An auditor who judges agent changes riskier will want a per-change flag to stratify on.
  - **The sidecar should therefore enrich the forge's own population, not replace it.** The auditor will reconcile to the forge, so changes with no linked run must appear too, flagged "no agent run recorded".
- **For a sidecar export to count as reliable IPE,** it must carry:
  - the period and query parameters;
  - record counts;
  - a reconciliation against forge counts;
  - the exporter version;
  - evidence that the report logic and ledger cannot be changed without authorization.
  - Signed checkpoints plus an open verifier the auditor can run cover the last item directly. Evidence the auditor obtains directly ranks higher (ISA 500, L2-2.03).
- **The biggest audit lever is enforcement, not logging.** These controls could be tested once as automated controls, plus the IT general controls over the sidecar's configuration and admin access:
  - an egress allowlist for model endpoints;
  - blocking merges where approver = sponsor;
  - requiring approval for sensitive tool operations.
  - "Fewer samples, less screenshotting" is a concrete benefit for the compliance buyer.
- **How each draft point would likely be tested (my inference from the general method):**
  - **CC5.1:** take the population of agent identities and their grants; sample access reviews (for example two quarters) to confirm agent identities were included and excess access removed.
  - **CC5.3:** inspect the detection configuration (network, DLP, SSE, or sidecar egress); take the population of detections for the period; sample triage and resolution.
    - The sidecar sees only agents that route through it, so its export must state that coverage boundary.
  - **CC8.1:** take the population of third-party AI components and their *changes* in the period; sample them for evaluation and approval before use.
    - Silent version changes behind a hosted model's alias are hard to list by hand, so a system-generated population of model changes observed in sessions is a natural piece of IPE.

### Gaps
- **Not found:** any CPA-firm publication on stratifying change populations by AI authorship, or on treating "prompter = approver" as an exception.
- **Not read:** Linford's per-frequency sample values (published as images) and the AICPA Audit Sampling guide.
- **Unconfirmed:** whether SOC 2 firms accept a single test of an automated control without SOX-style benchmarking documentation. Only secondary sources say so.
- **Not covered by any source found:** auditor reliance on a third-party tool's export. In particular, whether customers' auditors would need URnetwork's own SOC 2 report for the hosted ledger.

## 3. Do auditors, AIUC-1, bank and insurer supervisors, client terms or GRC templates require review by a human or by an independent model, and in what form?

### Takeaway
- **No source I found accepts a second model as the approver.** The required forms are:
  1. **Auditors and consultancies:** a separate, named human approver at merge or release.
     - A-LIGN's view reached me second-hand.
     - BrightByte requires an approver "not the same person who wrote the prompt".
  2. **DORA RTS Art. 17(1)(b)** (binding on EU financial entities): "mechanisms to ensure the independence of the functions that approve changes and the functions responsible for requesting and implementing those changes".
  3. **NYDFS, 21 May 2026** (advisory): "additional testing and validation procedures, including human oversight," "for AI-generated code prior to deployment in production environments".
  4. **MAS Guidelines, 7 Oct 2026** (effective 7 Oct 2027 and 7 Oct 2028). Each institution must:
     - define when "mandatory human review and validation of AI outputs prior to use is required";
     - keep "an approved list of AI tools";
     - use technical controls such as "network monitoring" to "detect unauthorised use of AI";
     - record agent identifiers, tools and guardrails in its inventory;
     - review oversight "decisions and interventions (including incidents as well as near misses)", while weighing "automation bias and decision fatigue".
  5. **AIUC-1 D003.4:** "Requiring human approval for sensitive tool operations", raised to a core control on 15 Oct 2026. Its examples are transactions, record deletion and permission changes, not code merges.
  6. **PCI DSS 6.2.3:** bespoke code must be reviewed. *If* the review is manual, it must be done by someone other than the author and approved by management. The conditional wording implies automated review can satisfy 6.2.3.
- **AI review is acceptable as an extra control** when "control activities exist around the AI tool/agent" (A-LIGN, second-hand).
- **No public Vanta or Drata template** for reviewing AI-written code was found.
- **Human review of agent pull requests degrades.** Approval rates rise and inline comments fall as reviewers habituate.

### Cited Findings
**Auditors and consultancies**
- **A-LIGN (second-hand).** Earlier notes recorded A-LIGN's view: "not seeing fully AI-only code review (no human) accepted as standard"; "Most clients keep a human at the approval/push-to-prod step" (assurance_demand.md). Another snapshot of the thread adds two points:
  - "AI in review is accepted, provided control activities exist around the AI tool/agent";
  - the forum expected "draft updates expected Q4".
  - Source: [Vanta Community](https://community.vanta.com/x/grc/msg_1Y7mC29K6p5d/ai-only-code-reviews-and-soc-2-change-management-e) [search summary; second-hand]

  - An agent with tool access "needs an account, a credential, a scope, and an audit log."

**AIUC-1** (standard pages fetched 1 Nov 2026)
- **D003 "Restrict unsafe tool calls"**: Mandatory, every 12 months, Preventative. — [AIUC-1 D003](https://standard.aiuc-1.com/reliability/restrict-unsafe-tool-calls.md) [opened]
  - Requirement: "Implement safeguards or technical controls to prevent tool calls in AI systems from executing unauthorized actions," "accessing restricted information, or making decisions beyond their intended scope."
  - **D003.1 "Tool authorization & validation"** (Should include): "Code or configuration showing function and tool allowlists, parameter validation logic".
  - **D003.3 "Tool call log"** (Should include): evidence may include the originating MCP server, tool name and version, inputs and timestamps.
  - **D003.4 "Human-approval workflows"** (Should include): "Requiring human approval for sensitive tool operations".
    - Evidence: approval-workflow configuration, code, or ticketing for sensitive, high-risk or multi-step operations.
    - Examples: financial transactions, irreversible record changes, and credential or permission changes.
  - **D003.5 "Tool call log reviews"** (May include).
  - "Alternative evidence" is allowed.
  - Crosswalk: SOC 2 "CC5.1: Select and develop control activities" and PI1.2–PI1.4. The page does not mention code changes or coding agents.
- **Changelog** — [AIUC-1 changelog](https://standard.aiuc-1.com/changelog) [opened]:
  - Latest release 15 Oct 2026; next release 15 Jan 2027.

**How good human review is**
- **"Habituation at the Gate: Rising Approval and Declining Scrutiny in Human Review of AI Agent Code"** (Yu, Liu, Jiang, Jia, Wang, Qian, Chen; arXiv 2606.22721; 21 Jun 2026) — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.22721) [opened, abstract]:
  - Data: the AIDev dataset; "400 repeat reviewers who collectively submitted 11,429 reviews over a seven-month observation period".
  - Approval rate for agent PRs rose "from 30.1% to 36.8%"; the gap reaches "+14.5 pp from first to tenth decile" of reviewer experience.
  - The shift is "agent-specific (human PR approval rates decline over the same period)" and "not explained by PR difficulty (median PR size is flat)".
  - "review latency increases rather than decreases (+3.5x)," while "inline comment volume decreases (-22%, p=0.0014)".
  - The authors' reading: "reflexive habituation under growing workload rather than rational trust calibration alone."
  - Scope: open-source repositories, not enterprise ones.

### Inferences
- **The common form is a separate human approver** who is distinct from the person who directed the agent. The approval is captured by the system with identity and timestamp, and AI review sits alongside it as a supplementary control.
  - **For financial institutions, add:** a documented oversight design, review of interventions and near misses, and evidence that automation bias is managed (MAS ¶5.9).
  - **No source treats a second model as an "independent function"** under DORA 17(1)(b) or as "human oversight". An independent-model reviewer is a *quality* control, not an *approval* control.
  - **Pitch4 should not suggest that model review can replace human approval.** It can offer model review as recorded extra evidence.
- **MAS reads like a sidecar spec, and arrives before the SOC 2 draft.** The approved tool list and request process (¶2.5(c)), network detection of unauthorized AI (fn 22), agent inventory attributes (fn 25), oversight logs and near-miss review (¶5.9(d)), and change-triggered re-approval with monitoring for silent changes (¶5.25) map almost one to one onto sidecar features. Singapore institutions need Sections 3–4 (identification and inventory) from **7 Oct 2027**, earlier than the SOC 2 draft.
- **Two reasons to capture review depth.** The habituation result and MAS's explicit concern with "automation bias and decision fatigue" both point to depth measures, not a single approval bit: approval rate per reviewer over time, comment counts, change requests, and reverts.
- **An agent change approved by the human who directed it would likely be challenged under DORA 17(1)(b)** (inference). The approving function is then not independent of the requesting function.
- **When signed review records add value.** Inside one company, SOC 2 auditors accept the forge's own approval logs, so signatures are not required. They add value when evidence:
  - crosses an organizational boundary, as when a supplier proves human review to a client under AT&T-style terms; or
  - faces doubt about authenticity, as after Delve.
  - That is where a paid tier can charge, not for the basic approval capture.

## 4. What evidence fields must the sidecar export, and what does the answer mean for pitch4?

### Takeaway
- **Export the change population as tested information produced by the entity.** It should enrich the forge's change population with agent metadata and carry its own completeness proof: period, parameters, counts, reconciliation, signature and a verifier.
- **Add four smaller populations:**
  - agent identities and grants (CC5.1; MAS fn 25);
  - approved versus observed AI, with detections (CC5.3; MAS ¶2.5(c) and fn 22);
  - third-party model change events (CC8.1; MAS ¶5.10 and ¶5.25);
  - human-oversight events and review-depth metrics (MAS ¶5.9; AIUC-1 D003.4).
- **The single most differentiating field is the sponsoring human for each agent run.** It enables a sponsor ≠ approver check across *all* agent-authored changes. That check is what DORA 17(1)(b), PCI 6.2.3.1 and the draft's change-management segregation point of focus turn on, and forge data alone cannot answer it.
- **The brief's hypothesis holds, with one correction.**
  - **Confirmed:** auditors will sample changes that include agent-authored ones, and, under the draft, ask about unapproved AI.
  - **Corrected:** the exports are "audit-critical" only when a customer's control description relies on them. They must then survive completeness and accuracy testing.
- **Signed review records** have no mandate inside one company. They are worth charging for across companies (supplier to client) and as auditor-verifiable evidence.

### Cited Findings
This section draws on the sources in Sections 1–3. Each line below names the field set and the requirement that motivates it.
- **Change population and segregation of duties.**
  - Change population from the system of record: Linford ("directly obtained from the source code library").
  - Separate approver: Noru, on draft CC7.2 and CC7.3 ("the evidence that a separate person approved it has to come from somewhere"); BrightByte ("not the same person who wrote the prompt").

  - Human oversight of AI-generated code before production: NYDFS.

- **Agent identities and grants:** Noru on draft CC5.1 ("considers robotic process automation and AI agents"); MAS fn 25 (agent identifiers, the tools and systems the agent can access, components, guardrails); AIUC-1 D003.1 (allowlists and permission schemas per agent).

**What this means for pitch4**
1. **Sell auditor-ready populations, not "logs".**
   - Rename the exports in the pitch as change, identity, AI-inventory, model-change and oversight populations, each with a reconciliation.
   - Of the record pieces only the sidecar can produce, the reconciliation and the sponsor field are the hardest to copy. A forge or GRC platform lacks the cross-harness run linkage; a single-harness vendor lacks the cross-harness view.
2. **Make segregation of duties an enforced, automated control.**
   - Block or flag merges where approver = sponsor, and agent identities that hold merge rights.
   - An auditor can test an enforced control once, instead of sampling 25–40 changes. That is a concrete efficiency claim for the compliance buyer, and a "differentiation" lever.
3. **Ship compliance mappings that carry the criteria version.** Map to 2017 and 2027 SOC 2 IDs side by side, plus AIUC-1, MAS, DORA Art. 17, NYDFS and PCI 6.2.3. Phrase each as "evidence for", never "satisfies". The renumbering creates confusion from 2027 to 2029, and AIUC-1's crosswalk already uses 2017 IDs.
4. **Add Singapore financial institutions to "where we start", and fix the timeline.**
   - MAS Sections 3–4 (approved tools, shadow-AI detection, inventory with agent attributes) apply from 7 Oct 2027.
   - Sections 5–6 (human oversight with near-miss review, third-party AI, change re-approval) apply from 7 Oct 2028.
   - NYDFS's advisory already asks for human oversight of AI-generated code.
   - Replace "December 2027: Draft SOC 2 criteria … apply" with the conditional, engagement-based wording in §1.
5. **Tiering (inference; willingness to pay untested).**
   - **Team:** the change population with agent flags and segregation-of-duties flags, the agent identity list, and approved-versus-observed AI. Auditors already accept forge logs, and GRC platforms (Vanta's Claude Code plugin and GrantGuard) are moving toward these, so withholding them risks losing the wedge.
   - **Assured:**
     - signed, client-verifiable review attestations per release, the cross-organization record the brief describes;
     - the auditor verifier and a read-only auditor seat;
     - version-aware mappings;
     - review-depth analytics;
     - **a SOC 2 Type 2 for URnetwork's hosted ledger.** A customer's auditor will treat a hosted ledger as a subservice organization, and the draft's CC8.1 points of focus include subservice SOC reports. This is an inference, and it ties to the trust gaps in L1-1.09.
6. **Do not claim AI review counts as approval.** Offer "independent model review, recorded" as additional evidence beside a human approver. No assessor text found accepts model-only approval, and the habituation data show why reviewers' depth should be measured.

### Gaps
- **Willingness to pay** for signed cross-organization review attestations was not tested.
- **Interface not checked:** whether Vanta, Drata or Secureframe accept pushed custom evidence through APIs, which decides whether to integrate or compete.
- **Unknown auditor appetite:** no auditor has said whether an enforced segregation check would be tested as an automated control, or how much reliance a third-party export would get without the vendor's own SOC 2. Interviews with 3–5 SOC 2 firms would settle both.
