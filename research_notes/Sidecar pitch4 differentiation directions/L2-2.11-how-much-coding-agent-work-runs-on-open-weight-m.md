## 1. How much coding-agent work runs on open-weight models?

### Takeaway
The answer depends on what is counted.

- **By people:** about one agent-using developer in five uses an open-weight model family in agents (11-20% in the Stack Overflow 2026 raw data). Only about 1 in 40 (2.4%) uses nothing else. Among users of open harnesses the shares rise to 44% and 4.9%.
- **By tokens:** on OpenRouter, open weights carry 55-86% of coding tokens (80% among the models OpenRouter lists) and 71% of all tokens.
- **By money:** open weights take only 23-54% of OpenRouter coding spend (33% among listed models), and 5% of enterprise metered AI spend (Futurum, in 1.4).

So open weights carry most developer coding tokens, a minority of gateway coding spend, and a small share of enterprise money. Almost all of it is used alongside closed models, not instead of them.

- **Estimates, with ranges:**

  | Measure | Estimate | Basis |

  | Agent-model users who use only open-weight families in agents | **about 1-2.5%** | SO raw: 2.4% |

- **"Open-weight teams" as a pure segment is very small, about 2-5% of agent users.** Mixed fleets are the norm: open-harness users are four times as likely as others to use open models (44% against 11%), yet 95% of them also use closed models.
- **The token-spend gap has a cause.** Open weights take the cheap, high-volume steps of a task (implementation, file reads and writes, shell), while closed models take most of the spend on the hard steps. Within a single coding task, a team's agents use both. A record that covers only open-weight calls would miss most of the spend and most of the hard decisions.

- No source measures coding tokens on self-hosted servers. Stack Overflow has no deployment-mode question, and OpenRouter sees only hosted traffic.
- I could not obtain the model mix per harness on OpenRouter; that data is loaded client-side from a chunk I did not find.
- JetBrains 2026 detail was blocked (403). Menlo and a16z 2026 breakdowns by use case were not found.

## 2. Of that open-weight coding work, how much runs on servers the team hosts rather than on third-party hosts?

### Takeaway
No source measures this for coding. The evidence splits by buyer.

- **Developers and small teams use third-party hosts.**
  - OpenRouter alone carried about 133T open-weight tokens in the week of 26 October 2026, and about a third of OpenRouter traffic is coding.
  - The open models that carry that coding traffic (DeepSeek V4.1 Flash, GLM 5.3, Kimi K3) are large mixture-of-experts models that need multi-GPU servers. Each is offered by 29 to 48 OpenRouter endpoints.
  - Local signals are large but concentrate on small models.
- **Enterprises already running open weights in production, across all workloads, mostly run them on infrastructure they control.** Futurum (1.4): self-managed cloud 46%, on-premises 42%, managed providers 27%, hyperscaler-native 29%.

My estimate:
- 80-90% or more of developer open-weight coding tokens run on third-party hosts.
- In enterprises with open weights in production, a majority of open-weight use runs on servers the team controls.
- Across the whole market, third-party-hosted tokens are probably the majority, because gateway volume is so large.

pitch4's "agents reach the inference servers you run" fits the enterprise group, not the developer group.

### Inferences
- **Developer open-weight coding runs mainly at third-party hosts.** Three facts point that way:
  - OpenRouter alone carries about 40T open-weight coding tokens a week (an estimate: 133T open tokens × about 32% code share).
  - The models that carry those tokens need data-center GPUs, which most of the segment (freelancers and small firms) does not have.
  - Local downloads favor small variants (30B-class mixture-of-experts models, 20B models) by factors of 2-18.

  The local signal is large in absolute terms (tens of millions of Ollama downloads). But it describes small models on workstations, which the 1.1 evidence shows struggle with tool calls. It does not describe the frontier-sized open models that win coding traffic.
- **Self-hosting at production scale is an enterprise and data-center pattern** (Futurum's 42% on-premises; Pegatron's NVFP4 GLM on Blackwell in 1.4). It is real, but smaller in count of buyers.
- **pitch4's privacy claim is false for most open-weight coding tokens.** "With open-weight models, nothing has to leave your control" and "Who sees prompts and code ... Open weights you run: Only your team" hold only when the team runs the server. In the majority case, the host sees prompts and code. Some hosts state they retain prompts (8 of 48 GLM 5.3 endpoints), and 13% of endpoints state no headquarters.

### Gaps
- No token-level measurement of self-hosted coding volume exists. vLLM, SGLang, Ollama and LM Studio publish no usage telemetry that I could find.
- Futurum's hosting split is for all workloads, not coding.
- Download counts cannot be converted to users or tokens.
- I did not check Artificial Analysis provider pages; OpenRouter's endpoint metadata was used instead.

## 3. Which companies run open harnesses (OpenCode, Pi, Cline, Kilo, Continue) on self-hosted weights in production, who buys for them, and in which sectors and regions?

### Takeaway
Named public evidence is still thin. Pegatron (OpenCode with GLM on premises; 1.4) remains the only named organization I can show running an open harness on self-hosted weights in production.

The named enterprise users of open harnesses are documented with hyperscaler or vendor-hosted inference, not self-hosted weights:
- Samsung, SAP and Oracle with Cline, by Cline's own account;
- Salesforce Agentforce, named by Cline as an enterprise user;
- Anaconda's enterprise base behind Kilo.

Vendors name the buyers as platform and developer-experience (DevX) teams (Cline), and CTOs and CIOs (Anaconda).

In the Stack Overflow data, the open-harness-plus-open-model segment skews towards:
- IT services and systems integrators (21%);
- freelancers and small firms;
- Europe, with Germany at 10%;
- China and Russia, which are over-represented (3% each, against 1% in the base).

The segment also holds more purchase authority than the base: 21.6% make the final purchase decision, against 15.7%.

## 4. Which third-party hosts offer attestation or signed receipts?

### Takeaway
Attested or signed open-weight inference exists at a few small hosts:
- **Phala Cloud (formerly RedPill)** returns a signed receipt with every response.
- **NEAR AI** returns response signatures.
- **Tinfoil** offers attestation with a weights Merkle root (Modelwrap, 1.5), but no per-request receipts.
- **Chutes, Venice, NanoGPT and Privatemode** offer some form of attestation.

None of the mainstream hosts (Together, Fireworks, Bedrock, Vertex, DeepInfra, Baseten, Groq) offers attestation or receipts. On OpenRouter, Together, Fireworks, Bedrock and Google do not even state the quantization on any sampled endpoint. Attested hosting is a tiny share of volume: Phala's 27.7B tokens a day on OpenRouter (its own figure) is about 0.1% of OpenRouter's roughly 26.9T a day.

The customer-side verifier is no longer empty space. teep, an open-source local proxy published in 2026, verifies six attested providers for Aider, Cline and OpenCode and fails closed. It does not describe storing receipts.

### Cited Findings

**Host-by-host**

- **Phala Cloud / RedPill** (docs now under docs.phala.com) [fetched; vendor docs and claims]:
  - **Hardware:** "TDX + H100 / H200 / Blackwell" with "CPU + GPU TEE". "Client SDK fetches the gateway's TDX quote with a fresh nonce and runs dcap-qvl locally". "Failed verification stops the workload."
  - **Receipts:** "Every response carries x-receipt-id". "Receipt headers come back on every response — even from curl". Every route returns "a signed receipt, and an attested-session reference for the serving upstream."
  - **Signature API:** `GET /v1/signature/{id}`, where `id` is a receipt ID (`rcpt-...`) or a chat response ID. The signed `text` is `"<request body hash>:<response hash>"`, using ecdsa by default or ed25519. The canonical receipt inside the response is always Ed25519, under the domain `dstack-kms-receipt-ed25519-v1`.
  - **Retention:** not stated. An older doc reportedly gave about a 30-minute window [search summary].
  - **Weights:** the page does not say whether weights are measured.

**A customer-side verifier already exists**
- **teep** (github.com/13rac1/teep; AGPL-3.0, with dual licensing "available for commercial use"; pseudo-version published 1 Nov 2026; binaries "coming soon") [fetched]:
  - "It's a local proxy that sits between your app and the AI provider."
  - **Providers it verifies:** Venice AI, NEAR AI (Direct and Cloud), Chutes, Tinfoil (Cloud and Direct), Phala Cloud and NanoGPT.
  - **What it checks:**
    - Intel TDX quotes or AMD SEV-SNP reports;
    - NVIDIA GPU attestation, with NRAS verification;
    - `tls_key_binding`;
    - model weight hashes, Sigstore provenance and transparency logs ("coverage varies by provider").
  - **Failure handling:** "If any enforced check fails, teep blocks the request". It "never forwards a request to an unverified provider."
  - **Clients:** Aider, Cline and Opencode are listed as clients; Claude Code is not.
  - **Records:** storing receipts is not described. Only request correlation IDs and an internal package for "HTTP traffic recording and replay for attestation verification" are mentioned.
  - Source: [pkg.go.dev, teep](https://pkg.go.dev/github.com/13rac1/teep)

### Inferences
- **The core of a third-party-host offer cannot be attestation verification.** Roughly 99.9% of OpenRouter open-weight tokens run on hosts with no attestation, and 31% of endpoints do not even state quantization. What every host can offer is a routing policy and record built from declared metadata: host, headquarters, datacenter, quantization, prompt retention. Hardware attestation is the premium tier for the few hosts that support it.
- **A verifier alone is no longer whitespace.** teep verifies across providers and fails closed, and Dstack's `pap` CLI (1.5) does the same for Phala. The whitespace 1.5 identified narrows to two things:
  1. keeping receipts under team keys beyond the provider's retention (unstated at Phala, absent at Tinfoil);
  2. linking each receipt to the agent session and change set, across self-hosted servers, attested hosts and hosted APIs.
- **OpenRouter may close part of the gap itself.** The empty `requiredAttestationTypes` field and Phala's "enterprise tier" claim suggest it is building attestation-aware routing. That is inference from a field name and a vendor claim.

### Gaps
- NEAR's response-signature specification is still unread (404).
- Phala's receipt retention is not stated.
- Whether Venice's and Chutes's OpenRouter endpoints are TEE-backed cannot be told from OpenRouter metadata.
- OpenRouter has not documented an attestation feature or confirmed Phala's enterprise-tier claim.
- I could not determine whether any attested host binds the quantization and runtime (as opposed to weights) into its receipt.

## 5. What the answers mean for pitch4's differentiation and offer

### Takeaway
The evidence argues for three changes:
1. **Stop leading with "organizations that run open weights" sized by Menlo's 11% of spend.** Lead with mixed fleets instead.
2. **Widen model access from "the inference servers you run" to "only approved inference, wherever it runs".** That means self-hosted servers, attested hosts and declared third-party hosts. Each call gets a record tiered as observed, signed or attested.
3. **Make "which host saw this code" a first-class record and policy.**

Verifying attested providers is worth offering, but as the premium tier, because attested hosts carry about 0.1% of gateway volume and an open-source verifier (teep) already exists. The distinctive piece is retention and linking under team keys, not verification.

### Cited Findings
- **pitch4's current claims** [fetched, local file] — [pitch4.html](/Users/brien/urnetwork/sidecar/pitch4.html):
  - "Organizations that run open weights so no vendor reads their code: The best fit ... Also a minority: open-source models hold 11% of enterprise LLM spend".
  - "With open-weight models, nothing has to leave your control."
  - "Agents reach the inference servers you run, such as vLLM, SGLang, Ollama, or llama.cpp, and nothing else."
  - "Proves: The sidecar beside the model server signs which weights it serves."
- **Mixed fleets are the norm:** 20.3% of agent-model users use open-family models in agents, but only 2.4% use them exclusively; for open-harness users, 44.0% and 4.9% [computed, Stack Overflow 2026, section 1].
- **Third-party hosting dominates developer volume:** about 133T open-weight tokens a week on OpenRouter; a 32% code share; 29-48 endpoints per leading open coding model [computed, sections 1-2].
- **Host disclosure is uneven:** 31% of endpoints give no quantization, 13% give no headquarters, and 8 of 48 GLM 5.3 endpoints retain prompts [computed, sections 2 and 4].
- **Attested hosting is small and verifiable by others:** about 0.1% of OpenRouter tokens (from a Phala claim), and teep fails closed across six providers [section 4].

### Inferences

**Directions, in order of evidence strength**

1. **Lead with mixed fleets.**
   - Replace the open-weight "best fit" line and the Menlo figure with: "one in five agent users already mixes in open-weight models; open weights carry most gateway coding tokens but a third of the spend".
   - Sell one record that covers hosted closed models (observed tier) and open models wherever they run.
   - This answers the "segment is small" objection, because the record does not depend on the team being open-weight-only.
2. **Fix the privacy claim and make the host part of the record.**
   - "Nothing has to leave your control" is true only for self-hosted servers. For open weights on a third-party host, the host sees prompts and code.
   - Record the host, headquarters, datacenter, declared quantization and retention policy for every model call, and flag calls that went somewhere the policy did not allow.
   - This is cheap. OpenRouter already exposes the metadata, and no harness records it per step (1.1).
3. **Per-repository model-route policy.** For example: "this repository's code may reach only our vLLM cluster or attested hosts in the EU at fp8 or better."
   - Enforce it at the sidecar and keep it on the ledger as evidence.
   - It fits Abanca's hybrid pattern and the origin and export screens (1.4); the over-weight of IT services and systems integrators doing client work (section 3); and the absence of any such control in Cline's or Kilo's enterprise materials.
4. **Verification of attested hosts as the premium tier, built on existing pieces.**
   - Interoperate with ACI receipts (Phala), NEAR signatures and Tinfoil attestation, and consider building on or with teep rather than writing a new verifier.
   - Differentiate on what teep and the providers lack:
     - retention beyond the provider's window, under team keys;
     - offline re-verification;
     - links from each receipt to the session and change set.
5. **Go-to-market adjustments.**
   - The self-serve segment is Europe-tilted (Germany 10%), small-firm and IT-services-heavy, and holds purchase authority (21.6% make the final decision).
   - Enterprise buyers of open harnesses are platform and DevX teams and CIOs (Cline; Anaconda/Kilo), and they route to Bedrock, Vertex and Azure. Support those as approved routes, with observed-tier records.
   - Reserve the self-hosted model-server sidecar and the "signed" tier for the 1.4 buyers: defense, EU banks and integrators, manufacturers.

### Gaps
- No evidence yet that any buyer will pay for host-policy enforcement or a receipt archive.
- Whether OpenRouter or the harness gateways (Kilo Gateway, OpenCode Zen) will ship native attestation enforcement and receipt export is unknown. That would shrink direction 4.
