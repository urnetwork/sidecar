## 1. How do public SOC 3 reports classify logging and SIEM, identity, key managers, remote access (ZTNA), compliance tools and encrypted storage?

### Takeaway
- **Across 8 SOC 3 reports (periods 2019–2025), carve-outs are mostly cloud infrastructure.**
- **Logging, monitoring and SIEM tools are usually vendors.** Of 6 reports that name a third-party logging, monitoring or SIEM service, 5 list it only as a tool. Only KnowBe4 (2020–21) carved out Datadog.
- **Identity and access-management services are carved out more often:**
  - Okta in 3 of the 5 reports that name it;
  - Auth0 in Matillion's;
  - Opal, an access-review and provisioning service, in Figma's.
- **Zscaler (remote access to production) and Vanta (compliance) appear only as tools.**
- **Key managers sit inside carved-out cloud providers.**
- **No report in the sample names an encrypted backup store or a zero-knowledge vault.**
- **The same kind of vendor is carved out by one company and listed as a tool by another,** as the Guide's "judgment" language (§2) predicts.

### Inferences
- **The pattern fits the control-dependency test in §2:**
  - services that perform an access control themselves (Okta, Auth0, Opal) are carved out more often;
  - services whose output the company reviews itself (Datadog, Panther, Elastic, PagerDuty) are usually tools;
  - cloud hosts are always carved out, because their physical and environmental controls cannot be replaced.
- **URnetwork's roles map onto that pattern:**
  - **Ciphertext store and relay:** resembles the "tool" pattern.
  - **Hosted relay that enforces egress grants, or a hosted kill switch:** resembles Okta and Opal (carved out) more than Datadog.
  - **But Databricks lists Zscaler as a tool,** although it "control[s] remote access to the production systems". So even a hosted enforcement point is not certain to be carved out.
- **Classification of a given vendor type is not settled practice.** It depends on the company's design and its auditor's judgment, so pitch4 cannot promise a classification. It can only design for one.

### Gaps
- No SOC 3 in the sample names Backblaze, Wasabi, Tresorit, 1Password, Keeper, Splunk Cloud or Sumo Logic, or names Cloudflare as a customer's vendor. Searches for such PDFs found none.
- Full SOC 2 reports, which carry the detailed CSOCs, are under NDA.
- I found no trust-center page that lists subservice organizations separately from subprocessors.
- The auditors of the Zapier, Figma, Databricks and People Data Labs reports were not identified in the extracted text.

## 2. Where do CPA firms draw the line, quoting the AICPA SOC 2 Guide, especially for logging platforms, zero-knowledge storage and compliance tools?

### Takeaway
- **The SOC 2 Guide's definition.** As Larson & Co. quotes it, a vendor is a subservice organization "only if" two things apply:
  - its services are "likely to be relevant" to report users' understanding of the system;
  - its controls are "necessary, in combination with" the service organization's controls.
- **All the firms read turn on reliance.** If the service organization performs the control itself on the vendor's output, with enough precision, the vendor stays a vendor.
- **Two firm examples map directly onto the sidecar:**
  - **Schneider Downs:** a log-monitoring vendor is a subservice organization if it detects suspicious activity, but a plain vendor if the company reviews the vendor's summary reports itself.
  - **Larson** (citing the AICPA guides): a capacity-monitoring vendor stops being a subservice organization only if the company independently monitors and reviews the projections. "The service auditor would need to use judgment to determine whether the review controls were precise enough". A SaaS tool that monitors configuration settings, with services attached, is a subservice organization.
- **A contract can tip the classification.** Schneider Downs adds that a contract stipulating that the vendor performs controls points to subservice status.
- **No firm text addresses zero-knowledge or ciphertext-only storage.** The only standards-body text on such a provider is PCI SSC FAQ 1233, which lets a provider that stores only data encrypted by another entity treat it as out of scope "if the TPSP has no access to the decryption keys or to the clear-text data".

### Cited Findings
**AICPA definitions (as published or quoted)**
- **2017 TSC glossary** [verbatim] — [TSC 2017, March 2020 version (Wayback)](https://web.archive.org/web/20230905175109/https://us.aicpa.org/content/dam/aicpa/interestareas/frc/assuranceadvisoryservices/downloadabledocuments/trust-services-criteria-2020.pdf): "vendor (or supplier). An individual or business (and its employees) that is engaged to provide goods or services to the entity. Depending on the services provided (for example, if the vendor operates certain controls on behalf of the entity that are necessary to achieve the entity's objectives), it also might be a service provider."
- **The SOC 2 Guide, as quoted by Larson & Company** [verbatim; "[emphasis added]" markers removed] — [Larson, "Is My Vendor a Subservice Organization?", Andrew Wan, CPA, CFE, Audit Partner; 9 Feb 2024](https://larsco.com/blog/is-my-vendor-a-subservice-organization)
  - "In this guide, a vendor is considered a subservice organization only if the following apply: The services provided by the vendor are likely to be relevant to report users' understanding of the service organization's system as it relates to the applicable trust services criteria. Controls at the vendor are necessary, in combination with the service organization's controls, to provide reasonable assurance that the service organization's service commitments and system requirements are achieved based on the applicable trust services criteria."

**Larson's examples** [verbatim]
- **Backup power maintenance (SOC 2): not a subservice organization.** "Service Organization personnel participate in post-maintenance testing used to verify the backup power system is working as intended, which serves as a primary control. In this instance, ABC Vendor's controls are not necessary…"
- **Capacity monitoring (SOC 2): a subservice organization, unless the company reviews independently.** "However, if the Service Organization were to independently perform high-level capacity monitoring activities and review the future capacity demands projected by XYZ Vendor for appropriateness, XYZ Vendor might not be considered a Subservice Organization… The service auditor would need to use judgment to determine whether the review controls were precise enough that the vendor controls would not be necessary."
- **Monitoring of configuration settings (SOC 2): a subservice organization.** "A Service Organization purchases from JKL Vendor a tool to monitor and report on the status of configuration settings that affect the operation of control activities. JKL Vendor also provides services around the use of that tool through a software-as-a-service (SaaS) model." Then: "management has effectively outsourced monitoring of the configuration settings to JKL Vendor… JKL Vendor would be considered a Subservice Organization." The page continues: "if JKL Vendor does not provide additional services (only provided the software and no additional services) then there is an argument the Company" (the sentence is cut off on the page).
- **Software development (SOC 1): not a subservice organization,** "because the Service Organization's controls alone are sufficient".
- **General rule.** "if the Service Organization's controls alone are sufficient … management may conclude that the entity is not a Subservice Organization. In these circumstances, management of the Service Organization would not need to, but may, indicate in its description of the Service Organization's system and in management's assertion that it uses the services of another entity."

**Schneider Downs, "SOC Reporting: Vendor or Subservice Organization?"** (Timothy M. Wolfgang; published 12 Jun 2018, modified 13 Dec 2023) [verbatim] — [Schneider Downs](https://schneiderdowns.com/our-thoughts-on/soc-reporting-vendor-subservice-organization/)
- "a vendor's controls are not necessary for the service organization to meet the SOC 1 objectives or SOC 2 criteria, while a subservice organization's controls are likely to be necessary".
- A vendor is likely a subservice organization if:
  - "user entities' understanding of the service organization's system requires the services provided by the vendor to be included in the service organization's system description; and"
  - "controls at the vendor are necessary, in combination with the service organization's controls…; or"
  - "A service organization's contract with the vendor stipulates that the vendor perform certain controls to address risks related to the vendor's service."
- **Logging example:** "consider a vendor that monitors a service organization's IT logs for events that could indicate unauthorized activities. If the vendor is responsible for analyzing the logs for notable activities and alerting the service organization to suspicious activities, … the vendor would be a subservice organization because the vendor is performing the control to monitor the logs. The same vendor would not be considered a subservice organization if the service organization was reviewing summary reports of logged events generated by the vendor, since the service organization would be responsible for monitoring the reports and would not be relying on the vendor for identifying suspicious activity."
- **Who decides:** "the determination is ultimately the responsibility of the service organization's management."
- **A companion page** (10 Apr 2024): the vendor is "'upgraded' to a subservice organization" when its controls are necessary; "The increased reliance on the vendor's controls is what classifies it as a subservice organization" — [Schneider Downs terminology](https://schneiderdowns.com/our-thoughts-on/soc-2-terminology-and-definitions/)

**Moore Colson, "SOC Reporting: Exploring the Definition of Vendors vs. Subservice Organizations"** (29 Jul 2021) [verbatim] — [Moore Colson](https://moorecolson.com/news-insights/soc-reporting-exploring-the-definition-of-vendors-vs-subservice-organizations/)
- Payroll example: if the vendor "provides summary reports of calculations and deductions for Jack's Fast Books to review and approve, and Jack's Fast Books maintains responsibility for final sign-off", then "Payroll ABC would be classified as a vendor only and not a subservice organization , because Jack's Fast Books is not relying on Payroll ABC for the performance of Jack's Fast Books' key controls".

**Schellman, "What is the Difference between Vendors & Subservice Organizations?"** (published 7 Jul 2022, modified 13 Feb 2026; author shown as "Schellman") [verbatim] — [Schellman](https://www.schellman.com/blog/soc-examinations/subservice-organizations-vs-vendors-within-soc)

### Inferences
- **Case (a), ciphertext only with customer keys, a customer replica and a customer-run verifier: most likely an ordinary vendor under the Guide's two-prong test.**
  - **Confidentiality** is protected by the customer's encryption.
  - **Integrity** is checked by the customer's verifier.
  - **Availability and retention** rest on the customer's replica.
  - So URnetwork's controls are not "necessary, in combination with" the customer's. That is the "software development" and "summary reports" pattern, where "the Service Organization's controls alone are sufficient".
  - **What the auditor then tests** is the customer's verification control and its CC9.2 vendor assessment.
  - **Inclusion in the description is optional:** management "would not need to, but may" mention the vendor.
- **The condition is precision.** Under the capacity-monitoring example, the auditor judges whether the customer's review is "precise enough". A verifier that checks every entry at every sync, alerts on failure and reconciles counts to the forge is precise. A quarterly manual spot check may not be.
- **Case (b), hosted enforcement, witness or retention the customer relies on: most likely a subservice organization.**
  - This is the configuration-monitoring SaaS pattern and the log-monitoring-vendor pattern: the vendor performs the control.
  - A contract term where URnetwork promises to perform the customer's controls (enforce egress, retain for N years, hold) would also tip classification under Schneider Downs' third point.
- **Hybrid exits.** An enforcement point becomes a vendor role again if the customer independently enforces or verifies the same rule. Examples: egress enforced at customer-run exits, with the hosted relay only forwarding; or witness signatures from a quorum that includes non-URnetwork witnesses.
- **PCI FAQ 1233 is the closest official precedent for "hosted blind".** It is PCI-only and hedged ("may be able to"). Pitch4 can cite it for PCI buyers, but must not claim it applies to SOC 2 or HITRUST.

### Gaps
- The AICPA SOC 2 Guide's own paragraphs, and DC 200's implementation guidance on vendors, were not read.
- No CPA-firm text addresses a ciphertext-only host. Only interviews can say whether auditors accept customer-held keys plus a verifier as "controls alone are sufficient".

## 4. HITRUST r2: is a provider that holds only customer-encrypted data an in-scope service provider, and under which scope component?

### Takeaway
- **Handbook v1.2 never uses encryption to decide scope.** Two tests decide instead:
  1. whether a third party supports a primary or secondary scope component (7.2.28);
  2. which party "manages performance" of each requirement statement (inheritance scoring).
- **In r2, every in-scope service provider must be covered by one of four strategies:**
  - inheritance from a HITRUST-certified provider;
  - reliance on a third-party audit report;
  - reliance on the assessed entity's own testing;
  - direct testing by the assessor.
- **Without coverage, the requirement statements are scored non-compliant (12.1.2),** and r2 must use the inclusive method.
- **Which component the hosted ledger falls under.** It fits "Other Supporting Tools" more naturally: the list names SIEM, source code repositories, change management tools, back-up tools and media, remote access solutions, password vaults and encryption software. "Data Storage Tools" apply only when "sensitive information from the primary scope components is stored". AAA platforms are those used "to authenticate with and/or access a primary scope component".
- **Case (a):** a ciphertext-only host whose every relevant control is performed by the customer manages performance of nothing. Its share would be 0%, and the customer's controls carry the score (inference).
- **Case (b):** a hosted relay that enforces egress, a witness, or server-side retention manages performance of requirement statements. It then needs URnetwork's HITRUST certification, a SOC 2 Type II, or the assessor testing URnetwork directly.

- **Case (a) under r2: URnetwork is probably still an "in-scope third party" to be identified in planning (12.1.1).** The hosted ledger supports change-management and logging requirements for in-scope systems, under 7.2.21.
- **But URnetwork would "manage performance" of none of those requirement statements** if the customer:
  - holds the keys (encryption software);
  - verifies integrity (log protection);
  - keeps the authoritative replica (backup and retention);
  - enforces egress at its own exits (remote access, transmission).

- **Payload minimization shapes the HITRUST answer.** If the ledger holds only hashes and metadata, the hosted store likely escapes "Data Storage Tools" (7.2.20). Raw sessions that may contain data copied from production systems are the risk.
- **Case (b) under r2: URnetwork shares performance of requirement statements.** For example: network access or remote access for a hosted egress relay; audit-log protection for a witness; retention for server-side holds.
  - **URnetwork can be covered in four ways:**
    1. Inheritance, which needs URnetwork to hold its own HITRUST e1, i1 or r2 certification and publish it in MyCSF, with the requirement statements in its assessment.

    3. Direct testing by the customer's assessor at URnetwork.
    4. The customer's own internal testing of URnetwork.
  - **Without one of these, the shared requirement statements score "not compliant".** This is the hard gate for hosted enforcement sold to HITRUST r2 buyers.
- **i1 and e1 buyers can carve URnetwork out** (requirement statements become N/A). r2 buyers may be able to carve out a provider inside the AI add-on (L3-3.01).

## 5. Do Vanta, Drata, Secureframe and their audit partners treat custom-test and custom-integration data as system-generated evidence?

### Takeaway
- **No platform help page or audit-partner text found says so.**
- **Vanta's auditor-facing help pages don't mention custom integrations or custom tests.** They say auditors "need to retain and download information to support their testing", and that some controls still need manual documents.
- **Vanta's product page markets auditor access to "test source data".** That is a vendor claim.
- **BARR says custom tests give auditors "more relevant information".** It does not say they earn reliance.
