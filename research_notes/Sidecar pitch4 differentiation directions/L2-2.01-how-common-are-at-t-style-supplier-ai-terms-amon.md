- **Binding**: a contract term, supplier requirement or code that suppliers sign up to. **Template**: a model clause that buyers may adopt. **Vendor**: marketing or a vendor's own data.

**The five AT&T terms tested** (AT&T Supplier AI Requirements, SAIR v2.1, October 2025; full text in L1-1.08):
1. a per-release SBOM naming the AI tool (C.3.c), plus "AI Generated" labels (C.3.e);
2. approval of each AI use (C.5.c, "in each instance");
3. named AI subcontractors with 30 days' notice (C.5.d);
4. no use of client data to "Model", a term that covers training and also "prompt/context learning, RAG or vector storage" and "data embedding" (C.1.b);
5. permanent deletion of client data after use, keeping only pseudonymized "metadata or audit logs … for traceability" under an escrowed mapping key (C.1.c–d).

**Method:**
- **Coverage:** I looked for public supplier documents (supplier portals, codes of conduct, purchase-order terms, AI addenda, data-protection schedules) from 43 large buyers:
  - telcos (6), banks and asset managers (8), insurers and brokers (8), big tech (7), pharma (6), defense primes (6), plus Cushman & Wakefield and BCG.
- **Depth:** I opened the primary text for 19 of them. For the rest, at least one targeted web search found no public supplier AI terms.
- **Limits:** for many buyers that was a single search, and most enterprise supplier terms sit in portals and MSAs that are not public. Counts below are therefore a **lower bound on prevalence among public documents**, not a market rate.

## 1. How common are the five AT&T-style terms among large enterprise buyers?

### Takeaway
- **The pattern.** AT&T is not alone. In 2024–2026 a wave of large buyers published binding terms that reach a supplier's *use* of AI to deliver work, which covers coding agents used to build deliverables. Of the 43 buyers I searched for, I found and read such terms at **13**:
  - **Telcos:** AT&T, BT.
  - **Banks:** Citi, HSBC, Barclays.
  - **Defense primes:** Lockheed Martin, Northrop Grumman, L3Harris.
  - **Big tech:** Google.
  - **Insurers:** Gallagher (broker), Cigna (health insurer).
  - **Others:** Cushman & Wakefield, BCG (a consultancy acting as buyer).

  Fidelity International partly fits. The Australian government's DTA model clauses form a matching government template.
- **Which terms are common.** Across those 13, three of AT&T's terms are a **pattern**:
  - **Permission before AI use or before client data enters AI:** 8 of 13. Four of them want approval per instance, per use case, or per tool *and* use case.
  - **No training on client data:** 11 of 13.
  - **Human review of AI output:** 7 of 13.
- **Where AT&T is an outlier:**
  - **Per-release SBOM naming the AI tool:** AT&T only, 1 of 13. Labels on AI-generated deliverables appear at 3.
  - **Delete after use, keeping pseudonymized audit metadata:** AT&T only, 1 of 13.
  - **Named AI subcontractors:** uncommon, 3 of 13.
  - **Explicit ban on using client data for RAG or context:** AT&T only. However, six buyers' "no input without authorization" clauses and two buyers' "no merging or commingling" clauses reach the same behaviour.
- **Two related asks that are emerging:**
  - **No retention of client data inside the AI tool:** Google, Northrop and L3Harris, plus Fidelity.
  - **Keeping records of AI use** that can be shown on request: BT, Cushman & Wakefield, Northrop, Lockheed and Barclays, plus the DTA template.
- **The terms are getting stricter:**
  - Citi moved from *notify* (May 2025) to *prohibited unless agreed in the Schedule or Work Order* (September 2026).
  - Lockheed added an AI clause between its 2024 and 2025 CorpDocs.
  - AT&T added a default-deny AI rule to its security requirements (SISR v8.0, March 2026).
  - Barclays issued a dedicated AI SCO with agentic-AI safeguards (v16.0, October 2026).

#### Detail and dating, by sector

**Telcos**
- **AT&T added a second, default-deny rule in March 2026.** SISR v8.0, "March 2026" (PDF 4 Mar 2026), §3.6 reads: "Supplier Entities must not use In-Scope Information in any Artificial Intelligence unless specifically authorized elsewhere (e.g., the Deliverables or a Statement of Work) in the Agreement."
  - Its AI definition includes "the ability to understand, process, and generate new and original content, such as text, images, video, audio materials, or software code … or the ability to act with autonomy similar to human organizational agents." — [AT&T SISR v8.0](https://globalsupplychain.att.com/misc/ATT_SISR_Appx.pdf) [opened]
- **AT&T's policy page still lists SAIR.** On 1 Nov 2026 the page lists the "AT&T Supplier Artificial Intelligence Requirements" as its only AI document, with no version shown. — [AT&T Supplier Policies](https://attsuppliers.com/Home/Policies) [opened via fetch tool]
- **BT Supplier Use of AI Policy, v1.0.** The issue date reads "1/9/2024"; the footer says "v1.0 10/07/2024"; the PDF is dated 12 Sep 2024. — [BT policy](https://www.bt.com/content/dam/bt-plc/assets/documents/selling-to-bt/supplier-use-of-ai-policy.pdf) [opened]

## 2. How do suppliers comply with these terms today?

### Takeaway
Compliance today is **contractual and manual**. I found no product that turns a client's AI clause into enforcement or evidence. The routes visible in primary texts are:
1. **Classifying out of scope.** Microsoft lets suppliers that merely use AI answer "no".
2. **Contract-level authorization.** The AI use is written into the Schedule, Work Order, SOW or Call-Off: Citi, AT&T SISR, Lockheed, HSBC.
3. **Asking for approval by email, per use case:** Gallagher's `AIUseCases@ajg.com`.
4. **Using the buyer's own provisioned AI tools under its guardrails:** BT.
5. **Negotiated carve-outs for consultancies:** C&W's §8.4 excuses consultancies from warranting the traceability of third-party tools they don't control.

Practitioner guides (vendors and consultants) recommend:
- an approved-tool list per client;
- flowing terms down to freelancers;
- a tool log plus vendor assurances about no training and no retention.

**First-hand supplier voice is absent from what I could reach.** Two Hacker News queries over 2025–2026, a LinkedIn-restricted search and an SEC-filings search found **0** first-hand accounts of a delivery lead complying with a client AI clause.

### Inferences
- **How compliance works today.** Granularity varies across buyers: per instance (AT&T), per use case (Gallagher), per tool *and* use case (C&W), per SOW (Citi, AT&T SISR, Lockheed, HSBC), and per model change (DTA, Barclays). A supplier with several such clients therefore keeps a different permission set for each engagement. Today that permission set apparently lives in contracts, email threads and people's heads; there is no evidence it lives in tooling.
- **The evidence buyers actually request is "records on request".** That is a population a supplier must be able to produce *later*, across every tool its people and subcontractors used. Without a cross-harness record, a supplier can only reconstruct it from memory, invoices and commit trailers. This matches the auditor-sampling gap in L1-1.08.
- **BT's route can create demand for a record that spans organizations.** BT supplies its own GenAI tools to supplier developers. That pushes the record toward the buyer's environment and away from the supplier's. A record that both sides can read, where the supplier writes and the client verifies, would fit this route. This is inference only; I have no BT evidence of demand for it.
- **Silence is not proof of easy compliance.** The absence of first-hand complaints may reflect where I could search (no Reddit, no gated supplier forums), or it may reflect low enforcement. I cannot tell which.

## 3. What does this mean for pitch4's differentiation and product offering?

### Takeaway
- **The binding trigger is real.** Banks, defense primes, big tech and services buyers impose AT&T-style *gates*: no AI without authorization, no training or input, human review, and records of which tools were used.
- **The lead offering should match that pattern:**
  - a per-engagement AI policy that the sidecar enforces;
  - approval receipts;
  - an inventory of tools and models actually used;
  - records available on request;
  - an attestation of non-use where AI is barred.
- **The per-release AI SBOM is not the lead.** Only AT&T asks for it, so it belongs in the packet as an export.
- **Retention needs splitting.** Clients are split between "keep records of AI use" (BT, C&W, Northrop, Lockheed, Barclays, DTA) and "don't retain client data in the AI tool" (AT&T, Google, Northrop, L3Harris, Fidelity). The design that satisfies both is *keep the signed metadata record; make session content retention a client-level setting, and by default do not persist content for work under a no-retention clause*.
- **A contractual pull toward private model access.** Northrop and L3Harris bar GenAI that third parties, including the AI provider, can access or that keeps copies. That gives pitch4's "agents reach only the team's own inference servers" a binding contractual reason in the defense supply chain, if the model server itself provably keeps no content.

### Inferences
- **Direction A. Make "contract-as-policy" per client engagement the core paid feature.**
  - **What it is:** an engagement record lists the approved harnesses, models and providers, the use cases, the data classes allowed as input, and the retention mode. Each entry points to its authority: SOW clause, Work Order, email approval ID, or the Gallagher-style use-case approval.
  - **Enforcement:** the sidecar refuses agent runs that match no approval in force. That mirrors the contracts' default deny (8 of 13 buyers).
  - **Output:** an approval receipt and inventory entry for every run.
  - **Why it is different:** GRC tools (Vanta's GrantGuard) audit settings, and attribution tools (git-ai, Agent Trace, aiprov) label commits. Neither ties runs to a *client's* contract terms.
- **Direction B. Add three engagement modes:**
  1. **"AI prohibited":** the default for clients like HSBC under the Framework Terms, Lockheed without a contract requirement, or Citi without a Work Order. The sidecar blocks model access for repositories tagged to the client and signs an **attestation of non-use**. This is a new artifact. It proves only what passed through the sidecar, and should be pitched with that caveat.
  2. **"AI permitted with approvals":** for AT&T, Gallagher, C&W and L3Harris. It produces receipts, labels, and the AT&T-style SBOM as an export.
  3. **"Private models only, no content retained":** for Northrop and L3Harris. Agents reach only self-hosted inference. The model-server sidecar signs a serving receipt that also attests that no prompt or output content was logged.
- **Direction C. Change the vault promise from "Archived instead of deleted" to "keep the record; the client decides the content".**
  - **Default for client-tagged work:** session content is not persisted, or is purged when the engagement closes. Signed, pseudonymized metadata is kept in the ledger, with the mapping key held by the team and escrowed (AT&T C.1.d).
  - **Why "never lose a session" breaks for client work:**
    - Northrop's "not stored, logged, cached" and L3Harris's "persistent copy" clauses could be read to cover a harness's own transcript store. Claude Code, for example, keeps transcripts locally. This is unverified; see open question 2.
    - "Never lose a session" therefore holds only for non-client work. For client work the promise becomes "never lose the *record*".
- **Direction D. Enforce a boundary between clients' context.**
  - Tag every context artifact with its client engagement: handoff notes, agent memory, team context files, embeddings and indexes.
  - Block retrieval across engagements, and export evidence that clients were kept separate.
  - Citi's no-merge clause, Gallagher's no-commingling clause and AT&T's RAG definition make shared team context a *contract risk* for suppliers with several clients. The fix is concrete and is not offered by coordination tools.
- **Direction E. Re-target the beachheads.**
  - **Strongest binding triggers:**
    - subcontractors to defense primes (Lockheed, Northrop and L3Harris clauses, which flow down);
    - bank suppliers (Citi, HSBC, Barclays);
    - services firms' suppliers (C&W, Gallagher);
    - consultancies that receive these terms (BCG's own supplier code shows consultancies pass AI terms down to their subcontractors).
  - **Weaker than assumed:** "Microsoft suppliers" is a weaker trigger for coding-agent records, because SSPA exempts tool users.
  - **Barclays-type agentic safeguards** map onto pitch4's existing pieces:
    - kill switch → lease revocation;
    - containment across steps → claims and leases plus approved reach;
    - post-action review → ledger replay.
- **Confidence:**
  - Medium-high that approvals, inventory and records lead; this rests on several buyers.
  - Medium that per-engagement modes are differentiated; I found no competing tool, but searched mainly the open web.
  - Low-medium that defense supply-chain clauses create real demand for private models; that depends on how primes read "accessible to the AI System Provider", which I found no guidance on.

### Gaps
- No buyer has said whether a cryptographically signed ledger export would be accepted in place of email approvals or ad hoc "records on request".
- I found no reading by a prime or bank of whether hosted APIs with zero data retention satisfy "not accessible to … the AI System Provider" (Northrop) or "accessible only by Buyer and Seller" (L3Harris).
- I found no evidence of buyers auditing or penalizing suppliers under these clauses, so urgency is unknown.
- How many suppliers hold several clients with conflicting AI terms (the core of Direction A) is unmeasured.
