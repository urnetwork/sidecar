## 1. What sets a vendor's tier, and does holding only ciphertext, or shipping software the customer self-hosts, lower it?

### Takeaway
Every tiering method I could read scores a vendor on a few attributes and tiers it by the worst one:
- what data it can touch;
- what systems it can reach;
- how critical it is to operations;
- which regulations apply.

None of them has an attribute for "the vendor holds no keys" or "the vendor has no plaintext access". Regulators also keep a host without keys inside the relationship:
- HHS says a cloud provider that stores only encrypted health data, without the key, is still a HIPAA business associate.
- DORA's register covers software licences as well as cloud services.

Blind hosting therefore lowers residual risk and shortens the data-handling part of a review, but it does not by itself lower the sidecar's inherent tier. The process on developer machines, the per-agent network paths and the gate on model access score high on system access and criticality, wherever the ciphertext sits. Self-hosting changes the relationship from service provider to software supplier, which moves the review to product and supply-chain security rather than removing it.

### Cited Findings
**Standards and frameworks**
- **NIST SP 800-161r1 (update 1)** groups suppliers by importance, not by data: "an assessment of these suppliers might result in groupings of multiple categories (e.g., "strategic/innovative," "mission-critical," "sustaining," or "standard/non-essential")". This "helps to focus attention and assign priority to those critical suppliers of the most strategic or operational importance to the enterprise and its mission and business processes." — [NIST SP 800-161r1-upd1 PDF](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/SpecialPublications/NIST.SP.800-161r1-upd1.pdf) [fetched]
  - Under "sustaining practices": "Use confidence-building mechanisms, such as third-party assessment surveys, on-site visits, and formal certifications (e.g., ISO 27001, IEC 62443-4-1) to assess critical supplier security capabilities and practices." — same source.
  - Access is a separate axis: "Continuous monitoring activities should be commensurate with the contractor's level of access to sensitive, classified, or regulated information". Service providers in scope include "those providers that require physical or logical access to ICT/OT". — same source.

**Third-party-risk platforms (vendor content describing their own methods)**
- **UpGuard** [fetched; vendor content, undated] — [UpGuard, "What is vendor tiering"](https://www.upguard.com/blog/what-is-vendor-tiering.md):
  - Criteria: "Tier vendors by security risk using five observable criteria: data access, operational continuity, regulatory mandates, strategic exposure, and security resiliency."
  - Intake: "A vendor tiering questionnaire captures observable exposure at intake (data types handled, system access, operational criticality, and regulatory flags) and scores those answers into a tier."
  - Rule of thumb: "Score what the vendor can touch and how badly operations fail if it drops."
  - Example tiers:
    - Tier One, "Critical": an "Identity and access management provider", driven by "Systemic availability and crown-jewel data path".
    - Tier Two, "High": a "Customer support ticketing platform", driven by "Sensitive customer data at volume and regulatory exposure".
  - Demotion: "When a critical relationship loses privileged access or exits a regulated workflow, demote it."
- **ProcessUnity:** the requester "answers a series of questions related to the service to be onboarded, including the importance of the vendor to ongoing business operations and the nature of the data being shared. The inherent risk score, and resulting tier placement, informs the scope and frequency" of review. — [ProcessUnity, Inherent Risk](https://www.processunity.com/third-party-risk-management-platform/inherent-risk/) [fetched; vendor content]
- **Vanta (help center):** "Inherent risk represents the risk a vendor poses before any mitigating controls are considered... based on factors such as data sensitivity, access level, and business criticality." **Regulators: a host without keys stays in scope, with lighter duties**
- **HHS (HIPAA), Guidance on HIPAA & Cloud Computing.** A CSP that stores ePHI is a business associate, and "This is true even if the CSP processes or stores only encrypted ePHI and lacks an encryption key for the data. Lacking an encryption key does not exempt a CSP from business associate status and obligations under the HIPAA Rules." — [HHS](https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/special-topics/health-information-technology/cloud-computing/index.html), read via the [Wayback snapshot of 31 Dec 2025](https://web.archive.org/web/20251231073222/https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/special-topics/health-information-technology/cloud-computing/index.html) [fetched; hhs.gov returned 403]
  - Duties scale down: "a CSP providing no-view services is not exempt from any otherwise applicable requirements of the HIPAA Rules. However, the requirements of the Rules are flexible and scalable to take into account the no-view nature of the services". — same source.
  - Controls are split: "where only the customer controls who is able to view the ePHI maintained by the CSP, certain access controls, such as authentication or unique user identification, may be the responsibility of the customer, while others, such as encryption, may be the responsibility of the CSP". — same source.
  - Breach safe harbor: if breached ePHI "is encrypted consistent with the HIPAA standards... the incident falls within the breach 'safe harbor' and the CSP business associate is not required to report the incident". — same source.
  - A no-view CSP must still not misuse ePHI "by blocking or terminating access by the customer to the ePHI". — same source.
- **EDPB Recommendations 01/2020 on supplementary measures (v2.0, adopted 18 June 2021):** — [EDPB PDF](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/system/files/2021-06/edpb_recommendations_202001vo.2.0_supplementarymeasurestransferstools_en.pdf) [fetched]
  - **Use Case 1** (hosting that does not need data in the clear): encryption is "an effective supplementary measure" if, among other conditions, "the keys are retained solely under the control of the data exporter, or by an entity trusted by the exporter in the EEA or under a jurisdiction offering an essentially equivalent level of protection" (para. 84).
  - **Use Case 6** (cloud providers "which require access to data in the clear"): if the provider "needs access to the data in the clear in order to execute the task assigned" and third-country law goes beyond what is necessary, "the EDPB is, considering the current state of the art, incapable of envisioning an effective technical measure" (para. 94).
  - Para. 95: "transport encryption and data-at-rest encryption even taken together, do not constitute a supplementary measure that ensures an essentially equivalent level of protection if the data importer is in possession of the cryptographic keys."
- **CJEU, EDPS v SRB (C-413/23 P, 4 Sep 2025):** pseudonymised data can be personal data for the controller but not for a recipient who cannot re-identify it, judged by "means reasonably likely to be used". — [Bird & Bird](https://cm.twobirds.com/en/insights/2025/eu-the-srb-decision-a-new-era-for-personal-data-and-data-processing-agreements), [Clifford Chance](https://www.cliffordchance.com/insights/resources/blogs/talking-tech/en/articles/2025/09/pseudonymized-data-after-edps-v-srb.html) [search summary; secondary]
  - The commentary says it is unresolved whether this changes Article 28 processor terms.
  - The ruling concerns pseudonymisation, not encryption.
- **DORA register of information:** financial entities must list "all ICT agreements, including cloud services, software licences and IT support arrangements", mapped to "19 standardised service categories (S01–S19)". — [BAHR (Norwegian law firm) newsletter, early 2026](https://bahr.no/newsletter/asset-management-compliance-dora-norwegian-asset-managers-must-report-their-ict-register-by-13-march-2026) [fetched; secondary]
  - A Copla guide says that whether something is in scope turns on an "ongoing basis" (support, maintenance, updates). — [Copla](https://support.copla.com/what-is-the-dora-register-of-information) [search summary; secondary]
  - I did not read the ITS annex itself; EUR-Lex returned a bot-check.
- **US federal:** CISA released the Secure Software Development Attestation Form on 11 Mar 2024, implementing the "requirement that producers of software used by the Federal Government attest to the adoption of secure development practices" (EO 14028, OMB M-22-18, M-23-16). — [CISA](https://www.cisa.gov/secure-software-attestation-form) [fetched]

**Practitioner voice on self-hosted purchases (HN thread [38534109](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=38534109), Dec 2023; 91 comments, 74 authors)** [fetched]
- toomuchtodo: "On-prem software is bought by orgs with low risk appetite or other compliance objectives that skew procurement towards running on prem." — [HN 38535120](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=38535120)
- poulsbohemian lists the friction: "Our security team scanned it with our chosen tools and you have to fix these things before we will deploy it." — [HN 38536864](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=38536864)

### Inferences
- [inference] **The tier is the worst of several attributes, and the sidecar's worst is not data.** The rubrics score data, system access, criticality and regulation. On system access, the sidecar:
  - runs beside agent harnesses on developer machines;
  - gives agents private IPs and port forwards;
  - gates which model servers agents reach.

  Those score "High" or "Critical" on system access ("Integration with production environments", "Read internal systems") whatever the hosted relay holds. Under a rule that takes the highest attribute, hosted blind does not move the overall tier.
- [inference] **Blind hosting moves residual risk and review length, not the inherent tier.** It removes or shrinks the data-handling sections:
  - who can read the data;
  - subprocessors with plaintext access;
  - breach notification (HIPAA safe harbor);
  - transfer analysis (EDPB Use Case 1).

  That is a real procurement advantage: fewer questions and a shorter DPA. It is not a different tier.
- [inference] **Splitting the engagement is the lever that could lower the hosted part's tier.** UpGuard demotes a relationship that "loses privileged access", and tiers attach to a relationship as described at intake. If the sidecar is presented as two engagements, the hosted part could be scored low on data access:
  1. Open-source software installed on endpoints, reviewed as a software supplier.
  2. A hosted relay and storage that holds only ciphertext.
- [inference] **Self-hosting changes who the vendor is, not whether it is reviewed.** BringYour becomes a software supplier, and the review moves to:
  - scanning the product (poulsbohemian);
  - SBOM and signed builds;
  - the update channel, telemetry and support access;
  - federal SSDF attestation.

  EU financial buyers would still record a licence with ongoing support in the DORA register. Whether it supports a critical or important function depends on how the team uses it.
- [inference] **HIPAA buyers will still ask for a BAA from a blind host.** Tresorit already signs BAAs despite zero-knowledge (section 2). If session or ledger content could contain PHI, pitch4 needs a no-view BAA template.

### Gaps
- I found no TPRM rubric or questionnaire item that scores "vendor holds no keys or plaintext". The default rubrics of Vanta, OneTrust and ProcessUnity, SIG 2026 scoping, CAIQ v4's key-management (CEK) items and HECVAT 4 were not readable (login, forms, or not found).
- I found no measured data on how often analysts lower the tier of E2EE or self-hosted vendors.
- GDPR Art. 34(3)(a), which exempts communication to data subjects when breached data is encrypted, was not fetched in this session.
- HITRUST expectations of health buyers were not researched.
- The ESAs' own Q&A on software licences under DORA was not found; the DORA scope points rely on a law firm and a vendor guide.

## 2. How do end-to-end-encrypted and self-hosted vendors present their attestations?

### Takeaway
Every E2EE vendor I checked pairs "we cannot read your data" with a full attestation set for its hosted service, never one instead of the other:
- **Tailscale:** SOC 2 Type II.
- **1Password:** SOC 2 Type 2 plus four ISO certifications and published penetration tests.
- **Bitwarden:** SOC 2 Type II, SOC 3, ISO 27001, HIPAA, and now FedRAMP in progress.
- **Tresorit:** ISO 27001, and it signs BAAs.

Self-managed products are sold without an attestation that covers the customer's deployment. GitLab's SOC 2 Type 2 and ISO 27001 cover only GitLab.com and GitLab Dedicated, yet most of its revenue is not SaaS (section 5).

On HN, no commenter said self-hosting or E2EE removed the need for SOC 2. At least eight vendor-side accounts presented them together.

### Inferences
- [inference] **"Hosted blind" works inside the review, not as an exemption from it.** The closest analogs (Tailscale's data plane, 1Password, Bitwarden, Tresorit) all hold SOC 2 and/or ISO 27001 for the hosted service. Their "we can't see it" statement answers the data questions; the attestation answers the operations questions:
  - access control;
  - change management;
  - availability;
  - incident response.
- [inference] **The self-managed precedent is strong but conditional.** GitLab sells mostly self-managed (about two-thirds of revenue; section 5) under attestations scoped to its SaaS. Self-managed buyers therefore buy without a deployment-scoped report. They probably rely on GitLab's company-level ISMS and on scanning the product, but that is unverified. A new vendor without any ISMS evidence has less to lean on, so the self-host route still needs supply-chain artifacts:
  - signed reproducible builds;
  - an SBOM;
  - a penetration-test letter;
  - a published protocol audit;
  - SSDF-style attestation.
- [inference] **Tailscale's metadata sentence is the template for pitch4's "lists all metadata our servers see".** That statement, plus 1Password's practice of publishing penetration-test reports, is what makes a blindness claim checkable.

### Gaps
- How GitLab self-managed customers' security teams actually review GitLab (SDLC questionnaire, ISO certificate, nothing) is undocumented in public sources I found.
- Mattermost's attestation scope (Cloud vs self-hosted) is unknown, because its trust center was not opened.
- Whether 1Password's and Bitwarden's SOC 2 scopes cover self-hosted Bitwarden or only the cloud service was not checked.

## 3. Which attestations close deals with teams of 5–150 engineers, and with regulated buyers?

### Takeaway
Of 11 distinct HN practitioners who said what buyers accept:
- **5** say a SOC 2 Type 1, a contractual promise to obtain one within about six months, or proof of being "in process" closes first deals.
- **7** say CISOs at larger and public companies expect Type II, and that it streamlines reviews. One person, x0x0, said both.
- ISO 27001 is rarely required in North America and comes after Type II for large or European enterprises.
- Attestations do not end questionnaires.

Regulated buyers put standards compliance first (ISC2: 77% overall, 84% in financial services) and add regime-specific paperwork:
- a BAA, even for a blind host;
- DORA register entries, LEIs and contract clauses;
- SSDF attestation for federal software.

A penetration test and CAIQ support a review but are not substitutes. In the comments I retrieved, no one described closing a regulated deal on them alone.

## 4. Do reviewers tell host-blind encryption apart from vendor-held CMEK, and will they pay more for it?

### Takeaway
Standards bodies and regulators draw exactly the line pitch4 needs:
- **CSA (Jan 2026):** BYOK/CMK "Does not mean the CSP cannot access your data", whereas holding your own key gives "High assurance that your CSP cannot decrypt".
- **CSA (2022):** customer-controlled key stores eliminate "a cloud service provider's ability to process customer data in plaintext".
- **EDPB:** encryption counts as a transfer safeguard only when the exporter keeps the keys, and at-rest encryption does not count when the importer holds them.

Large vendors sell the two as different products:
- **CMEK that keeps the vendor processing:** Microsoft Customer Key, used for eDiscovery and search indexing; Slack EKM, where Slack keeps working as normal.
- **Client-side encryption confined to top editions:** Google CSE in Enterprise Plus and similar editions; Microsoft DKE in E5-class licences.

But CSA says the terms "do not have an agreed-upon meaning", and Microsoft frames its hold-your-own-key product for about 5% of data because it breaks search, eDiscovery, DLP and co-authoring. I found no evidence that buyers pay a separate premium for blindness over CMEK: both sit in top tiers or paid add-ons. So reviewers can tell them apart when the question is put to them, and EU transfer rules reward blindness concretely, but money attaches to top-tier bundles, not to blindness alone.

### Cited Findings
**Cloud Security Alliance**
- **2022 paper:** CSA's Cloud Key Management Working Group released "Recommendations for Using a Customer Controlled Key Store" on 27 Sep 2022. Its listed benefits include "Elimination of a cloud service provider's ability to process customer data in plaintext". — [CSA press release](https://cloudsecurityalliance.org/press-releases/2022/09/27/cloud-security-alliance-offers-recommendations-for-using-customer-controlled-key-store) [fetched]
- **5 Jan 2026 blog, "Choosing the right key responsibility model":** — [CSA blog](https://cloudsecurityalliance.org/blog/2026/01/05/choosing-the-right-key-responsibility-model) [fetched]; companion infographic: [CSA Key Responsibility Models](https://cloudsecurityalliance.org/artifacts/key-responsibility-models) [fetched]
  - "Unfortunately, terms like BYOK, HYOK, and CMK do not have an agreed-upon meaning throughout the industry."
  - CMK: "the CSP typically stores and uses the keys within their KMS/HSM."
  - Cons of BYOK: "Does not mean the CSP cannot access your data—as long as you use keys within the CSP's KMS/HSM, the CSP can decrypt data".
  - HYOK: "The CSP has no involvement in key generation and no access to keys." Its pros are "High assurance that your CSP cannot decrypt certain data", "Fulfills legal or contractual requirements that demand keys stay under direct customer control", and "Best for multi-cloud or SaaS environments where you want a single, central KMS that all providers must talk to". Its cons include KMS overhead and "Latency and availability challenges".
  - Questions CSA tells teams to ask: "Is it acceptable for the CSP to have potential access to our keys (PMK, most CMK deployments)? For specific datasets, do we require a model where the provider cannot technically decrypt data?"

**EU regulators and sovereignty**
- EDPB Use Case 1 versus Use Case 6, and para. 95 (quoted in section 1). — [EDPB Recommendations 01/2020 v2.0](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/system/files/2021-06/edpb_recommendations_202001vo.2.0_supplementarymeasurestransferstools_en.pdf) [fetched]
- **EU Cloud Sovereignty Framework (European Commission, Oct 2025):**
  - Eight sovereignty objectives, each rated on a SEAL level. "The assessment considers both technical and organizational aspects, from where providers store encryption keys, to the origin of hardware components, to legal control over subsidiaries." — [heise](https://www.heise.de/en/news/Cloud-Sovereignty-Framework-How-the-EU-will-assess-cloud-sovereignty-10922224.html) [fetched; secondary]
  - Objective SOV-3 reportedly assesses whether the customer controls its keys and whether the provider can read decrypted content. A €180M procurement used the framework (awards reported in April 2026). — [ayedo](https://ayedo.de/en/posts/cloud-sovereignty-framework-seal-4/), [itsyndicate](https://itsyndicate.org/blog/cloud-sovereignty-framework-explained/), [Kiteworks](https://www.kiteworks.com/regulatory-compliance/european-digital-sovereignty-procurement/) [search summary; vendor blogs; the Commission PDF was not read; the SEAL scale and award dates conflict across sources]

**What vendor-held CMEK still lets the vendor do (vendor documentation)**
- **Microsoft Purview Customer Key:**
  - "You explicitly authorize Microsoft 365 to use your encryption keys to deliver value-added services like eDiscovery, anti-malware, anti-spam, and search indexing."
  - Encryption uses "both the keys you manage and the availability key that Microsoft protects".
  - "Revoking access to your keys triggers deletion of the availability key, resulting in cryptographic deletion of your data."

  Source: [Microsoft Learn, Customer Key overview](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/purview/customer-key-overview) [fetched].
- **Slack Enterprise Key Management** — [Slack Help, EKM](https://slack.com/help/articles/360019110974-Slack-Enterprise-Key-Management) [fetched]:
  - "available as a security add-on for the Enterprise Grid and Enterprise+ plans, and is included on the GovSlack plan."
  - "Org members can use Slack as normal, even if some data has restricted access."
  - Member profiles and IDs "may be encrypted at rest with keys generated and stored by Slack".
  - Search indexes for Slack Connect channels are "duplicated and encrypted with each customer's EKM keys". The service still builds search indexes, which requires plaintext.
- Anthropic's and Cursor's CMEK: the vendor decrypts while the key is enabled; Enterprise only (`L1-1.06`).

**Client-side, hold-your-own-key products (vendor documentation)**
- **Google Workspace Client-side encryption:**
  - "end-to-end encryption that Google servers and third parties can't decrypt... especially beneficial for organizations that store sensitive or regulated data, like intellectual property, healthcare records, or financial data."
  - "Supported editions for this feature: Frontline Plus; Enterprise Plus; Education Standard and Education Plus."

  Source: [Google Workspace Admin Help, About client-side encryption](https://support.google.com/a/answer/10741897) [fetched].
- **Microsoft Double Key Encryption** — [Microsoft Learn, DKE](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/purview/double-key-encryption) [fetched]:
  - "intended for your most sensitive data that is subject to the strictest protection requirements". It is described for the "Highly sensitive (about 5% of data)" class, with Microsoft-managed protection for the "Sensitive (about 15% of data)" class.

Microsoft 365 E5/A5/F5/G5 Information Protection and Governance" and EMS E5. — [Microsoft Purview service description](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/office365/servicedescriptions/microsoft-365-service-descriptions/microsoft-365-tenantlevel-services-licensing-guidance/microsoft-purview-service-description) [fetched]

**Practitioner signal**
- I found no HN comment, questionnaire text or survey in which a buyer chose or paid more for a vendor because it was host-blind rather than CMEK. The HN E2EE + SOC 2 queries returned 2 hits each, and neither discussed CMEK.

### Inferences
- [inference] **Reviewers can tell the difference when the vendor frames it in the authorities' terms.**
  - In CSA's own words, CMEK means "the CSP can decrypt data".
  - In EDPB terms, CMEK with server-side processing is Use Case 6 / para. 95, and host-blind is Use Case 1.
  - Under HIPAA, blind hosting maps to "no-view services".

  Because CSA admits the terms are ambiguous, a reviewer filling a form that asks "Do you support customer-managed keys?" will tick yes for Anthropic and Cursor too. Pitch4 must answer a different question: "Can the vendor technically decrypt?"
- [inference] **The money attaches to top-tier bundles, not to blindness.** Google and Microsoft put client-side keys in their top editions, and Slack sells EKM as an add-on to its top plans. That shows key control as a whole is monetised as a top-tier, regulated-buyer entitlement. I found no same-vendor price list that charges more for blind than for CMEK.

  Pitch4 should not expect buyers to pay extra merely for blindness. Blindness is the default architecture. Paid tiers should carry what is hard to do blind, which Microsoft's lost-feature list maps out:
  - a customer-run reader for search, eDiscovery and legal hold;
  - SIEM export from the admin side;
  - key escrow and recovery;
  - a dedicated region.
- [inference] **Where blindness has concrete regulatory value:**
  - EU buyers transferring to a US-linked host (EDPB);
  - EU sovereignty tenders that score key control (the Cloud Sovereignty Framework);
  - health buyers, through the breach safe harbor.

  These are the segments where "hosted blind" changes a decision, rather than just a review answer.
- [inference] **The testable one-liner.** Something like: "Our relay and storage never hold a key; with CMEK the vendor decrypts whenever the key is enabled (CSA); with us, neither a subpoena nor a breach of our servers yields plaintext." It should be backed by:
  - open relay code;
  - reproducible builds;
  - a key-transparency or verifier tool;
  - the published protocol audit.

### Gaps
- No buyer survey or analyst note (Gartner or Forrester, paywalled and not searched in depth) measures preference or willingness to pay for HYOK or E2EE over CMEK.
- The EU Cloud Sovereignty Framework PDF (SOV-3 criteria) was not read.
- How widely Google CSE or Microsoft DKE is actually deployed, by share of tenants or data, is unknown.
- I did not verify whether Google Cloud EKM, AWS XKS or Salesforce Shield decrypt in service memory under external keys.

## 6. Matrix of buyer type by requirement, and verdict on whether hosted blind changes the tier

### Takeaway
**Verdict on the tier:** hosted blind does not change the sidecar's inherent tier in any tiering method found, because the tier is set by the worst attribute, and the endpoint process and network grants score high.

**What hosted blind does change:**
1. It lowers residual risk and shortens the data-handling part of the review.
2. It is a recognised EU transfer safeguard (EDPB Use Case 1) that vendor-held CMEK is not.
3. It lightens HIPAA duties ("no-view", safe harbor) without removing the need for a BAA.

**What self-hosting changes:** it moves the vendor to software-supplier review. That is the route that can sell before SOC 2 Type II, except where a buyer's regime reaches licences and support (DORA) or software producers (federal SSDF).

**Timeline:** sign design partners on a Type 1 commitment; obtain SOC 2 Type 1 (Security only, scoped to the hosted relay, ledger and storage) when the hosted team layer goes live; open the Type II window immediately; defer ISO 27001.

### Cited Findings
The matrix below combines sources already cited in sections 1–5. Each cell names its basis. "Inference" marks cells without a direct source.

| Buyer type | Hosted (blind) route: what closes it | Self-hosted route: what closes it | What host-blind changes | Basis |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Engineering teams of 5–50, no third-party-risk function | Trust page, DPA, security.txt and VDP; Type 1 only if their own customers push them | Licence and support terms; open code | Answers "can the vendor read our code?" in the developer's own evaluation | ISC2: lower concern in smaller organizations (57% vs 82%); inference |
| Teams of 50–150 engineers running their own SOC 2 (Vanta- or Drata-style intake) | SOC 2 Type 1 or a contract clause "Type 1 within six months"; questionnaire; pen-test letter; Type II eases renewals | Supply-chain answers: SBOM, signed builds, vulnerability disclosure; a product scan before deployment | Data-access attribute drops for the hosted part; the overall tier stays at the highest attribute (system access) | Vanta and UpGuard rubrics; HN (mikeocool, tptacek, hobofan, x0x0); poulsbohemian |
| Consultancies serving enterprise clients | Driven by client terms (AT&T-style AI clauses, see `L1-1.08`); a third party holding client data may be barred | Easiest path: nothing leaves the firm | A blind host may satisfy "no third party with access", but this is untested | `L1-1.08`; inference |

### Inferences
- [inference] **Verdict, part 1 (tier): no change.** Hosted blind lowers one attribute (data access) for one component (hosted relay and storage). Tiering methods take the highest attribute, and the sidecar's highest is system access and criticality, from the endpoint process, per-agent network paths and model-access gate.
- [inference] **Verdict, part 2 (review): yes, a procurement advantage.** It is worth stating as such: fewer data-handling questions, a lighter DPA and BAA, breach safe harbor, and a recognised EU transfer safeguard. Against Anthropic's and Cursor's CMEK it is a real, citable difference (CSA; EDPB para. 95).
- [inference] **Verdict, part 3 (self-host): sells before Type II, with conditions.** A self-host Assured route can sell before SOC 2 Type II to buyers whose policies key on data holding, and to most 50–150-engineer teams. Regulated buyers still need supply-chain artifacts, and DORA buyers a register entry.
- [inference] **Timeline for pitch4:**
  1. Weeks 0–8: design partners sign with a "SOC 2 Type 1 (Security) within six months" clause, as mikeocool and tptacek describe.
  2. At hosted team-layer beta: auditor engaged and Type 1 scoped to the hosted relay, ledger and storage, with no consumer network in scope.
  3. Type 1 report, then an immediate 3–6-month Type II window (Brave used 3 months; `L1-1.09`).
  4. ISO 27001 only when EU enterprise deals require it.
  5. The self-hosted Assured install ships with an SSDF-style attestation, an SBOM, signed reproducible builds, a penetration-test letter and the published protocol audit, so regulated partners can start before Type II.

### Gaps
- The matrix rests mostly on regulatory text, vendor method pages and seller-side anecdotes. No buyer-side interviews or surveys of the 5–150-engineer segment were available.
- How an endpoint process with network reach is approved (IT, endpoint or MDM team, or third-party-risk team) was not researched. It may gate the first release more than SOC 2 does.
- The Microsoft SSPA/DPR row was not re-researched; see `L1-1.08`.
