# Sell the working network, not the toolkit

Y Combinator's advice through November 2026 is settled: sell the finished work rather than the tool, price it against the labor it replaces, and deliver it mostly with your own software so that margins climb toward software's. The position hardened over 15 months, from Jared Friedman's "automate the work entirely" (February 2025), through Aaron Epstein's "selling them the finished product at 100x the price" (February 2026), to Gustaf Alströmer's "they sell the service... they just do the work" (April 2026). Visiting Partner Charlie Warren's June 2026 playbook sets the rules: "You have to sell outcomes, not seats or tokens"; price per unit; cap the first pilots and distrust zero-margin ones; and automate delivery until services' roughly 30% margins pass 50%. By September 2026, companies that do the whole job made up more than a quarter of YC's batch, up from a tenth, and the Winter 2027 list adds no new services call. The outside evidence backs the budget argument and the growth: services spending runs about six times software's, and Sierra passed $400M ARR selling outcomes. It does not back most ways of billing for outcomes, which live almost entirely in customer support, are used by only 5 to 10% of vendors, get turned into fixed commitments by finance teams, and keep services margins wherever people do the delivery. For URnetwork, the finished work is a working private network on a team's own machines, and URnetwork's rules decide who can do that work. No URnetwork-run component may see plaintext and only a team's admins can admit a device, so the setup has to be URnetwork software running on the team's devices, with URnetwork's people seeing only what the team sends; that is YC's leverage model, reached by necessity. Team should therefore include that setup and its check rather than an engineer, because one hour of expert time at market rates is worth more than a three-seat Team's first year. Custom deployments should be fixed-scope packages: the first five capped, run by Brien, and paid half up front and half when the check passes. ur.io's Services page should lead with the team's network and keep Embed whole, with "global" meaning the team's own machines wherever they are, since URnetwork's v0 runs at one site with no dedicated addresses. The emails barely change, except that their buttons should land in the setup and the launch should count networks whose check passes. The open question is whether this audience has ever paid anyone for this work; one interview question and the first five quotes can answer it before any price is published.

### Warren's playbook prices against labor and treats variance as fatal

Charlie Warren's 11-minute Startup School talk is the most specific advice YC has published on the model, and it reads as a checklist ([YC library](https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Rk-how-to-build-an-ai-native-services-company)). Founders should pick markets where "the work is already outsourced and the customer cares about the final product, not how they got there," because "You're showing up where the budget already lives." They should build so that "The human is the interface of the customer, not the product," treat throughput and cycle time as product metrics, and accept that "Automating the process is the product." Variance is "the existential problem," because "Customers will fire you for variance faster than they will fire you for being a bit slower or a bit more expensive than the incumbents." The first pilots should be capped "to a small handful" and not standardized too early, and founders should "Be deeply suspicious of zero margin or negative margin pilots." Price is set against "the cost of labor, internal or outsourced." Per-unit pricing "is the cleanest," and outcome-based pricing "aligns incentives beautifully, but it can be harder for you to forecast." Cost-plus "caps your upside permanently," and "Straight line undercutting makes your work seem cheap." The economic bet is that traditional services firms "top out around 30% margins," while "AI operating leverage gets you closer to software margins, say 50% plus." His "Sam Altman test" asks whether better models make the service "stronger" or "commoditize you."

Other partners fill in the sales motion. Tom Blomfield wants pilots "much shorter," with "a paid commitment upfront," perhaps "a $10,000 or $20,000 charge on their corporate credit card," and with "time to first value" as the north star ([YC library](https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Mo-the-sales-playbook-for-founders)). At AI Startup School, Jake Heller contrasted "$20 per month versus $500 per contract," but he also reported that Casetext's customers chose "$6,000 per seat" for "predictable budgeting" ([YC library](https://www.ycombinator.com/library/c1-from-idea-to-650m-exit-lessons-in-building-ai-startups)). Bob McGrew measured forward-deployed work by "the value of the outcome you're delivering" and by "more and more product leverage against that outcome." A partner on the same episode advised getting "less custom per every customer" ([YC library](https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Mt-the-fde-playbook-for-ai-startups-with-bob-mcgrew)). By October 2025 the partners wanted "AI for AI FDE," because "the AI FDE can do it in minutes... It's actually just product." The Summer 2026 RFS took the next step: "coding agents have now gotten good enough to allow users to become their own forward deployed engineers" ([YC library](https://www.ycombinator.com/library/N0-unpopular-ideas-that-became-billion-dollar-businesses); [YC RFS](https://www.ycombinator.com/rfs)).

### YC worries about margins and consulting drift, and says nothing about networks

The partners' warnings take demand for granted and worry about economics. Harj Taggar said the last full-stack wave "forgot that you need gross margins" ([YC library](https://www.ycombinator.com/library/MQ-startup-ideas-you-can-now-build-with-ai)), and a 2024 Office Hours named "getting addicted to that consulting revenue" as the failure mode ([YC library](https://www.ycombinator.com/library/L8-yc-s-group-partners-discuss-doing-things-that-don-t-scale)). McGrew's "first, second, and third pieces of advice" on forward-deployed engineering were "Don't, don't do this at home... You're probably going to end up doing services." He conceded that the charge that it is just consulting carries "a real risk that it's right" ([YC library](https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Mt-the-fde-playbook-for-ai-startups-with-bob-mcgrew)). Heller predicted "a mass extinction event" of "pilot recurring revenue," and prices that fall once competitors arrive ([YC library](https://www.ycombinator.com/library/c1-from-idea-to-650m-exit-lessons-in-building-ai-startups)). Warren warned that "If revenue scales just in line with the number of humans you add, you'll have major problems."

YC is silent on what matters most to URnetwork. No RFS or talk addresses networking, VPN or connectivity companies, security vendors that sell managed outcomes, professional liability, contract terms for outcome deals, or the lower valuations of services firms. Its nearest infrastructure advice is McGrew's: an on-premises deployment means "you do have to fight the IT team," and it needs a sponsor who can grant "authority to operate."

## Outcome pricing thrives in support tickets and stalls in procurement

The case for selling finished work rests on budgets, and the budgets are real. Sequoia's March 2026 essay counts "For every dollar spent on software, six are spent on services" and puts the argument in one line: "A copilot sells the tool. An autopilot sells the work" ([Sequoia](https://sequoiacap.com/article/services-the-new-software)). The growth is real too. **Sierra, which tells customers "You pay for outcomes, not tokens used," reported more than $400M in ARR in October 2026, after adding over $150M in one quarter.** It took six months to double from $100M to $200M and five more to reach $400M, and investors had valued it at about 100 times revenue a year earlier ([Sierra](https://sierra.ai/blog/our-customers-ambition-is-accelerating-our-growth); [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2025/11/21/bret-taylors-sierra-reaches-100m-arr-in-under-two-years/)). Fin shows the mechanism. It was "margin negative" at launch, with a resolution rate "in the 30s." Because each resolution is billed, every point of improvement since, up to the "high 60s," has been revenue ([Chargebee](https://www.chargebee.com/blog/how-fin-is-rebuilding-fpa-for-outcome-based-ai/); [Growth Unhinged](https://www.growthunhinged.com/p/intercoms-bet-on-ai)). Salesforce agreed in June 2026 to buy Fin for about $3.6B ([Salesforce](https://investor.salesforce.com/news/news-details/2026/Salesforce-Signs-Definitive-Agreement-to-Acquire-Fin/)), and in April HubSpot moved its support agent to "$0.50 per resolved conversation" ([HubSpot](https://www.hubspot.com/company-news/hubspots-customer-agent-and-prospecting-agent-now-you-pay-when-the-task-is-complete)). Buyers are souring on seats for agents. **Only 9% of 1,019 B2B buyers call per-seat pricing the fairest model for agent services**, and the share that prefers to pay per outcome rose from 11% to 19% ([SaaStr on G2](https://saastr.com/g2s-2026-ai-agents-report-only-9-of-buyers-think-seat-pricing-is-fair-57-want-usage-based-and-74-will-shop-you-at-renewal)). Buying finished work also beats building it: MIT's NANDA study found that purchased tools succeed about two-thirds of the time, against one-third for internal builds, though its sample is small ([The Register](https://www.theregister.com/2025/08/18/generative_ai_zero_return_95_percent)).

The case against is about how outcomes are billed and delivered, and it is stronger than the slogans admit. **Bain wrote in August 2026 that outcome pricing has "found a real home in one category: customer support," and that about 10% of AI-native vendors use it.** AlixPartners found that "only 4 of the 65" companies it studied had fully adopted it, Kyle Poyar called it "out of reach for 95% of the market," and Gartner judged its rise "more buzz than reality" ([Bain](https://www.bain.com/insights/ai-pricing-a-reality-check-on-effort-usage-and-outcomes/); [AlixPartners](https://www.alixpartners.com/insights/102nf2s/outcome-based-software-pricing-hype-or-reality/); [Growth Unhinged](https://kylepoyar.substack.com/p/2025-state-of-b2b-monetization); [CIO Dive](https://www.ciodive.com/news/agentic-ai-outcome-pricing-models/829023/)). Where it is tried, finance teams pull it back to fixed commitments. Salesforce went from $2 a conversation to $0.10 an action plus per-user licences in May 2025, and then to flat, multi-year enterprise agreements. In October 2026 Marc Benioff conceded that "Some still want pure user pricing; it's predictable" ([Salesforce](https://www.salesforce.com/blog/flex-credits/); [The Register](https://www.theregister.com/2025/12/12/ai_agents_salesforce_pricing/); [TechTarget](https://www.techtarget.com/it-strategy/news/366651390/Outcomes-based-AI-pricing-works-in-theory-but-in-practice)). Bain's version is that "Procurement teams and CFOs can approve a defined commitment more easily." The market has settled on prepaid budgets counted in outcomes: Fin's buckets, Zendesk's November 2026 "Outcome Budget" and Salesforce's flat agreements. Outcomes that the vendor defines also breed disputes. One analyst said Zendesk's model means "the vendor is grading its own homework," and BCG, citing an a16z survey, reports that 47% of buyers struggle to define measurable outcomes at all ([Futurum](https://futurumgroup.com/?p=97869); [BCG](https://www.bcg.com/publications/2025/rethinking-b2b-software-pricing-in-the-era-of-ai)).

When people do the delivery, margins stay at services levels, whatever the label. AI products averaged **45% gross margin in 2025**, against 70 to 80%-plus for software, and Bessemer's fastest growers ran about 25%, "often negative" ([ICONIQ](https://www.iconiq.com/growth/reports/state-of-ai-2026); [N47](https://www.n47.com/insights/the-forward-deployed-engineer-dilemma); [Bessemer](https://www.bvp.com/atlas/the-state-of-ai-2025)). Forward-deployed engineering, the usual way to deliver, can cost **more than "$200,000 per quarter per use case"** by Gartner's estimate. Gartner counts about 2,000 such engineers against four times that demand. It says many providers now use "forward deployed" as a label for ordinary implementation and professional services, and it expects about 70% of vendor-led AI engineering efforts to be abandoned by 2028 ([CIO Dive](https://www.ciodive.com/news/can-forward-deployed-engineers-fdes-fix-ai-gartner/831833/)). N47 puts the threshold for a deployment team at "$1M+ annual contract values." Palantir, where the model began, had 125 customers after 17 years, and its top 20 produced 67% of its 2019 revenue ([N47](https://www.n47.com/insights/the-forward-deployed-engineer-dilemma); [Palantir S-1](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1321655/000119312520230013/d904406ds1.htm); [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2020/08/21/leaked-palantir-s-1-shows-company-has-125-customers-after-17-years/)). The labs now sell deployment themselves. OpenAI's Deployment Company has more than $4B behind it. Anthropic has a services venture with three private-equity and banking partners, plus a $100M academy to train 10,000 deployment engineers ([OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/openai-launches-the-deployment-company); [Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/news/enterprise-ai-services-company); [Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/news/claude-frontier-academy)). The cautionary cases are familiar. Klarna rehired people after its AI support produced "lower quality," Builder.ai's AI turned out to be largely engineers, and a tribunal held Air Canada liable for a refund policy its chatbot invented ([Entrepreneur](https://www.entrepreneur.com/business-news/klarna-ceo-reverses-course-by-hiring-more-humans-not-ai/491396); [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2025/05/20/once-worth-over-1b-microsoft-backed-builder-ai-is-running-out-of-money); [ABA](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/business%5Flaw/resources/business-law-today/2024-february/bc-tribunal-confirms-companies-remain-liable-information-provided-ai-chatbot/)). Services multiples stay low: roll-ups buy service firms at about one times revenue, and service firms that adopted AI still trade far below software firms ([Air Street](https://press.airstreet.com/p/ai-rollup-mirage-technology-2025)).

The two sides agree more than they seem to. What has worked is a defined result that both sides can check, priced against the labor it replaces, delivered mostly by software, and billed as a commitment a finance team can approve. Sequoia's own caveat explains why support led: Sierra benefits from "a graceful failure mode (escalation to a human agent)... Not all companies are so lucky" ([Sequoia](https://www.sequoiacap.com/article/generative-ais-act-o1/)). Sierra adds that outcome pricing "only works where the software is highly autonomous and highly attributable" ([Sierra](https://sierra.ai/blog/outcomemaxxing)). Two uncertainties remain. Most growth figures are self-reported, and no 2025 or 2026 survey asks how buyers want to pay for finished infrastructure work, as opposed to software.

## URnetwork's outcome is a working network its software builds on the team's machines

For a team, URnetwork's finished work is a private network that works: every laptop, server and CI runner reachable by private address; services online at the team's own domain, with the TLS keys kept on its machines; mail at that domain, received on a machine the team runs; sign-in from its own identity provider, with roles; and traffic that leaves through machines it chooses. The labor this replaces is what privacy-first teams now do themselves or pay someone to do: running a mesh control plane, tunnels, a reverse proxy, a mail server and a password vault. Infrastructure buyers already buy this kind of result. Teleport's customer Turo says "Everything was up and running in two hours." Cloudflare's case study says "In just over 3 months, Indeed had deprecated its VPN entirely," and ngrok's says Databricks cut "customer deployment time from months to days" ([Teleport](https://goteleport.com/case-study/turo/); [Cloudflare](https://www.cloudflare.com/case-studies/indeed/); [ngrok](https://ngrok.com/customers/databricks)). Zscaler sells multi-year contracts that include "premium deployment assistance and support," and booked over $770M of them in one quarter ([TradingKey](https://www.tradingkey.com/news/transcripts/262150882-tradingkey)). The owner's request fits how this category is bought. The tests below show where it fits URnetwork's buyer.

| Test | What the evidence says | A URnetwork team deployment |
|---|---|---|
| Someone already pays for the work | Warren's best markets are where "the work is already outsourced" | Self-hosters pay in their own time. One company found self-hosted NetBird failing for "10-15% of users" ([HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=46846275)), and self-hosting is "a very demanding hobby" ([HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=46582112)). Small companies without IT staff often pay a contractor; how many are on URnetwork's list is unknown |

The buyer is the person who would otherwise do the setup, which fits the person-first hypothesis the owner adopted (Q-OB-13) |

Two of the six tests count against a paid service for the segment the owner adopted, and four favor a setup done by software. The self-hosters at the heart of the hypothesis pay in time rather than from a budget, and they care exactly how the work is done, so Warren's two market tests fail for them. Small companies that already pay a contractor would pass, but nothing yet shows how many are on URnetwork's list. The other four tests point one way: a check turns the result into something a contract can name, a setup the customer's agent can run improves as models do, the person who would do the work is the buyer, and the only way to make delivery cheaper per customer is to build each lesson into the software.

### The directive turns "do the work for them" into "the software does it on their devices"

URnetwork's standing rule narrows YC's "do the work for them." **No URnetwork server receives content or keys in plaintext, servers originate only denials, and no URnetwork-run client holds Network rights.** Only a team's admins can add a device or a member ([DESIGN.md §2.8, I-1 to I-10](file:///Users/brien/urnetwork/sidecar/DESIGN.md)). There are two sanctioned exceptions: the abuse-report and role-mail listener, and the support conversation, which a person and the AI support agent read in full by the owner's choice ([DESIGN-COMPUTE.md](file:///Users/brien/urnetwork/sidecar/DESIGN-COMPUTE.md)).

A URnetwork engineer therefore can't terminate a team's TLS, hold its keys, run its mail receiver or index its files. Staff also shouldn't join a customer's network, even as invited members: once staff can be invited in, "only your admins can add a device" stops being a clean promise. Delivery partners are out for the same reason, since each one would be another reader.

What remains is the pattern privacy-first vendors already follow. Proton promises "no one can access your business data, not even us," and still helps customers migrate. 1Password delivers its services remotely and leaves the customer "responsibility for all system configurations." Tailscale's support can turn off Tailnet Lock only with a secret the customer chose to give it, and says plainly that without one "the tailnet cannot be recovered" ([Proton](https://proton.me/support/proton-for-business); [1Password](https://1password.com/legal/professional-services-catalog); [Tailscale](https://tailscale.com/kb/1226/tailnet-lock)).

For URnetwork, the work itself has to run where the keys are. URnetwork's software performs each step on the team's devices, and the team's admins approve each new device on their own. URnetwork's people see only what the team chooses to send support, under the disclosure the owner set in Q-OB-21. That is Warren's "The human is the interface of the customer, not the product," and the RFS's users who act as "their own forward deployed engineers." URnetwork reaches it through a privacy rule rather than a margin target, and it is the only version of the service that passes the Sam Altman test.

### "Global" has to describe the team's machines, not URnetwork's buildings

The market sells global reach as dedicated egress, priced per location. GoodAccess adds a gateway with a static IP for $49 a month in 35+ locations, NordLayer a dedicated-IP server for $40 a month, and ngrok dedicated IPs at $900 a month per region, while Cloudflare provides at least two dedicated egress IPs "in two different cities" as an Enterprise add-on ([GoodAccess](https://www.goodaccess.com/pricing); [NordLayer](https://nordlayer.com/pricing/); [ngrok](https://ngrok.com/pricing); [Cloudflare](https://developers.cloudflare.com/cloudflare-one/policies/gateway/egress-policies/dedicated-egress-ips/)). URnetwork can't sell that in v0. **Its own infrastructure is one site, with no off-site copy and "no dedicated addresses for user traffic or mail."** A second site will be "decided from operating data" ([DESIGN.md, owner decisions 89 and 94, R-OPS-10](file:///Users/brien/urnetwork/sidecar/DESIGN.md)). The member exit network does span many countries. But the Team campaign waits on Team being visibly separate from it (Q-OB-1), so the exit network can't be what "global" means for Team ([OVERVIEW.md](file:///Users/brien/urnetwork/mmm/ur.io/docs/OVERVIEW.md); [PLAN.md §7](file:///Users/brien/urnetwork/mmm/outbound/PLAN.md)).

The design already has the private version. ## A $10 Team can include a setup, not an engineer

The infrastructure market keeps human help out of cheap team plans. Across about 25 networking and developer-platform vendors, self-serve tiers at $4 to $18 a user include ticket support and docs. Implementation help starts at Enterprise, described at Tailscale and NetBird in the identical words "Solutions Engineer consultation and implementation assistance" ([Tailscale](https://tailscale.com/pricing); [NetBird](https://netbird.io/pricing)). Where a low tier does include a person, the help is light: GoodAccess offers onboarding advice from $7 a user, Netmaker's $99 plan includes a "Free Implementation Session," Proton's support team helps with migration, and account managers or training come only past a seat threshold, 30 licenses at NordLayer and 101 users at 1Password ([GoodAccess](https://www.goodaccess.com/pricing); [Netmaker](https://www.netmaker.io/pricing); [Proton](https://proton.me/support/proton-for-business); [NordLayer](https://nordlayer.com/pricing/); [1Password](https://1password.com/business-pricing)). The trend runs toward less included help, not more. On 15 October 2026, Tailscale removed Enterprise's "dedicated professional services" and Premium's priority support, in favor of paid SLA tiers and separately scoped add-ons ([PricingSaaS](https://pricingsaas.com/news/article/tailscale-drops-premium-support-for-sla-backed-silver-gold-and-platinum-tiers)). Cloudflare's October update said "Large or small, every customer would prefer not to have to call support" ([Cloudflare](https://blog.cloudflare.com/enterprise-for-all-update/)).

The arithmetic explains why. Professional services run at about **30% gross margin, against 81% for subscriptions**, in Benchmarkit's 2025 survey, which I saw only through a secondary summary ([Orb](https://www.withorb.com/blog/saas-growth-margin-statistics)). A 2015 study of 13 public SaaS companies found services teams hard to run "at better than breakeven" below about $200,000 in contract value ([Tunguz](https://www.tomtunguz.com/professional_services_margins/index.md)). A three-seat Team brings **$80 a year on the annual plan, or $120 on the monthly one**. Zscaler's public price list values an expert's day at $2,500, about $312 an hour ([Zscaler FY24 price list](https://static.carahsoft.com/concrete/files/8016/9048/7202/ZscalerGovPriceList_FY24_final.pdf)). One included hour of engineering therefore gives away 2.6 to 3.9 times a three-seat Team's first year, so a deployment led by a person would lose money on every small team. A light human touch is a different matter. One product-led vendor that phoned every trial user saw 32% more closed deals, though it published no baseline, and 69 to 80% of self-serve products put a person in front of their enterprise users ([Close](https://close.com/blog/calling-all-new-trials); [ChartMogul](https://chartmogul.com/reports/saas-conversion-report-2/)).

So the service Team includes should be the setup itself, delivered by software with a person behind it, in two phases. **At the Team launch, before the setup software exists, every new team gets written help from a person while it sets up**, inside the support conversation and its disclosure, and if the admin sends a plan, written from a template in the admin guide, a person reads it. This is "doing things that don't scale" for the launch cohort, which the campaign's reach keeps small: about 5,100 networks can be emailed, and the plan expects roughly 59 clicks ([PLAN.md §3](file:///Users/brien/urnetwork/mmm/outbound/PLAN.md)).

**When the guided setup ships, Team adds it.** The setup is a flow in the app and the `ur` CLI, also written as steps a team's own agent can follow, as YC's "Software for Agents" RFS asks ([YC RFS](https://www.ycombinator.com/rfs)). It plans the network, does each step on the team's devices, leaves every admission to an admin's device, and ends with a check of every machine, name and mailbox. At the handover the admin gets a runbook and a page listing what URnetwork's servers see of this network, which is the document the follow-ups' "Forward this to whoever signs off on security" needs. No calls, SLA or response time are promised, which keeps the Services page's standing position ([Services.jsx L9-12](file:///Users/brien/urnetwork/mmm/ur.io/react/src/pages/Services.jsx)). The setup is a design follow-on for DESIGN-UI.md, and it strings together screens and commands the design already has: Team's TM1 to TM9, and the CLI's auth keys and OAuth clients ([DESIGN-UI.md](file:///Users/brien/urnetwork/sidecar/DESIGN-UI.md)).

Anything beyond that should be sold as a custom deployment: several places, many services, or a move from a setup the team hosts itself. It should be scoped the way the market scopes and priced by YC's rules. Infrastructure vendors scope services by quantity, place and time box ("for up to 3 pair... within 4 weeks"), and Zscaler's credit catalog states "No fractional credit redemption or custom scoping is allowed" ([Zscaler catalog](https://www.zscaler.com/resources/data-sheets/zscaler-professional-services-credit-offerings.pdf)); Warren calls per-unit pricing "the cleanest." URnetwork's units would be places, published services, domains with mail, and moves from a self-hosted setup, quoted together as one fixed price alongside the written plan. **Half should be paid to start, which is the paid commitment Blomfield asks for, and half when the agreed check passes.** A pass-or-fail check is "observable" and "contractible" in Bain's terms, and as a Gartner analyst put it, "If the vendor isn't taking on the risk, why are you bothering with outcome-based pricing?" ([CIO Dive](https://www.ciodive.com/news/agentic-ai-outcome-pricing-models/829023/)).

Cap the first five, as Warren advises, and have Brien run them, since YC tells founders to see themselves as "the forward deployed engineers of their own company" ([YC library](https://www.ycombinator.com/library/MS-state-of-the-art-prompting-for-ai-agents)); any calls follow the conversation program's rules of no recording, no transcription and no AI note-taker ([PLAN.md §9](file:///Users/brien/urnetwork/mmm/outbound/PLAN.md)). Quote them between about $1,000 and $5,000. That band is my judgment, not market data: it sits below enterprise vendors' entry packages, $5,250 for Zscaler's remote health check and $7,800 for its essential deployment, and close to one or two months of the "couple thousand a month" a small business pays a part-time admin ([HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50394143)). Publish a price only after the first five. Each deployment must leave a new step in the setup, so that human minutes per deployment fall, the reverse of N47's warning sign. The terms should state in writing that the team's admins approve and own the configuration and that the work is remote, as privacy-first vendors already do.

| | Included in every Team | Custom deployment |
|---|---|---|
| At the Team launch | Written help from a person while the team sets up; a person reads the admin's plan | Several places, many services or a move from a self-hosted setup; one fixed price quoted with the plan; half to start, half when the check passes; five at a time |
| When the guided setup ships | The setup on the team's devices, its check, and the handover: a runbook and what URnetwork's servers see | The same work, now delivered mostly through the setup, with fewer human minutes each |
| Never | Calls, SLAs, response times, staff inside the network, keys or passwords | Hourly rates, cost-plus, ongoing operations, delivery partners |

## Conclusion

The question was how to add services to a network product. The evidence turns it into a question about the product's setup. For most AI-native firms, YC's advice to sell the finished work is a margin strategy. For URnetwork it is also the only form its rule allows, because the people who could do a team's work for it are exactly the readers the product promises to keep out. The rule brings an unexpected benefit: a company that can't put its people inside a customer's network can't drift into the consulting business McGrew warns about, so every gain in delivery has to show up as software. Its cost is that the largest budgets YC now points to, deployments inside regulated buyers' walls, stay out of reach until the assured stage.

The weak point is the buyer, not the page or the price. YC's best markets are the ones where someone already pays for the work, and URnetwork's chosen segment pays in its own hours. One interview question and five quotes will show whether a paid deployment line exists, or whether services belong only inside Team, as the reason a Team gets set up at all. Either way, the product stays the same. Until operating data justifies a second site, "global" should stay a promise about the customer's machines rather than URnetwork's datacenter. That is both the honest claim and the private one.
