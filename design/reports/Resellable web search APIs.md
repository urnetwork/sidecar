# Mojeek and Parallel Outrank Brave for Resale

Yes: several web search APIs license results on terms materially looser than Brave's, though none of their self-serve contracts covers URnetwork's whole use (a per-search charge at cost plus 20%, results handed to the person's agents and coding harnesses, and a copy in the encrypted record), so every route still needs at least one signed permission. **Mojeek** fits best on paper: its binding terms allow commercial "subscription services" and "AI agents and assistants", its £3-per-1,000 Business plan lets customers keep results "indefinitely", and its data terms are the cleanest found (no end-user IP addresses, 90-day fault logs kept out of model training, optional zero retention), though no independent benchmark has scored its 9-billion-page index and it cannot be paid without an account key. **Parallel** is the strongest AI-native alternative, with the only such contract written around a customer's "End Customers", the top score on the one independent benchmark (83 against Brave's 75), prices of $1–$5 per 1,000 searches, and a gateway that sells search for $0.01 a request with no account. URnetwork's rule that its servers never see a query narrows the field more than any license does. No search provider handles queries inside an enclave a client can verify, so the provider always reads the query, and URnetwork stays blind only if the person's device pays the provider directly over x402 (Brave, You.com, Exa, Tavily, Parallel, Keenable and Linkup accept it, at the cost of a public payment trail). A relay of URnetwork's own holding an account key, even inside an enclave the device verifies, is a server component that reads queries, which URnetwork's owner directive rules out (DESIGN §0); so providers sold only by account key, Mojeek among them, are out of reach until they offer a keyless or per-device path. **Gamma buys no web search today**: none of the 13 gamma-enabled subnets sells it, and Desearch (SN22), the one subnet-backed search API, takes card or TAO. Resellers of scraped Google results are the cheapest and the most relaxed about storage, but a relay that turns users' queries into scraper calls would stand where Perplexity stands in Reddit v. SerpApi, pleaded as a direct circumventor, so they should be ruled out.

## Mojeek and Parallel license this use; Brave sits at the strict end

Brave's Search API Terms of Use (1 September 2026) license results only for use "with the Customer Applications". They forbid four things URnetwork's plan touches: storing results beyond "transient storage required for operation", making the API available to any third party, replicating its functionality, and any attempt to "redistribute, resell, or sublicense the Search Results" ([Brave Terms of Use](https://api-dashboard.search.brave.com/documentation/resources/terms-of-service)). The FAQ is blunter: "**It is prohibited to retain any and all data received**" ([Brave FAQ](https://api-dashboard.search.brave.com/documentation/resources/help-feedback)). A cost-plus charge per search sits next to the resale ban. A copy in the encrypted record still counts as storage, because the clause limits keeping results, not who can read them. Brave does sell storage rights by custom plan. Its last public storage tiers, in June 2025, cost **$26 and $45 per 1,000 queries, five to nine times the base rate** ([Brave API page, June 2025 snapshot](https://web.archive.org/web/20250612135902/https://brave.com/search/api/)). It also licenses results to other engines outside its price list, Kagi among them ([Kagi blog](https://blog.kagi.com/waiting-dawn-search)). So the right baseline for every alternative is a negotiated Brave Order Form, not the self-serve plan.

Mojeek's terms read as if written for URnetwork. Its data "may be used in any application, including internet search experiences and artificial intelligence applications such as retrieval-augmented generation, grounding, AI agents and assistants". Customers "may use Data for commercial purposes, such as subscription services… **without attribution to Mojeek**". They may reorder the data, mix it with other engines' results and generate new content from it. On a plan with storage rights they "may **retain Data indefinitely**" ([Mojeek API terms](https://www.mojeek.com/about/legal/api-terms.html)). Business (£3 per 1,000) and Enterprise carry those rights, while Startup (£2) allows only a one-hour cache for the same user ([Mojeek Search API](https://www.mojeek.com/services/search/web-search-api/)). Two clauses need Mojeek's written reading. "**Do not use Data as part of any search index**" could catch the record's own encrypted search index if stored results are indexed, so either keep result text out of that index or get a letter from Mojeek; and automated access is barred "unless you are an authorised Services user", which keeps the key off devices. The terms say nothing on wholesale resale, let either side end them "immediately, on notice", and let Mojeek vary them in writing for Enterprise customers, which is the route for both questions.

Parallel's Customer Terms (effective 10 November 2026) are the only AI-native contract built around an application that serves its own customers. The license includes the right "to make Customer Applications available to End Customers" and to fold output into what they receive. The condition is that "Customer Output generated from one query shall be **primarily for the use of one End Customer only**, and shall not be copied, cached, stored, or made available to other End Customers or other third parties" ([Parallel Customer Terms](https://parallel.ai/customer-terms)). Nothing limits what URnetwork charges, and keeping one person's results in that person's record fits the one-customer rule. Two clauses need written confirmation. Section 2(c)(xi) bars "intentionally or knowingly" diverting requests away from Parallel, "such as by making available Customer Outputs from previous queries for future uses", which an agent built to reread old results instead of searching again could trip. The terms also bar storing output "to create the AI and Data Selling Services", a defined term whose reach over a metered search service is unclear. End Customers must accept terms "at least as protective of the Parallel IP", and each customer stays on the version in force when it joined, which protects URnetwork if Parallel later tightens them.

A middle group is looser than Brave but leaves gaps. You.com's online MSA "assigns to Customer all of our right, title, and interest" in its outputs ([You.com MSA](https://45321510.fs1.hubspotusercontent-na1.net/hubfs/45321510/Legal/You.com%20MSA%20(Online)%20(December%202025).pdf)), yet its API Acceptable Use Policy (22 October 2026) bars keeping "significant portions of Search Content" for re-use, reselling it "as a standalone dataset, feed, or index", or offering the service "as a standalone API or service bureau" without a contract ([You.com AUP](https://you.com/acceptable-use-policy-may-2026)); a person's own past searches arguably fall below that line, though the reading is untested. Tavily limits use to "internal business purposes, including for integration with Customer Applications" and exempts that integration from its resale ban; it says nothing on storage, requires customers to bind "Downstream Users" to its AUP, and names authorized resellers and marketplace partners ([Tavily terms](https://www.tavily.com/terms); [Tavily AUP](https://www.tavily.com/acceptable-use-policy); [Tavily partnerships](https://www.tavily.com/partnerships)). Desearch's ten-section terms ban only "Reselling our Services without authorization" and say nothing about storing or passing on results ([Desearch terms](https://www.desearch.ai/terms-of-service)). Staan, the European index that supplies Qwant and Ecosia, answers "Yes" to white-label and sub-licensing, but only in an FAQ, while its binding terms are "provided upon registration" ([Staan FAQ](https://staan.ai/faq)). Jina "imposes no restrictions on the Customer regarding the use of the Output" ([Jina legal](https://jina.ai/legal)), but its search proxies Google and Bing, as the section on scraped results explains.

The rest are as strict as Brave or stricter. Exa forbids anyone to "copy, distribute, transmit, display… or offer for sale any information contained on, or obtained from or through, the Services" without written permission, which reaches further than Brave ([Exa terms](https://exa.ai/assets/Exa_Labs_Terms_of_Service.pdf)); yet its partner program invites partners to "**Resell the API**", and OpenRouter already serves Exa to its own users as default search ([Exa partners](https://exa.ai/partners); [Exa for inference providers](https://exa.ai/use-cases/inference-providers)), so Exa is a partner deal or nothing. Kagi permits "sending Results to an AI model to answer your users' queries" but bans storage beyond "transient caching" and any proxying of raw results "as a… substitute for direct Kagi API access", which a generic MCP search tool risks ([Kagi API terms](https://kagi.com/privacy/api)). Perplexity's Search Addendum denies the customer ownership of search output, lets Perplexity "retain, copy, distribute and otherwise use Search Data", and forbids sending personal data without written authorization, which no free-form search box can guarantee ([Perplexity Search Addendum](https://www.perplexity.ai/hub/legal/perplexity-api-terms-of-service-search)). Linkup licenses web content only under the EU text-and-data-mining exception and forbids "acting as a wrapper" ([Linkup terms](https://www.linkup.so/terms-of-use)); Valyu forbids reselling outputs or storing them indefinitely ([Valyu agreement](https://www.valyu.ai/valyu-platform-service-agreement)); Firecrawl bans "any commercial purposes except as expressly authorized" ([Firecrawl terms](https://www.firecrawl.dev/terms-of-service)); and Yep, Ahrefs' index, needs "express written consent" before anyone exploits access to it commercially ([Yep terms](https://platform.yep.com/legal/terms-of-service/)).

Two small indexes are permissive by silence but impractical. Marginalia's commercial key carries "no commercial usage restriction or attribution requirement", but it allows only 15 queries a minute ([Marginalia commercial license](https://buy.polar.sh/polar_cl_hKnbSGWkflTozpXajwIu2ycB2fYmLM8jDxsuh06Kvmt)). Yandex's terms say nothing on storage or resale but forbid reordering results. They also route queries to the Russian company Yandex LLC, which calls for a sanctions review first ([Yandex Search API terms](https://yandex.com/legal/cloud_terms_search_api/en/)).

The big companies offer nothing usable. Google's Custom Search API is closed to new customers and ends on 1 January 2027, and its full-web Web Search Service is partner-only ([Google Custom Search](https://developers.google.com/custom-search/v1/overview); [Google Web Search Service](https://developers.google.com/web-search-service)). Microsoft's Web IQ, the only pure-retrieval API from a big company, allows content only for LLM use inside the customer's application, at most **200 characters per source**, and bars access "outside of, the Application", resale, and storage beyond per-user LLM summaries ([Web IQ terms](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/webiq/webiq-legal)), which rules out both a results page and raw results in a person's harness.

Handing results to Codex or Claude Code through a local MCP tool is where several contracts bite hardest. Claude Code's documentation says its search backend "is not configurable" and that another provider needs "an MCP server that exposes a search tool" ([Claude Code tools reference](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/tools-reference#websearch-tool-behavior)). So MCP is the right shape, and Mojeek ("any application") and Parallel ("one End Customer") permit it. Elsewhere, Kagi's proxy ban, Brave's bans on making the API available or replicating it, Perplexity's "solely within the Customer Applications" and DataForSEO's naming of "MCP endpoints" as needing a separate agreement ([DataForSEO legal FAQ](https://dataforseo.com/help-center/quick-answers-to-frequently-asked-legal-questions)) all point to the same design: a URnetwork-defined tool that returns URnetwork's own result format, never an endpoint compatible with a provider's API, and never a provider key on the device. Brave's own MCP server reads `BRAVE_API_KEY` from the user's machine ([Brave MCP server](https://github.com/brave/brave-search-mcp-server)), which is exactly what Brave's key clause forbids when the key is URnetwork's.

## Keeping URnetwork blind leaves one design today: x402 from the device

Whichever provider wins, it reads the query, because **no search provider found handles queries inside an enclave a client can verify**. On Bittensor, the standard SDK posts each request to miners as JSON over plain `http://` ([Bittensor dendrite.py](https://github.com/RaoFoundation/bittensor/blob/master/bittensor/core/dendrite.py)), so every miner queried sees it. Desearch's current version keeps queries from miners only by answering them from its own central index ([Desearch and Bittensor](https://www.desearch.ai/docs/guide/introduction/desearch-x-bittensor)). Gopher (SN42) was the one data subnet that scraped inside SGX enclaves. It changed owner on 25 March 2026, and its API host now fails ([Gopher miner docs](https://developers.gopher-ai.com/docs/subnet/miners); [Finney chain read](https://entrypoint-finney.opentensor.ai)).

The real question is whether URnetwork reads the query too, and every license above assumes it does. Each is written for a key the customer holds on its own server. Brave, Tavily, You.com and Oxylabs each forbid sharing or transferring that key ([Brave Terms of Use](https://api-dashboard.search.brave.com/documentation/resources/terms-of-service); [Tavily terms](https://www.tavily.com/terms); [You.com MSA](https://45321510.fs1.hubspotusercontent-na1.net/hubfs/45321510/Legal/You.com%20MSA%20(Online)%20(December%202025).pdf); [Oxylabs terms](https://oxylabs.io/legal/self-service-tos)). Keys issued per person do exist: Exa's team API mints keys with their own budgets, Yep provisions keys programmatically, and Jina keeps balances on keys ([Exa billing](https://exa.ai/docs/admin/billing.md); [Yep](https://ahrefs.com/yep); [Jina Reader](https://jina.ai/reader)). But each of these puts an account credential on the device. Two designs avoid it.

In the first, **the device opens its own TLS session to the provider through URnetwork's tunnel** and pays each request with x402, which needs no account. URnetwork's servers carry only ciphertext and the server name, and the provider sees an exit address rather than the person's. Seven providers sell search this way:

| Provider | Keyless price | What it covers | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Brave** | $0.005 per request | Web, LLM Context, news, images, videos and place search | [Brave x402](https://search.agent.s.brave.app/llms.txt) |
| **You.com** | $0.005 per search; $0.01 over MPP on Tempo | Search only | [You.com x402](https://you.com/docs/administration/machine-payments/x402) |
| **Exa** | $0.004 lite, $0.007 auto; $0.001 per page of contents | Search and contents | [Exa x402 guide](https://exa.ai/docs/reference/x402-guide) |
| **Tavily** | $0.01 per search | Advanced search only | [Tavily x402](https://docs.tavily.com/documentation/machine-payments/x402) |
| **Parallel** | $0.01 per search or extracted URL | Search and extract, through a gateway that takes x402, Tempo MPP or a card | [Parallel agentic payments](https://docs.parallel.ai/integrations/agentic-payments) |
| **Keenable** | $0.01 | Search or fetch | [Keenable](https://docs.keenable.ai/pay-per-request) |
| **Linkup** | $0.01 flat | Search | [Linkup x402](https://docs.linkup.so/pages/documentation/platform/x402.md) |

Exa computes each price "upfront based on your request parameters", which suits cost-plus metering, but keyless does not always cost the same as an account. Parallel charges **2–10 times** its account rate, Keenable 2.5 times ($0.01 against $0.004 with a prepaid key) and Linkup twice, while Tavily goes the other way: its keyless advanced search ($0.01) undercuts its own pay-as-you-go rate of two $0.008 credits. You.com's Contents API cannot be bought over x402, and its keyless requests get no zero data retention. Rate limits follow the payer and the address. Exa allows 10 paid requests a second per wallet, so a single URnetwork wallet would cap all keyless Exa traffic at 10 a second. Parallel's gateway allows 60 requests a minute per IP address, which shared exit addresses would reach quickly.

The payment never needs to know the search. In x402's default "exact" scheme, a payment is a signed USDC transfer authorization under EIP-3009, as Keenable documents for its own service ([Keenable](https://docs.keenable.ai/pay-per-request)). That signature covers only payer, payee, amount, a validity window and a nonce, nothing about the HTTP request it pays for ([EIP-3009](https://eips.ethereum.org/EIPS/eip-3009)). A device can therefore ask URnetwork's treasury to sign "$0.005 to Brave", naming only payee and amount. URnetwork then meters each person per search without seeing what they searched. Alternatively, URnetwork can fund a small wallet on the device and let the device sign alone. Either way, the device should pass the signer nothing copied from the request.

The cost is a new metadata leak, because the exact scheme settles **one public on-chain transfer per request**. If URnetwork's wallet pays, the chain publishes URnetwork's search volume to the second, and anyone can send a probe query through URnetwork's apps and watch the payment appear. If a per-person wallet pays, the chain ties every search time to one address and gives the provider a stable identifier for all of that person's searches. Brave's privacy notice warns that such a transaction "may be linked to you by others" ([Brave privacy notice](https://api-dashboard.search.brave.com/documentation/resources/privacy-notice)). Only Exa offers batching today, through Circle Gateway nanopayments. The buyer makes one on-chain deposit and then signs authorizations off-chain, which Circle settles as periodic net balances; Circle sees every authorization ([Circle batched settlement](https://developers.circle.com/gateway/nanopayments/concepts/batched-settlement.md)). An x402 batch-settlement scheme and MPP payment sessions also exist, but no search provider documents either ([x402 batch settlement](https://www.x402.org/writing/x402-batch-settlement); [MPP sessions](https://mpp.dev/payment-methods/tempo/session)).

Paying in crypto changes no license term, but it can change who the customer is. If each person's own wallet pays, that person arguably becomes the provider's direct customer, and URnetwork resells nothing. Only each provider can confirm that reading, and it would leave limits such as Brave's storage ban binding the person whose record keeps the copy. A pooled URnetwork wallet hides the person but keeps URnetwork as the reseller; a per-person wallet does the reverse.

A second design would keep **URnetwork's account key inside a relay running in a trusted execution environment** (TEE) that the device verifies before sending a query. The provider would see URnetwork's account and the relay's address, never the person; the key would never leave URnetwork's custody, and a card-funded prepaid balance leaves no per-request record on any chain. But the relay is URnetwork's own server component and reads every query inside the enclave, and URnetwork's owner directive says such a component "is a design error and is never offered as an option" (DESIGN §0); the exceptions I-2 and D-TRUST-4 name do not stretch to it. Remote inference differs: there the TEE is a third-party provider's, under the owner's own TEE rule, not a server of URnetwork's. So the design that DESIGN-COMPUTE.md §21 adopts is x402 from the device, and the providers that sell nothing keyless stay out of reach until they offer a keyless or per-device credential: Mojeek, which takes a bank card through Stripe and processes no automatic payments ([Mojeek Search API](https://www.mojeek.com/services/search/web-search-api/)); Desearch, which takes card or TAO; Staan, which does not yet take cards ([Staan FAQ](https://staan.ai/faq)); Parallel's no-retention EU endpoint, which nothing published says the payment gateway can reach; and the zero-retention tiers sold per account, Exa's at **$12,000 a year** ([Exa zero data retention](https://exa.ai/docs/admin/security/zero-data-retention.md)), Parallel's with its $1,000-a-month Scale plan ([Parallel pricing](https://parallel.ai/pricing)), and You.com's and Brave's only under enterprise contracts. Asking these providers for x402 or per-device keys is the path to them.

**Gamma pays for none of this.** At finalized block 9,570,155 (14:58 UTC on 23 November 2026), gamma was on across the network. Thirteen subnets had enabled it and 11 had registered service entries, and **none of the 11 sells web search** ([Finney chain read](https://entrypoint-finney.opentensor.ai)). The published services cover inference, GPU pods, cloud storage, confidential sandboxes, coding tasks, robotics, media generation and URnetwork's own plans; [Lium's](https://lium.io/api/gamma/service.json) and [engy's](https://engy.ai/services.json) are examples. The two subnets that sell search or web data, Desearch (SN22) and Data Universe (SN13), have not enabled gamma.

Desearch is the only general web search API backed by a subnet, and it is an ordinary USD service. It takes card or "TAO where supported", with no documented TAO mechanics, and its Bittensor page tells users they "pay in USD" ([Desearch billing](https://www.desearch.ai/docs/guide/introduction/pricing-and-billing); [Desearch and Bittensor](https://www.desearch.ai/docs/guide/introduction/desearch-x-bittensor)). The shortest path to gamma-paid search is to ask Desearch to enable gamma on SN22, publish a service document, and credit a USD balance to each spending hotkey, as Lium does. SN22's on-chain repository sits under the same Datura GitHub organization as Lium's ([Finney chain read](https://entrypoint-finney.opentensor.ai)). Even then, gamma would buy a payment rail, not privacy, since Desearch keeps queries for 12 months unless zero retention is arranged ([Desearch privacy policy](https://www.desearch.ai/privacy-policy)). Data Universe sells X and Reddit records at $0.15 per 1,000 through its API, and it is worse for privacy: it shows each job's usernames and keywords to miners, who scrape them through an Apify actor ([Data Universe on-demand docs](https://github.com/macrocosm-os/data-universe/blob/main/docs/on_demand.md)).

## Prices cluster at Brave's $5, but data terms split the field

On self-serve plans, a 10-result search costs **$1 to $8 per 1,000** almost everywhere. At cost plus 20%, a person would pay between $0.0012 and $0.0096 per search, against $0.006 through Brave. The cheapest raw search with a published price is $1 per 1,000, from three sources: Desearch's results-page-style `GET /web` (up to 20 results), Parallel's turbo and fast modes, and Perplexity's Fast search ([Desearch pricing](https://www.desearch.ai/pricing); [Parallel pricing](https://docs.parallel.ai/getting-started/pricing.md); [Perplexity pricing](https://docs.perplexity.ai/docs/getting-started/pricing.md)). Mojeek's £2–£3 stays under Brave's $5 unless a pound buys more than $1.66. Brave's own rate is also the market's most common one, shared by You.com, Perplexity's standard search, Parallel's basic and advanced modes, and Linkup. Kagi ($12) and Web IQ ($7) cost more ([Kagi pricing](https://kagi.com/api/pricing); [Web IQ pricing](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/web-iq/resources/pricing)).

Capacity separates the providers more than price does. Mojeek Business stops at 10 queries a second and 400,000 a day, about 12 million a month, where Brave's Search plan allows 50 requests a second ([Brave pricing](https://api-dashboard.search.brave.com/documentation/pricing)). For metering, Desearch returns each call's cost in an `X-Desearch-Cost-Usd` header ([Desearch OpenAPI](https://api.desearch.ai/openapi.json)), and Exa lets each key carry its own budget. Parallel's spend limits "never block requests", and Staan offers no spending cap at all, so for those two URnetwork's broker or the device must enforce budgets itself ([Exa billing](https://exa.ai/docs/admin/billing.md); [Parallel FAQ](https://docs.parallel.ai/resources/faqs.md); [Staan FAQ](https://staan.ai/faq)).

Quality evidence is thin, and what exists favors the providers with less friendly licenses. The only independent comparison found, Artificial Analysis's Search Index (data to 19 November 2026), scores **Parallel's advanced search 83, Exa's auto search 80, Perplexity's Search API 80 and Brave's LLM Context 75**. For reference, it gives OpenAI's web search 74 and a model with no search at all 33 ([Artificial Analysis](https://artificialanalysis.ai/agents/search-api)). The index covers 26 products from 14 providers, yet its visible view showed no score for Mojeek, Kagi, Yep, Web IQ or Staan. In other words, the indexes with the best terms are the unmeasured ones.

Desearch ran its own benchmark on SimpleQA Verified and left Brave out ([Desearch benchmarks](https://www.desearch.ai/benchmarks)). Desearch found the reference page in the top 10 more often than any provider it tested: 99.1%, against 90.7% for Parallel, 89.0% for Perplexity, 79.5% for Exa and 63.5% for Tavily. But it trailed Parallel, Perplexity and Exa at returning text that states the answer, at 86.6% against 95.0–98.9%. Index size points the same way: Mojeek reports more than nine billion pages ([Mojeek about](https://www.mojeek.com/about/)), while Exa says it tracks two trillion URLs and serves 100 billion pages ([Exa FAQ](https://exa.ai/docs/admin/faqs.md)). Mojeek explicitly allows combining its results with other engines', so its terms permit pairing it with a benchmarked index for coverage.

Data handling splits the field more sharply than price. Mojeek's data processing agreement is part of its terms ([Mojeek DPA](https://www.mojeek.com/about/legal/data-processing-agreement.html)). It says Mojeek "does not receive or record End User IP addresses". It keeps the query, parameters, timestamp and key for up to 90 days, solely to fix faults, and never copies them "into analytics, customer-support, backup or **model-training systems**". No sub-processor touches queries, and on specific plans Mojeek offers zero retention, holding queries "only in volatile memory". Brave keeps a 90-day query log, sells zero retention only to enterprise customers, and leaves query data out of its own processing agreement ([Brave privacy notice](https://api-dashboard.search.brave.com/documentation/resources/privacy-notice)).

Parallel may train on customer inputs unless zero retention is on, which comes with the Scale plan once an agreement is signed ([Parallel Customer Terms](https://parallel.ai/customer-terms); [Parallel pricing](https://parallel.ai/pricing)), but its EU endpoint does "not retain request or response content" and serves only the basic mode, at $5 per 1,000 ([Parallel privacy policy](https://parallel.ai/privacy-policy); [Parallel search modes](https://docs.parallel.ai/search/modes.md)). That endpoint is the only no-retention search found with a published price and no minimum commitment, the one possible exception being Jina's per-request opt-out, whose price could not be read.

Other providers use or keep queries by default. Exa uses query data for "training and fine-tuning models" ([Exa privacy policy](https://exa.ai/privacy-policy)); Tavily may use it to improve results and passes some queries to "third-party search index providers (e.g., Google)" ([Tavily privacy](https://www.tavily.com/privacy)); Yep's terms let Ahrefs "train our machine learning models" on search terms ([Yep terms](https://platform.yep.com/legal/terms-of-service/)); Desearch keeps queries for 12 months; and Perplexity may redistribute them. Jina states that it never trains on API data, and it honors a per-request `DNT` header that stops caching and logging ([Jina Reader](https://jina.ai/reader)). That is the best self-serve privacy promise among AI-native providers, but its search forwards queries to Google and Bing. Zero retention also costs less than its sticker price suggests. Exa's $12,000 year includes $12,000 of credits, and Parallel's Scale fee buys credits too ([Exa billing](https://exa.ai/docs/admin/billing.md); [Parallel pricing](https://parallel.ai/pricing)). So for an end-to-end-encrypted product, zero retention is a minimum spend commitment to make from launch with any provider that trains on queries by default.

## Scraped Google results put the buyer in Perplexity's seat

Where results come from decides who can sue whom. Brave, Mojeek, Exa, Parallel, Perplexity, Linkup, Yep and Staan run their own indexes. Brave says flatly that there is "no fallback to another search engine for any query" ([Brave Search API](https://brave.com/search/api/)). Exa crawls as `ExaSearchBot` and Parallel as `ShapBot` ([Exa FAQ](https://exa.ai/docs/admin/faqs.md); [Parallel crawler](https://docs.parallel.ai/resources/crawler.md)). Staan fills in "certain results" through an unnamed US provider ([Staan privacy policy](https://staan.ai/privacy-policy)). Desearch builds its index with SN22 miners and its own crawler, but names no source for its cheapest endpoint, whose failures surface as "upstream_failed" ([Desearch errors](https://www.desearch.ai/docs/guide/introduction/rate-limit)).

Two providers mix in the big engines. Tavily falls back to "third-party search index providers (e.g., Google)" when its own index misses. You.com's zero-retention page promises that with it on, "your queries do not go to Google or Bing", which implies they can when it is off ([Tavily privacy](https://www.tavily.com/privacy); [You.com zero data retention](https://you.com/docs/administration/zero-data-retention.md)). Three are proxies: Jina's open-source search tries Serper's Google results first, then Google and Bing results pages ([Jina searcher.ts](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/jina-ai/reader/main/src/api/searcher.ts)); Firecrawl's unnamed upstream accepts Google-style `tbs` time filters ([Firecrawl search](https://docs.firecrawl.dev/features/search.md)); and Kagi blends results from "third-party API providers for SERP-style results" into its licensed sources ([Kagi blog](https://blog.kagi.com/waiting-dawn-search)).

The APIs that scrape search engine results pages (SERPs) invert Brave's profile. They are cheap, from **$0.30 per 1,000** at Serper's top volume to $2 for DataForSEO's live mode ([Serper](https://serper.dev/); [DataForSEO pricing](https://dataforseo.com/pricing/serp/google-organic-serp-api)). They also almost never restrict storage: DataForSEO's terms "set no limit on how long a client keeps its own copies" ([DataForSEO legal FAQ](https://dataforseo.com/help-center/quick-answers-to-frequently-asked-legal-questions)). Their obstacle is resale. SerpApi, SearchApi.io, Bright Data and Oxylabs forbid reselling the service, or access to it, without written permission ([SerpApi legal](https://serpapi.com/legal); [SearchApi terms](https://www.searchapi.io/legal/terms); [Bright Data agreement](https://brightdata.com/license); [Oxylabs terms](https://oxylabs.io/legal/self-service-tos)). DataForSEO says "white-label API access, MCP endpoints… and per-query wallet resale models" need a separate agreement. It also bars any use of results that would "compete with or adversely affect" the search engines themselves ([DataForSEO terms](https://dataforseo.com/terms-of-service)). Only Serper's terms say nothing on the subject ([Serper terms](https://serper.dev/terms)).

The legal risk has moved onto buyers. Google's suit against SerpApi pleads only anti-circumvention claims under DMCA §1201, and it has failed twice. On 20 July 2026 the court dismissed for good the claims over results with no copyrighted content. On 9 November it dismissed the amended complaint, giving Google until **10 December 2026** for a last try ([July order](https://chatgptiseatingtheworld.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/Google-v-SerpApi-order-granting-motion-to-dismiss-July-20-2026.pdf); [November order](https://serpapi.com/blog/content/files/2026/11/62.0--2026-11-09--ORDER-GRANTING-MOTION-TO-DISMISS-WITH-LEAVE-TO-AMEND-by.pdf); [scheduling order](https://storage.courtlistener.com/recap/gov.uscourts.cand.461513/gov.uscourts.cand.461513.64.0.pdf)).

Reddit's suit against SerpApi went the other way, and against a customer. On 31 July 2026 the Southern District of New York let §1201 and conspiracy claims proceed against **Perplexity, a paying SerpApi customer**, as a direct circumventor. The court reasoned that Perplexity "set[s] the parameters for circumvention activity" and "conduct[s] the actual queries itself". That makes it "more than a bystander to, or a downstream beneficiary of" the scraping ([Reddit v. SerpApi opinion](https://storage.courtlistener.com/recap/gov.uscourts.nysd.651592/gov.uscourts.nysd.651592.104.0.pdf)). Turning each person's query into a scraper call does exactly what that opinion describes, at agent volume. Google's complaint pleads statutory damages of $200 to $2,500 per violation ([Google complaint](https://storage.courtlistener.com/recap/gov.uscourts.cand.461513/gov.uscourts.cand.461513.1.0.pdf)).

The scrapers' legal shields do not reach this. SerpApi's covers collection "but not for how that data is ultimately used" and treats DMCA violations as illegal use outside its cover. SearchApi.io's excludes "your use, storage, redistribution, or commercialization of data" ([SerpApi legal](https://serpapi.com/legal); [SearchApi terms](https://www.searchapi.io/legal/terms)). Europe adds the Innoweb ruling, which held that a real-time meta-search engine re-utilises the database it queries ([Kluwer Copyright Blog](https://legalblogs.wolterskluwer.com/copyright-blog/eu-does-innoweb-hinder-innovation-on-the-web/)). Both US rulings came at the pleading stage, and secondary liability under §1201 is undecided, but the direction is clear.

Reliability is falling at the same time. Since September 2026, providers have reported what they call "poisoned" Google results, a practice Google has not confirmed. SerpApi's incident for its Google Search API was still open on 23 November ([SerpApi status](https://status.serpapi.com/incidents/bhj19czp9b04)), and DataForSEO now sells a `resolve_poisoned_results` option at twice the base price ([DataForSEO pricing](https://dataforseo.com/pricing/serp/google-organic-serp-api)). For agents, silent corruption of this kind is worse than an outage, because nothing signals the error. Scrapers should be ruled out. Google-proxying APIs (Jina's search, Firecrawl's search and Kagi's blend) should be treated as carrying the same upstream exposure, one step removed.

## Switch off every generator and fetch pages on the device

No provider runs its generative features in an enclave a client can verify, so every endpoint that writes text with a model is out. On Brave that means Answers, the deprecated Summarizer (including the web search `summary` flag) and POI Descriptions, which Brave calls "LLM generated descriptions" ([Brave POI descriptions](https://api-dashboard.search.brave.com/api-reference/web/poi_descriptions)); Brave's own MCP local-search tool includes those descriptions, so it cannot be reused as it stands. Elsewhere it means Exa's answer, deep and summary modes and its highlights, which are "Text snippets the LLM identifies as most relevant" ([Exa search reference](https://exa.ai/docs/reference/search.md)); Tavily's `include_answer`, research and the "NLP summary" of its ultra-fast mode ([Tavily search reference](https://docs.tavily.com/documentation/api-reference/endpoint/search.md)); Parallel's Task, FindAll and Monitor APIs; Perplexity's Sonar and the `criteria` option of its Context API; Linkup's `sourcedAnswer`, `structured` and Fetch `schema`; Firecrawl's `json`, `summary` and `query` formats; and Kagi's FastGPT. Defaults are the trap. Desearch's AI Search "by default… also writes a summary" unless `result_type` is pinned to `ONLY_LINKS` ([Desearch search API](https://www.desearch.ai/docs/guide/apis/search-api)). You.com's Extract sends complex PDF pages "through an advanced model" unless `parse_effort` is `lite` ([You.com Extract](https://you.com/docs/guides/extract.md)). So the device must set these parameters explicitly on every call. Perplexity is the one provider whose contract states that its search "does not generate Output through the use of artificial intelligence, including large language models" ([Perplexity Search Addendum](https://www.perplexity.ai/hub/legal/perplexity-api-terms-of-service-search)). One question stays open for every provider: none says whether its default snippets are verbatim page text. Parallel's advanced mode, for example, runs a "retrieval and compression pipeline" ([Parallel search modes](https://docs.parallel.ai/search/modes.md)), and its output should be confirmed verbatim before it counts as retrieval.

Brave sells no page fetch at all. Its LLM Context endpoint returns chunks of indexed pages that match a query, not the page a person names ([Brave LLM Context](https://api-dashboard.search.brave.com/documentation/services/llm-context)). Nearly every alternative fills that gap for about **$1 per 1,000 URLs**: You.com's Extract ($0.90) returns Markdown or HTML with PDF tables intact, Exa's `/contents` ($1) adds section filters, link styles and publish dates, Parallel's Extract ($1) handles "JavaScript-heavy pages and PDFs", Perplexity's Context API ($1) takes batches of URLs, Linkup's Fetch costs $1 or $5 with JavaScript rendering, and Staan's Fetch and Desearch's Extract cost €0.50 and $0.50 ([You.com APIs](https://you.com/docs/choose-the-right-api.md); [Exa contents](https://exa.ai/docs/reference/get-contents.md); [Parallel Extract](https://docs.parallel.ai/extract/extract-quickstart.md); [Perplexity pricing](https://docs.perplexity.ai/docs/getting-started/pricing.md); [Linkup pricing](https://docs.linkup.so/pages/documentation/platform/pricing.md); [Staan](https://staan.ai/?view=machine); [Desearch pricing](https://www.desearch.ai/pricing)). Exa's contents ($0.001 a page) and Parallel's extract ($0.01 a URL) are also sold keyless. Every "structured" or schema-based extraction on offer runs a model, so that step belongs on URnetwork's TEE inference, applied to the returned Markdown.

The better extractor is the person's own device. It can fetch a URL itself, as Claude Code's WebFetch already does on the client ([Claude Code tools reference](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/tools-reference#webfetch-tool-behavior)). It can then convert the page with an open-source extractor such as Firecrawl's AGPL core or Jina's Apache-licensed Reader ([Firecrawl](https://github.com/firecrawl/firecrawl); [Jina Reader repository](https://api.github.com/repos/jina-ai/reader/git/trees/main?recursive=1)). No provider then learns which pages a person reads, no extraction terms apply, and each page costs nothing. The Ninth Circuit's August 2026 ruling in Amazon v. Perplexity supports treating such fetches as the person's own access. It held that "It is the user who 'accesses' Amazon's computers, with the help of the Assistant", while leaving open cases where the operator controls the agent ([Amazon v. Perplexity](https://cdn.ca9.uscourts.gov/datastore/opinions/2026/08/04/26-1444.pdf)). Paid extraction then becomes a fallback for pages a device cannot render.

## Conclusion

Architecture decides this comparison before price does. Every license studied assumes a key the customer keeps on its own server, and URnetwork's plaintext rule forbids exactly that server, so the architecture chooses the vendors: x402 from the device admits Brave, You.com, Exa, Tavily, Parallel, Keenable and Linkup, while Mojeek, Desearch, Staan and every zero-retention tier are sold only by account key, which no design within the owner directive can use until the provider offers a keyless or per-device path. Parallel is the strongest alternative on the device path, with the best independent score, terms written for end customers and a keyless gateway, though its no-retention EU endpoint is account-only. Mojeek holds the only license that already grants indefinite storage and the cleanest data terms, so it is worth asking for x402 or per-device keys and testing on URnetwork's own queries; a storage flag for each provider in the result format would then keep only storable results in the record. That leaves three letters to get before launch: from Parallel, on per-search charging, on the record copy under §2(c)(xi) and the "AI and Data Selling Services" clause, and on zero retention; from Mojeek, on its search-index clause, an Enterprise variation and a keyless or per-device path; and, if Brave stays, a Brave Order Form, which remains Brave's real offer.

Two implications reach beyond search. x402 opens a privacy leak the end-to-end encryption story has not yet had to name: query text never reaches the chain, but timing, volume and payer do, so unbatched per-request payment leaves a public trail of URnetwork's search volume and timing, and batching (offered today only by Exa, through Circle) belongs among the selection criteria. And search is the first purchase the compute balance cannot route to gamma; asking Desearch to enable gamma on SN22 costs little, but it buys a payment rail, not privacy, because Desearch still sees and keeps every query.
